PPO's Fine Print

Between 2019 and 2022 the field went looking for where PPO's advantage actually lives, and found a good deal of it in code nobody had written down. This is what the three studies that went looking actually measured — and, just as usefully, what they never tested at all.

3 works · 4 PDFs · ~250,000 trained agents Reviewed 2026-09-04

Proximal Policy Optimization is one of the most used algorithms in reinforcement learning, and its 2017 paper explains its improvement in terms of a single idea: a clipped objective that stops a policy update from moving too far in one step. That is the contribution the paper claims, and it is the one everybody cites.

The trouble is that every working implementation of PPO also does perhaps thirty other things the paper never mentions — rescaling rewards, rescaling observations, initialising one particular layer far smaller than the rest, decaying the learning rate, clipping the value loss. None of these are in the equations. All of them are in the code. The three works below are the field's attempt to find out how much of PPO's reputation belongs to the equation and how much to the fine print.

They reached an uncomfortable answer, and then a more careful one.

How the question developed

2017the claim

The clipped objective is the contribution

Schulman et al., Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms

PPO is introduced as a simpler, better-behaved alternative to trust-region methods. The mechanism offered is the clipped surrogate objective. The surrounding engineering is not presented as part of the method.

2020the doubt

Take the fine print away and much of the advantage goes with it

Engstrom, Ilyas, Santurkar, Tsipras, Janoos, Rudolph & Madry — Implementation Matters in Deep Policy Gradients: A Case Study on PPO and TRPO, ICLR 2020

The authors name nine code-level optimizations present in PPO's reference implementation but absent from its paper, then run a full factorial over four of them — value clipping, reward scaling, orthogonal initialisation, learning-rate annealing — with the learning rate re-tuned separately inside every one of the sixteen cells, so that no setting is handicapped by hyperparameters chosen for a different one. Eighty agents per environment.

Their conclusion is that reward normalisation, learning-rate annealing and initialisation each move the results materially, and were necessary to reach PPO's best scores in the grid they searched.

Worth knowing before citing it

The headline figure is a distribution plot, not a bar chart: no effect size is reported anywhere for the individual optimizations. The paper shows that the landscape changes shape, not by how much any one switch is worth.

2021the survey

Fifty choices, a quarter of a million agents, one at a time

Andrychowicz, Raichuk, Stańczyk, Orsini, Girgin, Marinier, Hussenot, Geist, Pietquin, Michalski, Gelly & Bachem — What Matters for On-Policy Deep Actor-Critic Methods? A Large-Scale Study, ICLR 2021

Rather than argue about one switch, this study exposes roughly fifty design choices in a single unified codebase and samples them in large random searches across five continuous-control tasks — varying one group at a time while everything else is drawn at random, so each result is averaged over the settings of the rest instead of measured against one hand-tuned baseline.

The output is an importance ranking, and it does not match what the literature emphasises. Some of the largest effects come from choices that are barely discussed.

A naming trap

This paper was retitled between preprint and publication. The arXiv copy is "What Matters In On-Policy Reinforcement Learning? A Large-Scale Empirical Study"; the ICLR version of record is the title above. Searching the old one finds only the preprint — which is worth having anyway, because the camera-ready is ten pages of main text and all thirty-eight pages of supporting experiments live only in the preprint.

2022the consolidation

The folklore, finally written down

Huang, Dossa, Raffin, Kanervisto & Wang — The 37 Implementation Details of Proximal Policy Optimization, ICLR Blog Track 2022

Not a study but a catalogue: every undocumented detail in the reference implementation, named, explained, and reproduced to matching performance. It is the document that turns "everyone knows you have to do this" into something a newcomer can look up — and it is careful to say which details it has tested and which it is merely recording.

Three different things called "normalization"

This is the single most useful thing to take from reading all three together. The word normalization is used across this literature for at least three operations that touch different quantities, use different statistics, and do not imply one another. One of these papers even describes another's operation using the wrong one of the three names.

A claim that says "normalization" without saying which one is not a citable claim.

The disagreement this dissolves

On the Walker2d task, Engstrom's figure shows reward scaling helping while Andrychowicz's shows value-target normalization hurting by 42%. These look like a contradiction and are not: they are different interventions. Anyone writing "the literature says normalizing helps on Walker" is choosing which paper to quote without saying which operation they mean.

What is actually established

Each claim below carries its evidence status. measured means an experiment exists and reports a number. weak means an experiment exists but its design limits what it shows. silent means the experiment does not exist in these papers — which is neither evidence for nor against, and is the status most often misread as a finding.

Shrinking the policy's last layer at initialization

measured

Scaling the policy output layer's initial weights down by 100× — so the first action distribution is near-zero-mean, low-variance and barely depends on the observation — is worth about +66% on the hardest task, by the paper's own reckoning.

But read the shape: it is a cliff, not a slope. Scales of 0.001, 0.01 and 0.1 sit inside each other's error bars everywhere; only leaving the layer at its default 1.0 is clearly bad. What makes the result credible is the control — the same rescaling applied to the value head does almost nothing, which is exactly what the explanation predicts.

Andrychowicz §3.2, Fig. 24 · control: C58, Fig. 19

Value-target normalization is powerful and unpredictable

measured

Rescaling the critic's target with a running statistic is +78% on one task and −42% on another. On the task where it hurts, 99% of the best configurations had it switched off. The authors write, in the paper, "We are not sure why the value function scale matters that much."

Their recommendation is accordingly conditional — check whether it helps — not use it. This is the closest thing in the literature to a warning that scale choices are environment-specific rather than universally good.

Andrychowicz C66, §3.3, Fig. 37

Advantage normalization barely registers

weak

The near-universal practice of standardizing advantages per minibatch was measured, and the verdict is a shrug: "seems not to affect the performance too much." It is absent from the paper's recommendations, and the authors' own default leaves it off.

Two cautions in the other direction. It is ahead on three tasks and behind on two, all confidence intervals overlapping — but pooled across tasks it accounts for 53.5% of the best configurations against 47%, a mild tilt in its favour that a flat "no effect" hides. And no significance test was run on it at all.

Andrychowicz C67, §3.3, Fig. 35 · Engstrom: silent · Huang: documents, does not test

Separate policy and value networks beat a shared trunk

weak

Separate networks won on four of five continuous-control tasks and took 61% of the best configurations; a smaller discrete-action comparison agrees on two of three. As an outcome this is real.

As evidence it is softer than it sounds. The shared arm was additionally forced to draw its value-loss weight from a set spanning five orders of magnitude, so much of that arm was handicapped by a weight no practitioner would choose — and the marginal for that weight was never reported, because the experiment was re-run with the shared arm removed. The effect also reverses on one task.

Andrychowicz C47, §3.1 · Huang §"shared vs separate"

Why a shared trunk would be worse

silent

The intuitive story — in a shared trunk the value loss dominates the combined gradient, and the actor is left reading out features shaped only by value regression — is not measured anywhere in this literature. One author states it in a single sentence as a conjecture and points at a different paper for the answer. Nobody reports gradient magnitudes, loss ratios or feature usage.

And the same literature supplies a direct counterweight: for Atari, the reference implementation shares its convolutional trunk between policy and value, and the only recurrent PPO in this corpus is built on that shared trunk. The real convention is architecture-conditional — separate when the trunk is a cheap two-layer network, shared when it is an expensive feature extractor.

Conjectured in Huang; not measured by Engstrom (whose networks never share a parameter) or Andrychowicz

Normalizing the critic's target per batch

silent

Nobody does it. The nearest relative — value-function normalization — differs on both counts that matter: its statistic is running rather than per-batch, and it is undone at read-out, so the quantity the advantage is built from never leaves raw units. Recomputing a target's scale from scratch every batch appears nowhere in these three works, and normalization of the value target appears nowhere among the 37 catalogued details.

Checked across all three works

The limit on all of it

Every ablation in this literature was run on fully-observed continuous-control physics tasks, with feed-forward networks and Gaussian policies. Not one experiment in any of these three works varies the action space, adds recurrence, or introduces partial observability while measuring the effect of a normalization or architecture choice.

The one paper that covers discrete and recurrent settings runs no ablations in them, and says so itself, noting that prior conclusions drawn in physics tasks "do not necessarily transfer to other games such as Atari".

So the honest reading is narrow. These papers are strong evidence about a well-studied family of settings, and they are silent — not reassuring, not discouraging — about partially-observed, recurrent, discrete-action agents, and about any reward built from an internal state rather than a task score. Silence licenses running the experiment yourself. It does not license borrowing the conclusion.

Two things I had wrong

"No significant effect"

corrected

I described the advantage-normalization result that way. It is wrong twice over: the authors ran no significance test, so the phrase attributes a statistical claim nobody made — and "no effect" overstates their data, which tilts mildly toward normalizing when pooled across tasks.

The defensible wording is longer and duller: a small, environment-dependent, sign-inconsistent effect that the confidence intervals cannot separate from zero, neither recommended nor warned against.

The shared-trunk mechanism

corrected

I leaned on it repeatedly as an explanation. It is a conjecture stated once in passing by one practitioner, measured by nobody, and the same literature contains a counterexample in which the shared trunk is standard practice. It may still be true — but it cannot be cited to these papers, and in our own setting it would have to be measured directly.
