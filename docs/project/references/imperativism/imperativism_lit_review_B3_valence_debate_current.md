## What this batch is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question.** Why do some experiences feel good and others bad? Philosophers call this quality **valence**. What began around 2010 as a debate about pain alone (see Batch B1) had by the 2020s become a debate about *all* feelings — pains, tastes, fears, moods.

**The two sides, as they stand.**
- **Imperativism**: a feeling is a command. Unpleasant pain says, roughly, "less of this!"; a pleasant taste says "more of this!".
- **Evaluativism**: a feeling is a perception of value. Pain presents the damage (or the sensation) as bad for you. Peter Carruthers (2018, 2023; reviewed in Part A) is its main current defender.

**What the three pieces here add.**
- **Barlassina (essay, c. 2020)** gives a short plain-language case for the *reflexive* form of imperativism: a feeling commands more or less of *itself*. Among his examples, a rat with a certain brain lesion still shows "liking" reactions to salt but no longer seeks it. He admits he cannot yet say why evolution gave us feelings at all.
- **Kauppinen (2021)** defends a rival imperativism that points at the world. On his view fear says, in effect, "make sure this danger doesn't happen again", backed by a standing concern (not being hurt). He offers answers to the standard objections: how commands can be reasons, how they vary in intensity, and why people take painkillers.
- **Martínez & Barlassina (2026)** reply to Carruthers (2023). Their test: a brain state that predicts what you will *do* better than it reports what the *world* is like, and that sits nearer action than sensing, is a command. They argue that pleasure and pain signals, as psychology and neuroscience describe them, pass this test.

**Where it stands.** Unresolved. Imperativists disagree among themselves about whether the command concerns the world or the experience. No reply by Carruthers to the 2026 paper is held in the corpus. This review records the positions without taking a side. For this project's artificial agents, the internal damage signal is called **nociception**, not pain.

- **Vocabulary.**
  - *First-order / world-directed* imperativism: the command concerns the body or world ("less of this damage!").
  - *Reflexive* imperativism: the command concerns the experience itself ("less of me!").
  - *Higher-order* imperativism (Klein 2015): the command concerns a separate sensation ("don't have that sensation!").
  - *Metasemantics*: a theory of what makes a state have the content it has.
  - *Indicative* content describes; *imperative* content commands; *pushmi-pullyu* states do both (Millikan).
  - *Pain asymbolia*: a condition in which patients feel pain without being bothered by it.

## 1. Kauppinen (2021) — Relational imperativism about affective valence [tier: full]

**Citation:** Antti Kauppinen, "Relational Imperativism about Affective Valence," in *Oxford Studies in Philosophy of Mind*, Vol. 1 (Oxford University Press, 2021), ch. 12. doi:10.1093/oso/9780198845850.003.0012.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Kauppinen 2021 - Relational imperativism about affective valence.pdf` — **accepted manuscript** ("Forthcoming in Oxford Studies in the Philosophy of Mind Vol. 1 / Final Draft, March 31, 2020", 36 pp.). Page citations are manuscript pages ("ms. p. N", matching the page numbers printed on the draft); they do not match the published chapter's pagination. Some references in the draft are still cited as "ms"/"forthcoming" (e.g., Barlassina's "Beyond Good and Bad", published 2020).

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

Kauppinen widens the question from pain to **all feelings that are good or bad** — pains, disgust at a rotten apple, being annoyed that your team lost, fear, joy, moods such as anxiety and calm. What gives them their good-or-bad feel (**valence**)? The popular answer by 2020 was *evaluativism*: fear of a charging lion feels bad because it presents the lion as bad for you. Kauppinen defends a version of the rival answer, *imperativism*: the antelope's fear feels bad because it is, in effect, a command — "Run!". His version, **relational imperativism**, pairs two contents in one experience: a picture of how things are that matters to one of your standing concerns (a bear is near; I want not to be hurt), and an authoritative command to make sure that sort of thing does not (or, for positive feelings, does) happen again.

#### Key Findings

1. **What valence must explain.** Valence *inherently motivates* (a feeling of gratitude can move you to return a favour without a prior desire) and *rationalises* (a toothache makes calling the dentist make sense). He explicitly does **not** accept that valence is a "common currency" for comparing options in deliberation (against Levy & Glimcher 2012; Carruthers 2018).
2. **Doubts about evaluativism.** No one has explained what a *non-conceptual* representation of goodness is; merely tracking what is good for you (Cutter & Tye 2011) is not the same as things *appearing* good. And there is the Humean worry: representations do not by themselves motivate.
3. **Map of imperativist views.** First-order (Martínez 2011: command about the body/world), higher-order (Klein 2015: command about the sensation, "Don't have that sensation!"), and reflexive (Barlassina & Hayward 2019: the experience commands "Less of me!"). Kauppinen stays closest to Martínez.
4. **The relational view.** Negative valence = indicative content that bears on a background concern + subjectively authoritative command to see to it that states of affairs like that won't obtain, by behaviour typical of that affect. Pain: "See to it that your ankle isn't damaged by protecting it!". Positive affects command exploring or relishing more of the same.
5. **Pain asymbolia.** The command's authority comes from a background concern. Patients who lack concern for their bodies therefore feel pain that is not unpleasant — so imperativism explains asymbolia at least as well as evaluativism (answering Bain 2014).
6. **Three objections answered.** (a) *Rationalising*: commands we accept get projected onto the world, so the bear *looks* to-be-fled-from; this may even be what "non-conceptually representing badness" amounts to. (b) *Intensity*: a more intense affect is a command that demands a larger **opportunity cost** — simpler than Klein & Martínez's priority-ranking model. (c) *"Shooting the Officer"* (why take painkillers if pain is just a command about the ankle?): it begs the question just as the messenger objection does against evaluativism; and the motive to take painkillers comes from a *separate* displeasure at being in an unpleasant state, not from the pain itself.

#### Initial Takeaway

This chapter is a representative 2020s position: imperativism generalised from pain to all affect, with explicit replies to each standard objection accumulated since 2011 (reasons, intensity, painkillers, asymbolia). It also shows that the imperativist camp is itself divided — first-order vs. higher-order vs. reflexive — and that Kauppinen suggests a partial reconciliation: evaluativists' "seeming bad" may be what an accepted command looks like from the inside.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Technical Analysis

**Explanandum (§1.1, ms. pp. 3–5).** Valence ≠ pleasantness (anger would then motivate through unpleasantness, which he denies). The primary target is *inherently motivating phenomenal character* ("inherent" rather than "intrinsic", since motivation is not necessary). Secondary target: subjective rationalisation. Excluded: valence as deliberative common currency; "If there's a common currency to deliberation, it's utility" (ms. p. 5).

**Framework (§1.2).** Sets aside no-explanation views (Smuts 2011) and externalist attitude views (Heathwood 2007; Jacobson 2019). Assumes *Phenomenal–Intentional Parallelism*: same intentional content ⇔ same phenomenal character, neutral between reducing either to the other (ms. p. 6).

**Reconstructed argument for evaluativism (ms. p. 7).** (1) parallelism; (2) valenced affects' content rationalises world-directed action and value judgement; (3) that requires world-directed content represented as good/bad; (4) so affects represent something as good/bad. Kauppinen doubts (3) via the non-conceptual-value and Humean worries (ms. pp. 8–9).

**Indicative vs. imperative content** is taken from Barlassina & Hayward (2019): function of carrying information / truth conditions / uptake by belief vs. function of directing / satisfaction conditions / uptake by motivation (ms. p. 10). Imperative content (not just force) is assumed, citing Millikan 1984, Castañeda 1975, Hanks 2007.

**Reconstructed argument for imperativism (ms. pp. 11–12).** (1) parallelism; (2) valenced character inherently motivates when taken at face value; (3) that requires imperative, not indicative, content (Humean); (4) valence consists at least partly in imperative content.

**Relational Imperativism (ms. p. 14).** "An experience has positive (negative) valence for S when it has a) indicative content p that represents an actual or possible state of affairs that bears on a (possibly novel) background concern of S and b) subjectively authoritative imperatival content telling S to see to it that states of affairs relevantly like p will (won't) obtain in the future by engaging in some behaviour characteristic of the affect type." Worked examples (ms. pp. 15–20):

| Affect | Indicative (a) | Background concern (bc) | Imperative (i) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pain | My ankle is damaged | May there be no bodily damage to me! | See to it that your ankle isn't damaged by protecting it! |
| Sensory displeasure | There's a rotten taste in my mouth | May I not have the taste of spoiled food in my mouth! | See to it that you will not have this sort of sensation in the future by avoiding what caused it! |
| Attitudinal displeasure | Sweden beat Finland | May the Finnish team do well! | See to it that Sweden won't beat Finland in the future! |
| Fear | There's a bear poised to do something I really wouldn't like to me | May I not get hurt! | See to it that there won't be [such] a bear … by seeking safety! |

Key clarifications: contents are non-conceptual (no concept of the future is needed); imperatives are *not* urges, since conative uptake can be blocked by beliefs and plans (hence idle commands in attitudinal displeasure, grief); concerns can be generated by the experience itself (a first taste of spicy food, ms. pp. 18–19); non-conscious appraisal is neither sufficient nor necessary for valence (ms. pp. 16–17). Positive affect: "Explore further possibilities for realizing or relishing this sort of thing in the future!" (ms. p. 21), linked to Fredrickson's (2001) broaden-and-build account. Moods: anxiety commands removal of unspecific threats (so only diffuse fight-or-flight readiness); some depression commands ceasing pointless activity; tranquillity commands keeping things as they are (ms. pp. 22–23).

**Asymbolia (ms. p. 19).** Bain (2014) and Klein (2015) explain asymbolia by absent concern for one's body. Bain claimed imperativism cannot explain "why not caring about one's bodily integrity should prevent pain's imperative content". Kauppinen: authority derives from concern, so lack of concern removes the authority and hence the unpleasantness; and evaluativism has the harder task of saying why not caring stops me from representing damage as bad ("That's bad for me, but I don't care about it", ms. p. 19).

**Intensity (ms. pp. 27–30).** Objection (Cutter & Tye 2011): commands cannot differ in intensity. Klein & Martínez's model adds a ranking of worlds to each imperative; i is more forceful than j when both rankings prefer i's satisfaction (the Autovon priority-call analogy). Kauppinen's worry: this needs imperatives that refer to each other, implausible for simple creatures. His alternative, with worlds valued in "utils":

- w1: line held, some losses (up to −5); w2: line held, all soldiers die (−10); w3: line not held, no losses (0).
- "Hold the line at any cost!" ranks w1 > w2 > w3; "Hold the line at the cost of some but not nearly all …" ranks w1 > w3 > w2.

**Forcefulness of Imperatives** (ms. p. 29), stated in the paper in words, rendered here symbolically. Write `Sat_i` for worlds where imperative i is satisfied and `L(w)` for the addressee's loss in world w:

$$
i \succ_{\text{force}} j \iff \exists\, u > v:\;\; i \text{ ranks } \{w \in \mathrm{Sat}_i : L(w) \le u\} \text{ above } \{w \notin \mathrm{Sat}_i : L(w) \le u\},\;\; j \text{ does so only up to } v
$$

This is the reviewer's compressed rendering of the paper's verbal definition, not a formula from the paper. Intensity of affect = forcefulness of its imperative; a more intense joy commands sacrificing more to explore more of the same.

**Shooting the Officer (ms. pp. 30–33).**
1. Negative valence = a command to see to it that the indicated content won't obtain.
2. Being so commanded is not non-instrumentally bad and gives no reason to leave the state.
3. So relational imperativism cannot explain reasons to take painkillers.

Reply 1: premise 2 begs the question — if imperativism is true, unpleasantness *is* the command state, so if unpleasantness is intrinsically bad, so is the command (Bain's 2019 "Leibniz's Law Argument", borrowed). Reply 2: deny *reflexive motivation* (Barlassina & Hayward 2019). Grief motivates wishing the world different, not grief-killers; fear says flee, not "take an anti-fear pill"; strawberry pleasure motivates more strawberries, not more pleasure. Motivation to take painkillers comes from a contingent *attitudinal displeasure* at undergoing unpleasantness, backed by a background concern to avoid unpleasant experiences (citing Cutter & Tye 2014 for an evolutionary explanation). Prediction: we lack inherent motivation to get rid of experiences as such, and are sometimes not displeased at unpleasant states (functional fear, grief, athletes' pain).

#### Argument Reconstruction (summary of the overall case)

- A1. Valence inherently motivates and subjectively rationalises (explananda).
- A2. Evaluativism explains rationalisation but struggles with motivation and with what non-conceptual value representation is.
- A3. First-order imperativism explains motivation; its known problems are rationalisation, intensity, positive/negative polarity, reflexive motivation (painkillers) and asymbolia.
- A4. Relational imperativism answers: polarity via "won't/will obtain" relative to indicated content; rationalisation via projection into affective practical seemings; intensity via opportunity cost; painkillers via separate attitudinal displeasure; asymbolia via concern-grounded authority.
- C. Relational imperativism is a better account of valence than evaluativism and than higher-order or reflexive imperativism.

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- The account needs *three* ingredients (indicative content, background concern, command), so the explanatory load is spread; whether the valence is in the command or in its relation to the concern is not fully separated.
- The rationalisation reply rests on a projection mechanism cited from Hume, Mackie and Blackburn, not on evidence about affect specifically.
- The "no reflexive motivation" claim directly contradicts a premise of Barlassina & Hayward (2019); it is a live disagreement inside imperativism, not settled here.
- The draft is an accepted manuscript; quoted wording could differ slightly from the printed chapter.
- No empirical data; psychological sources (Frijda 1986; Fredrickson 2001; Levy & Glimcher 2012) are used illustratively.

## 2. Martínez & Barlassina (2026) — The informational profile of valence: The metasemantic argument for imperativism [tier: full]

**Citation:** Manolo Martínez and Luca Barlassina, "The Informational Profile of Valence: The Metasemantic Argument for Imperativism," *The British Journal for the Philosophy of Science* 77(3): 847–865, September 2026 (accepted 30 June 2023; electronically published 17 June 2026). doi:10.1086/726998.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Martinez and Barlassina 2026 - The informational profile of valence - The metasemantic argument for imperativism.pdf` — published version (typeset, 19 pp.). Page citations are journal pages (journal page = PDF page + 846).

> **Extraction note.** In the text layer, plus signs are rendered as "1" (e.g., "110 pleasantness" for "+10", "bodily 1 external temperature" for "bodily + external"). Numbers below are given in the corrected form, inferred from the surrounding text.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

By the 2020s the debate about valence — why some experiences are pleasant and others unpleasant — had narrowed to two theories. **Imperativism** says a pleasant or unpleasant experience carries a command ("more of this!", "less of this!"). **Evaluativism**, in Peter Carruthers's version, says it represents something as good or bad — specifically, as increasing or decreasing biological fitness. Carruthers (2023) had argued that because pleasure and pain signals reliably carry information about value, standard scientific theories of mental content make them representations of value, and that neuroscience describes them that way. Martínez and Barlassina reply by proposing a neutral test for whether a brain state *describes* or *commands*: look at what it carries information about and where it sits between sensing and acting. Applied to what psychology and neuroscience say about valence, the test comes out on the side of commands.

#### Key Findings

1. **Two tests for describe vs. command.** A state that tells you more about the *world* than about what you will *do* is descriptive (indicative); one that tells you more about what you will do is a command (imperative). And a state close to sensory intake is descriptive; one close to behaviour production is a command. Evaluative content is a kind of descriptive content, so a state that fails the descriptive test cannot be evaluative.
2. **Valence tells little about the world and a lot about behaviour.** In a classic thermal study (Mower 1976), how pleasant a temperature feels depends on whether the body is too hot or too cold, so pleasantness carries almost no information about the temperature itself — but thermal pleasantness strongly predicts what people do to warm up or cool down.
3. **Valence sits late in processing.** It is computed only after combining external input with bodily state, hunger, learning and more — information about the input is lost "for the benefit of behaviour control" (p. 856).
4. **Against the evaluativist rescue.** Saying pleasantness represents the fitness value of "body temperature + external temperature" makes the represented property an unnatural disjunction ("low body and high outside, or high body and low outside"), whereas "more/less of this!" is a unified content.
5. **Against Carruthers's neuroscience objections.** Imperativism does not predict valence in motor cortex (the command says *what*, not *how*) or prefrontal goal areas (a command is not an intention: a marathon runner feels "less of this!" but keeps running). Neuroscientists' "value" talk is about guiding choice, so it already fits commands; in decision models "value" is a technical measure of preference, and in a leading learning model (Juechems & Summerfield 2019) valence is distance from one's goals.
6. **Conclusion.** "Pace Carruthers, the cognitive neuroscience of valence has always been about imperatives" (p. 862).

#### Initial Takeaway

This is the most recent round of the imperativism–evaluativism exchange in the corpus, and it moves the debate onto shared scientific ground: both sides now argue about how to interpret reward, decision and learning research. The authors deliberately stay neutral between first-order imperativism (Martínez) and reflexive imperativism (Barlassina & Hayward), claiming only that *any* imperativism beats evaluativism. The paper's criterion is information-theoretic, but it computes no information quantities; the verdicts are qualitative readings of published studies and models.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Technical Analysis

**The divide (§2).** Imperativism: (un)pleasant states have imperative content that grounds valence. First-order (Martínez 2011, 2015b, 2022): "Get more of this apple!", "Get less of this bodily damage in the back!" (p. 849). Reflexive (Barlassina & Hayward 2019b): P has "get more of P!", U "get less of U!". The paper uses "imperativism" as an umbrella and is "intentionally ambiguous" about the referent of "this" (p. 849). Evaluativism (Carruthers 2018, 2023): indicative *evaluative* content — pleasant states evaluate objects as good (fitness-enhancing), unpleasant as bad (fitness-reducing) (pp. 849–850). Fn. 7: evaluativists must hold evaluative content is indicative, or the view collapses into imperativism. Fn. 1: theories of all affect (Barlassina & Hayward, Carruthers, Martínez) are distinguished from pain-only theories (Bain 2013; Klein 2015). Fn. 8: historical higher-order/same-order evaluativism in Lotze (1852) and Brentano.

**Metasemantic framework (§3).** Sender–receiver motif (Millikan 1984; Skyrms 2010; Godfrey-Smith 2014): source → sender → representation → receiver → destination (fig. 1). Two principles, offered as defeasible heuristics, not definitions (fn. 9):

- **Informational Asymmetry Principle** (after Zollman 2011; Martínez & Klein 2016): with `S` the source variable (world), `D` the destination variable (behaviour) and `M` the state, uncertainty reduction measured e.g. by mutual information:

$$
I(M;S) > I(M;D) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{indicative, not imperative}; \qquad I(M;D) > I(M;S) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{imperative, not indicative}
$$

with approximate equality giving a pushmi-pullyu state (Millikan 1995) (p. 851). The inequality form is the reviewer's transcription of the paper's verbal principle and its stated mutual-information reading. Uncertainty is relative to whatever information is available to the state's consumer (fn. 11).
- **Processing Stage Principle**: closer to sensory intake than to behaviour production → indicative; closer to behaviour → imperative; intermediate → pushmi-pullyu (p. 852). Not merely "far from the periphery" (abstract beliefs are far from it too; fn. 12).

Fn. 10: teleosemantic accounts (Shea 2018) tie the distinction to explanatory asymmetries; the authors gloss over this because, in the cases at hand, explanatory asymmetry depends on informational asymmetry.

**Bridging to evaluative content (§3.2).** Since evaluative content is a species of indicative content, "if a certain state is not indicative, then a fortiori it is not evaluative" (p. 853). An evaluativist would need to give up indicativeness or propose a metasemantics with a sui generis informational profile for evaluative states; "no explicit metasemantics for evaluative states has ever been proposed" (p. 853).

**Behaviour production (§4.1).**
- Valence as "behaviour controller" (Schlader et al. 2011): marmite taste → another bite; rotten-fish smell → move away. Reliable correlation ⇒ high information about behaviour, and the behaviours are more-of/less-of actions as imperativism predicts.
- Low information about the world: Mower (1976), redrawn as fig. 2 — pleasantness (1–9 scale) against stimulus temperature (20–45 °C) for hypothermic, neutral and hyperthermic body states: rising, inverted-U and falling curves respectively. So "(un)pleasantness carries almost no information about external temperature, or about bodily temperature" (p. 854). (The text describes two body states and three stimulus levels, while the figure shows three body-state curves; the argument does not depend on the difference.)
- Evaluativist rescue — pleasantness represents the fitness value of the body+external combination — rejected: the property is disjunctive, and cognition extracts unified objects (Fodor 1974; Putnam 1980; Kim 1992; Spelke 1990; Feldman 2003; DiCarlo & Cox 2007).
- Behavioural thermoregulation (Weiss & Laties 1961; Flouris & Schlader 2015), food (Kringelbach 2004) and sex (Georgiadis & Kringelbach 2012) show the same pattern.
- Processing stage: valence encodes an interaction of external and bodily temperature, so it is later than temperature sensing; citations to Berridge et al. (2009) and Schultz et al. (1997) that pleasantness depends on internal state and experience.

**Rejoinder inspired by Carruthers 2023 (§4.2).** "If valence had imperative content, it should be realized in motor cortex … or in prefrontal cortex … each prediction is disconfirmed" (paraphrased, p. 856). Replies: "get more/less of this!" is not a motor instruction or action plan — it tells what, not how; it is not an intention either (marathon runner hitting the wall). Evaluativism, by contrast, makes valence's motivational role indirect, requiring further pro-attitudes (quoting Carruthers 2023, p. 538). "Indicative states do the thinking; imperatival states do the moving" (p. 857).

**Values without evaluations (§5).** Carruthers: valence is realised in orbitofrontal cortex, thought to code outcome value, so imperativism would need a "wholesale reworking" of neuroscience (quoted p. 857–858). Reply: neuroscientific value talk picks out imperatival states — Glimcher (2014) "hidden internal representations which guide choice"; Grabenhorst & Rolls (2011) linking rewards, actions and costs to select actions; Juechems & Summerfield (2019) value as "a summary of whether the agent is approaching or retreating from its goals"; Farnsworth & Elwood (2023) negative value as the gap between current state and a goal state (p. 858). Since "the models … do the walking", two models are examined:

- *Valence-based decision-making (§5.1)* (Rangel et al. 2008; Glimcher 2014): represent the decision problem; compute outcome values expressed as degrees of valence; select action. Table 1 (apple-or-water):

| | Hungry, not thirsty | Thirsty, not hungry |
|---|---|---|
| Pick apple | +5 pleasantness | +1 pleasantness |
| Pick water | +1 pleasantness | +10 pleasantness |

Valence then carries (idealised) perfect information about the choice but little about the source, because it combines availability, physiological state, risk aversion (Glimcher & Rustichini 2004) and temporal discounting (McClure et al. 2004); fn. 15 explains why more internal variables mean less information about input. "Value" here is a decision-theoretic measure of preference, and whether preferences are indicative-evaluative (Broome 1991) or not (Lewis 1988) is disputed — so value talk alone cannot settle content.
- *Valence-based learning (§5.2)*: prediction-error updating (the carambola example). Evaluativist reading: a stored evaluation is updated; imperativist reading: a command's strength is scaled. In Juechems & Summerfield's (2019) model (fig. 3): agents have goal states; actions aim to reduce distance from them; the valence of an action corresponds to the resulting change in distance (increase → unpleasant, decrease → pleasant); stored valence is updated accordingly. So valence is uninformative about the world (it mixes world and internal variables) and highly informative about behaviour (agents tend to take distance-reducing actions).

#### How the paper replies to Carruthers (2023)

Carruthers's 2023 case (reviewed in [Part A, entry 5](imperativism_lit_review_A_imperative_theories.md)) had three prongs: an informational/teleosemantic argument that value-tracking signals represent value; a claim that learning and decision science need only value representations; and brain-location evidence (valence in value areas, not motor/goal areas). The paper's responses, as the text states them:

| Carruthers 2023 claim (as this paper represents it or as Part A records it) | Martínez & Barlassina's response |
|---|---|
| Valence carries information about adaptive value, so informational metasemantics assigns it value content | Accepts an informational metasemantics but adds two comparative principles; valence carries *less* information about the source than about behaviour, and is late in processing, so it is imperatival. Any value it could be said to track is a disjunctive combination of world and body variables (thermal case). The paper does not discuss Carruthers's specific appeal to Shea (2018) or Rupert (2018) (Rupert is not cited). |
| Valence should be in motor or prefrontal cortex if imperatival; it is not | Imperativism predicts neither: the command is neither a motor instruction nor an intention (§4.2) |
| Orbitofrontal "value" coding supports evaluativism; imperativism would require reworking neuroscience | Neuroscientific "value" is choice-guiding and goal-distance based, i.e., already imperatival (§5) |
| Decision-making and learning are explained by value representations | In those models valence is highly informative about behaviour, uninformative about the world, and late — so the models' own structure favours imperative content (§5.1–5.2) |

Not addressed in this paper (checked against the text): Carruthers's attention argument (looking at gruesome scenes), his motivational-hedonism charge, and his multiple-appraisal replies to counterexamples.

#### Argument Reconstruction

- P1. (Metasemantics) A state that is more informative about behaviour than about the world, or closer to behaviour production than to sensory intake, has imperative rather than indicative content (defeasible heuristics).
- P2. Evaluative content is a kind of indicative content.
- P3. In behaviour production, valence is highly informative about more-of/less-of actions and weakly informative about world variables, and is computed after integrating sensory with internal information (thermal, food, sex evidence).
- P4. The same holds for valence in valence-based decision-making models and in valence-based learning models.
- P5. The evaluativist moves available (disjunctive fitness contents; brain-location predictions; neuroscientific value talk) fail.
- C. Valence has imperative, not evaluative, content; imperativism (in any version) is superior to evaluativism.

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- The two principles are applied qualitatively: no mutual-information estimate is computed for any dataset, and the informational claims about decision and learning models are about idealised models, which the authors acknowledge (p. 860).
- The Mower (1976) data concern *judged thermal pleasantness*, not pain. The generalisation to all valence relies on citations to reviews of food and sex pleasure.
- "Being informative about behaviour" could hold for a value representation that is reliably consumed by action selection. Whether the comparative principles discriminate commands from value signals that are simply close to action is exactly what an evaluativist would dispute; the paper places the burden on evaluativists to supply a rival metasemantics.
- The paper is neutral between first-order and reflexive imperativism, so it does not adjudicate the intra-imperativist disputes recorded in Kauppinen (2021, entry 1) and Part A.
- No response from Carruthers to this paper is held in the corpus.

## 3. Barlassina (c. 2020) — Valence: A reflection [tier: full]

**Citation:** Luca Barlassina, "Valence: A reflection," short essay, forthcoming (at the time of the PDF) in *Emotion Researcher, ISRE's Sourcebook for Research on Emotion and Affect*. No DOI.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Barlassina - Valence - A reflection.pdf` — **author copy** of a pre-publication essay (9 pp., page-numbered 1–9, header "Forthcoming in: Emotion Researcher"). Page citations are the manuscript's own page numbers ("ms. p. N").

> **Dating note.** The PDF carries no date. The year is inferred from the text: the author says "One year after, I am still puzzled" about a question posed in Barlassina & Hayward (2019), and cites "Beyond good and bad" (published 2020) as *under review* and "Loopy regulations" as *forthcoming*. So the essay was written c. 2020, i.e., **before** Kauppinen (2021) and well before Martínez & Barlassina (2026), despite its place in this batch. ### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

A short, informal essay for an emotion-research newsletter audience. It asks what makes very different experiences — a toothache, fear of flying, an orgasm, the smell of flowers — all count as pleasant or unpleasant. It accepts Carruthers's (2018) case that valence is probably one natural kind (one brain network, the same drugs affecting physical and social pain, a common currency for choice), and asks what that kind *is* at the level of information processing. It then argues that the two best-known answers fail because both point valence at the world, and presents **reflexive imperativism**: an unpleasant experience says "Less of me!" and a pleasant one "More of me!".

#### Key Findings

1. **Against world-directed evaluativism** (Bain 2013; Carruthers 2018; Cutter & Tye 2011).
   - *The sadist and the do-gooder*: a doctor and a sadist both see a terrible neck wound as bad, but for one the sight is unpleasant and for the other pleasant.
   - *Thinking otherwise*: after a hot pepper you gulp milk while knowing your mouth is not damaged, so the unpleasantness cannot be the evaluation "there is bad damage in my mouth".
2. **Against world-directed imperativism** (Martínez 2011).
   - *Hunger*: hunger always commands "More food!", but not all hunger is unpleasant.
   - *A salty story*: rats with damage to the central nucleus of the amygdala still show pleasure-type facial reactions to salt (Galaverna et al. 1993) but no longer seek salt (Flynn et al. 1991) — if pleasure were "More salt!", these rats would be issuing the wrong command.
3. **Against any world-directed theory.** A mood of misery is unpleasant without being about the world. And pain motivates you to get rid of *the experience*, which no content about the ankle can do.
4. **Reflexive imperativism.** Valence is a command about the experience itself. The decision-making system turns "Less of this sensation!" into a world-directed plan (drink milk); it can fail to find one (long-standing misery), or the link can be broken (the amygdala-lesioned rats). Most world-directed motivation (typing, affectless hunger) does not come from valence at all.
5. **Open question the author concedes.** If we already have world-directed motives and commands without feeling, why did evolution give us valence at all? "I don't know why that is the case" (ms. p. 8).

#### Initial Takeaway

The essay is a compact, non-technical statement of the reflexive-imperativist position around 2020, useful as a plain-language summary of arguments developed at length in Barlassina & Hayward (2019) and Barlassina (2020) (both in Part A). Two things matter for the field's history: it cites an **animal "liking vs. wanting" dissociation** (preserved hedonic taste reactions with abolished salt seeking) as evidence against world-directed imperatives, and it openly leaves the **function of valence** unexplained.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Technical Analysis

**The question.** Metaphysical, not causal: "You are God, and here is an experience that lacks valence. What should you add to it to turn it into an (un)pleasant experience?" (ms. p. 2). The answer is sought at the *cognitive* level — what information the state carries, how it is encoded and used — rather than the physical level.

**Background premise: valence as a natural kind.** Contra Bramble (2013)-style jokes about flowers and orgasms, three reasons from Carruthers (2018): (1) a single domain-general network activated across stimuli and modalities; (2) the same interventions (paracetamol) modulate physical and social pain; (3) valence can serve as a common currency for choice (ms. p. 1).

**Content schemas compared** (all in words in the essay; tabulated here):

| Theory | Content of a backache | Content of eating a cookie | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| World-directed evaluativism | the condition of the back is bad | the cookie is good | world |
| World-directed imperativism | Less of this damage in the back! | More of this cookie! | world |
| Reflexive imperativism | Less of me! (this experience) | More of me! (this experience) | the experience itself |

**Motivational architecture of reflexive imperativism (ms. pp. 6–7).** Experience with reflexive imperative → *mind-directed* motivation (for/against the experience) → decision-making system computes the best way to satisfy it → *world-directed* motivation (another bite; drink milk). Three qualifications:
1. The system may find no way to satisfy it (months of misery: unpleasantness and not wanting it, without world-directed motivation).
2. The causal chain can be interrupted: in amygdala-lesioned rats, the decision system is set to output "don't eat salt" regardless of the pleasant experience's "More of me!".
3. Most world-directed motivations are causally independent of affect (motor commands while typing; hunger episodes that feel neither good nor bad still motivate eating). Analogy: skiing normally makes one thirsty, but most thirst is not caused by skiing.

**Empirical sources cited.** Galaverna et al. (1993): bilateral central-amygdala lesions leave rats' hedonic taste-reactivity responses to salt intact. Flynn et al. (1991): such damage abolishes sodium appetite — rats reject solutions above 0.2% NaCl (ms. p. 4). These are cited, not re-analysed; the essay reports them in one sentence each.

#### Argument Reconstruction

**Against world-directed evaluativism.**
- *Sadist/do-gooder*: (P1) both experiences represent the injury as bad; (P2) one is unpleasant, one pleasant; (C) valence is not fixed by world-directed evaluative content.
- *Thinking otherwise*: (P1) if unpleasantness = "there is bad damage in my mouth", knowing it false should stop you acting on it; (P2) it does not; (C) unpleasantness ≠ that evaluation.

**Against world-directed imperativism.**
- *Hunger*: (P1) all hunger has "More food!"; (P2) not all hunger is unpleasant; (C) "More food!" does not make hunger unpleasant (sufficiency fails).
- *Salt*: (P1) lesioned rats show pleasure to salt; (P2) world-directed imperativism says pleasure = "More salt!"; (P3) the rats' behaviour fits "Less salt!"; (C) the pleasure is not "More salt!".

**Against all world-directed theories.**
- *World-undirected moods*: misery is unpleasant but not about the world → valence does not depend on world-directed content.
- *Mind-directed motivation*: pain motivates against the experience; world-directed contents can only motivate against states of the ankle → no world-directed content explains it.

**For reflexive imperativism.** It distinguishes neutral from valenced experiences with the same world-directed content (the pen; do-gooder vs. sadist), explains mind-directed motivation directly, and explains world-directed behaviour via the decision system, including failures (misery) and dissociations (the rats).

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- This is an opinion-style essay; each argument gets a paragraph, and replies by opponents are not considered. Kauppinen (2021, entry 1) rejects the mind-directed-motivation premise (grief motivates wishing the world different, not taking grief-killers) and offers a first-order reply to the hunger/polarity problem; Carruthers (2023, Part A) answers mood-type counterexamples with multiple appraisals.
- The rat argument infers "pleasure" from taste-reactivity facial expressions and treats lesion-abolished intake as evidence about the content of pleasure; how the same data bear on evaluativism is not discussed.
- The "Hunger" argument assumes a first-order imperativist must identify hunger's unpleasantness with "More food!"; Kauppinen's relational version adds background concern and authority to address this.
- The author concedes that the evolutionary function of valence is unexplained (ms. p. 8); Part A records a learning-based evolutionary rationale in Barlassina & Hayward (2019, §7.2); this essay does not mention it and says the author is "still puzzled" by the question.
