# Imperativism Field History — Part B2: Philosophy 2015–2019 (Imperativism and Evaluativism Mature)

**Topic folder:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/`
**Batch:** B2 of the field-history expansion. 8 references: 6 reviewed in full, 2 as short summaries. Reviewed one at a time, 2026-09-17.
**Reviewer:** literature-reviewer
**Companion files:** [[imperativism_lit_review_A_imperative_theories]] (Part A: Klein 2007, Carruthers 2018/2023, Barlassina & Hayward 2019 *Mind*, Barlassina 2020). Part A is not edited here.

## What this batch is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question behind the whole field.** Pain has a sensory side (where it is, how strong, burning or aching) and a side that makes it *feel bad* and *push you to act*. What is that second side?

**Where things stood in 2015.** Philosophers had two newer answers. **Imperativism** says the bad feeling is a *command*, such as "protect this ankle!". **Evaluativism** says it is an *experience of something as bad for you*, such as "this damage is bad for me". Both were still sketches.

**What happened in 2015–2019 (the eight papers here).**
- Imperativism got fleshed out:
  - a book-length defence (Klein 2015);
  - an account of how pain gives reasons (Martínez 2015);
  - a formal signalling-game model of why pain signals mainly tell you what to do rather than what is wrong (Martínez & Klein 2016);
  - an account of how one command can be *stronger* than another, i.e. pain intensity (Klein & Martínez 2018).
- Evaluativism got a handbook statement (Bain 2017) and a book review that pushed back on Klein (Bain 2017).
- The argument then moved to a sharper question: **why do people take painkillers?** Painkillers remove the feeling, not the damage.
  - Bain (2019) argues that the unpleasantness is bad in itself, and that we take painkillers because we *want* it gone.
  - Barlassina & Hayward (2019) argue that unpleasantness commands "**less of me!**", aimed at the experience itself rather than the body. They use rat studies in which liking a taste and seeking it come apart as evidence.

**Why it matters for the history.** In these years the field split two things apart: motivation to *protect the body* and motivation to *get rid of the feeling*. That distinction lines up with later empirical work that separates avoidance from relief. No paper here settles the debate. Each entry reports its claims as its authors made them.

## 1. Martinez (2015) — Pains as Reasons [tier: full]

**Citation:** Martínez, M. (2015). Pains as reasons. *Philosophical Studies* 172: 2261–2274. DOI 10.1007/s11098-014-0408-7
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Martinez 2015 - Pains as reasons.pdf`. Published Springer version, online 22 Nov 2014; journal pagination.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Martínez assumes imperativism about the *affective* (pleasant/unpleasant) component of pain. On his view, what makes a pain feel bad is that it carries a command whose schema is "**See to it that bodily damage *d* does not exist**" (p. 2261). The paper asks how such a command can do what pain obviously does:
- push us to act;
- give us *good* reasons to act;
- make us want to take painkillers.

**Key findings.**
1. Philosophers distinguish *motivating* reasons (what actually moves you) from *justifying* reasons (what makes an action sensible). Pain-related behaviour is either *body-directed* (tending an injury) or *pain-directed* (taking painkillers, asking for an epidural). That gives four claims, C1–C4, and Martínez treats all four as plausible.
2. Pains are "**benevolent-dictator imperatives**" (p. 2264): commands that force compliance and are usually also good advice. A parent's "switch off the light and go to sleep" is his example.
3. **How pain motivates.** Imagine every goal and feeling you have as a ranking of possible outcomes, and your action plan as the best compromise among those rankings. A pain is a ranking whose weight you cannot change much ("I'm a 0.25-to-0.27 kind of guy; now aggregate me", p. 2267).
4. **Why pain's advice is usually good.** Its content was fixed by evolution (teleosemantics), so obeying it has typically been useful.
5. **Why we take painkillers.** Martínez denies that pain inherently commands its own end: "No pain … is directing us to do something about itself" (p. 2269). We silence pains that have become "**spammy**", meaning insistent but useless. This is the same general tendency that makes clinicians click away excess alerts ("alarm fatigue"). Spammy cases include:
   - a toothache after the dentist is already booked;
   - surgical pain;
   - neuropathic pain;
   - hyperalgesia.
6. The principle that unpleasant pain is bad for you in itself ("Bad by Default", from Bain 2013) is "probably false" (p. 2272).

**Initial takeaway.** This is the paper that gave imperativism its standard answer to the painkiller question: seeking relief is a *derived* response to an unhelpful command, not something built into pain. That answer is the target of Bain (2019, [§7](#7-bain-2019--why-take-painkillers-tier-full)) and Barlassina & Hayward (2019, [§8](#8-barlassina--hayward-2019--loopy-regulations-the-motivational-profile-of-affective-phenomenology-tier-full)).

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Technical analysis

**Content and its semantics.**
- The imperative is individuated by *satisfaction conditions* to a first approximation (p. 2262).
- On refinement, an imperative induces a *ranking* `J` over possible worlds. "Fix my computer, and the sooner the better!" is satisfied by any fix but prefers quicker fixes (p. 2265).
- Footnotes 2–3 say this elaborates a model from Klein & Martínez ("forthcoming", reviewed here as [§6](#6-klein--martinez-2018--imperativism-and-pain-intensity-tier-full)). Strictly, content is the pair of a satisfaction set and a ranking. The paper restricts itself to "selfish imperatives".

**The aggregation model (§4.2).** An agent (Iris: sleepy, toothache, plans an all-nighter) has several conative states, each inducing a ranking. They are combined by seeking the *Kemeny-optimal* aggregate (Kemeny 1959; Ailon et al. 2008).

The distance between two rankings counts pairwise disagreements (the Kendall tau distance):

$$
d_{R_a,R_b}(i,j)=\begin{cases}1 & \text{if } i>_{R_a}j \text{ and } i<_{R_b}j,\ \text{or } i>_{R_b}j \text{ and } i<_{R_a}j\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}
$$

$$
K_\tau(R_a,R_b)=\sum_{i,j} d_{R_a,R_b}(i,j)
$$

The paper's worked example (p. 2266): with three worlds, the rankings `w1>w2>w3` and `w1>w3>w2` agree on the pairs (w1,w2) and (w1,w3) and disagree on (w2,w3), so the distance is 1. The fully reversed ranking `w3>w2>w1` disagrees on all three pairs, so the distance is 3.

Let `R_w` be the set of pairs of ranking `R_i` and weight `w_i ∈ [0,1]`. The paper's weighted distance from a candidate ranking `S` is

$$
D(S,R_w)=\sum_{\langle R_i,w_i\rangle\in R_w} w_i\,K_\tau(R_i,S)
$$

and the action-producing ranking minimises it:

$$
\forall S:\quad D(S,R_w)\ \ge\ D(R_{\text{action}},R_w)
$$

The agent then acts to place the actual world as high as possible in `R_action` (p. 2267).

*Reviewer notes on the formula.*
- The text calls `D` a "weighted average", but the printed formula is an unnormalised weighted sum. With the weights fixed, dividing by their total does not change which `S` minimises `D`, so the chosen action is the same.
- **Reviewer illustration** (not in the paper). Take two worlds, "tend the tooth now" (T) and "study" (St). The pain ranking is `T>St` with weight `w_p`; the study intention is `St>T` with weight `w_s`. Then `D(T>St) = w_s·1` and `D(St>T) = w_p·1`, so the aggregate follows the pain exactly when `w_p > w_s`.

  Martínez's claim is that pains allow only a narrow, externally fixed band of `w_p`. Personal-level deliberation can move the weights of intentions, but not pains: "we are simply wired in a way such that there is not much room for conscious, personal-level tinkering with the weights of pains" (p. 2267).

#### Argument reconstruction

| Step | Claim | Support in text |

**The two-world dilemma (§7).**
- Bain (personal communication) holds that a world W2, where damage heals without pain, is preferable to a world W1 where the same damage heals with pain.
- *Horn 1:* the pain in W1 plays no role in the healing. Then it is spammy, and silencing it is justified only extrinsically.
- *Horn 2:* the pain does play a role. Then W1 and W2 differ too much to support a judgement about intrinsic badness.

**What the paper leaves open.** Martínez calls the spam mechanism "one option … overlooked" and not necessarily the main mechanism; "That is to a large extent an empirical matter" (p. 2269). Barlassina & Hayward (2019, [§8](#8-barlassina--hayward-2019--loopy-regulations-the-motivational-profile-of-affective-phenomenology-tier-full)) treat it as his answer and attack it as a dilemma.

## 2. Klein (2015) — What the Body Commands: The Imperative Theory of Pain [tier: short]

**Citation:** Klein, C. (2015). *What the Body Commands: The Imperative Theory of Pain*. MIT Press. DOI 10.7551/mitpress/10480.001.0001
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Klein 2015 - What the body commands - The imperative theory of pain (publisher preview).pdf`
**Partial copy.** This is a 37-page MIT Press preview. It contains the front matter, the full table of contents, the acknowledgments, **Chapter 1 only** ("Puzzles about Pain", pp. 1–12) and the bibliography. Chapters 2–14 are absent. Everything below comes from Chapter 1 and the table of contents.

**Claim.**
- "pains are imperatives … that content is a command to protect a part of your body" (p. 1); for example, "avoid putting weight on" the ankle.
- Klein defends **pure imperativism**: pains have no psychologically relevant content beyond the command. It is combined with intentionalism about phenomenal character (pp. 6–9).
- Crucially, the theory is about *pain per se*. Following Hare (1964), Klein reserves "pain" for the bare sensation, which "carries no implication that the pain actually hurts" (p. 10). Motivation "to remove the pain itself" (hurt, suffering) is "not … part of the intrinsic motivational force of pain" (p. 12). The table of contents shows that unpleasantness and suffering get separate treatment:
  - ch. 4 "Pain and Suffering";
  - ch. 14, with subsections "Evaluativism" and "Second-Order Imperativism".

**Method / type.** Philosophical monograph that argues from pain's biological role. Pain belongs to the "homeostatic sensations" (thirst, hunger, itch, dyspnea), and "pains of recuperation" are the central case, following P. D. Wall (pp. 2–4).

**Key result or argument.**
- Two puzzles: pains are strongly and *directly* motivating, and pains are "remarkably uninformative" because their causes are so diverse (actual damage, potential damage, exertion, recuperation). "Ordinary sensations inform but don't necessarily motivate. Pains motivate without informing" (p. 3).
- Commands share exactly that profile: they are not truth-apt and are "directly and nondeliberatively action-guiding" (p. 5).
- So one component, a command, does what the "ordinary view" does with two (information plus an appended affect, desire or evaluation) (p. 3). "In ordinary cases, pain is a not [sic] a symptom but part of the cure" (p. 10).
- Location, intensity and quality are all to be explained as features of the commanded protection (pp. 8–9).
- The table of contents also lists: the body as a "minimal practical authority" (ch. 6); an ordinal account of intensity (ch. 8); "Why Not Felt Evaluations?" (ch. 10.6); pain asymbolia as "lost capacity" (chs. 11–12); masochism (ch. 13).

**Place in the field's history.** This is the first book-length defence of imperativism, and in its words the first pain monograph "in such a long time" (Bain's review, §5). It consolidated Klein (2007, 2010, 2012). It also fixed the *body-protection* version of imperativism that later work either extends (Martínez & Klein 2016; Klein & Martínez 2018) or rejects as the wrong target (Barlassina & Hayward 2019 call Klein's view of unpleasantness "higher-order imperativism", p. 240).

**Relevance.** It sets the main alternative framing for this corpus. On this framing, the push to protect the body belongs to the pain signal itself, while the *badness* of pain is a separate, typically but not necessarily present layer.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2015 · philosophy
- Question it asked: What common feature makes a sensation a pain, and how can one theory explain pain's direct motivational force together with its uninformativeness?
- Position / finding in one line: Pains are pure imperatives commanding protection of a body part; this explains why they motivate without informing, while hurting (unpleasantness) is treated separately from pain per se.
- Responds to / builds on: Klein 2007, 2010, 2012; Hall 2008; Martínez 2010/2011; Bain 2011; Hare 1964; Wall (on recuperation); Lewis 1969; Raz 1986; Byrne 2001; Sherrington (epigraph)
- Measures (empirical only): n.a. (conceptual monograph)
- Dissociation reported (empirical only): n.a. (conceptual monograph)
- Survey claim check: yes, with a scope caveat. The survey lists this as a key imperativist source; "imperativism explains pain's motivational force and uninformativeness directly, and predicts its content is action-directed". Chapter 1 says exactly this: "Pains motivate without informing" (p. 3), and the content "is a command to protect a part of your body" (p. 1). *Caveat:* the command theory is about pain per se, not its unpleasantness; pain "carries no implication that the pain actually hurts" (p. 10). Only 12 of roughly 200 pages were available.
- PDF version: publisher preview

## 3. Martinez & Klein (2016) — Pain Signals Are Predominantly Imperative [tier: full]

**Citation:** Martínez, M. & Klein, C. (2016). Pain signals are predominantly imperative. *Biology & Philosophy*. DOI 10.1007/s10539-015-9514-y
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Martinez and Klein 2016 - Pain signals are predominantly imperative.pdf`
**Version caveat.** This is the **author manuscript**: a LaTeX draft, 28 manuscript pages, PDF created Dec 2015, no journal typesetting. Volume and page numbers are not given because the PDF does not show them. Citations are "ms. p."

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Imperativists usually state pain's content as an English sentence, such as "Protect your nail!". The authors call that helpful but "ultimately unsatisfactory if taken literally" (ms. p. 3), because sensations are not sentences.

They recast the claim in the **Lewis–Skyrms sender–receiver framework**, where a signal's content can be measured:
- A *sender* sees the state of the world and sends a message; a *receiver* acts on it.
- A message is *indicative* to the extent that it carries information about world states.
- It is *imperative* to the extent that it carries information about the receiver's acts.

**Key findings.**
1. **The model.** Nail-bed nociceptors (sender) signal to motor cortex (receiver). The states are severe, mild or no mechanical stimulation; the acts are protect with very high, high or low priority, or remain at ease. There is also a background condition, *inflammation*, which the sender detects but the receiver does not.
2. **Inflammation shifts the best act** one step toward stronger protection: mild touch on an inflamed toe calls for what severe touch would otherwise call for.
3. **Result.** In a best-paying equilibrium, messages carry more information about acts than about states whenever the probability of inflammation is strictly between 0 and 1 (and not very near the ends). The gap is largest at 1/2 (Fig. 1, ms. p. 13). The paraphrase becomes "M1: Protect your nail (with very high priority) … M4: Don't do anything" (ms. pp. 12–14).
4. **Why.** Many different causes map onto the same few protective acts. A message tells you what will be done but not which state produced it.
5. **Three indicativist replies are rejected.** (a) Redefining states as disjunctions. (b) Adding more messages: pain physiology and McGill-questionnaire descriptors do not track injury type. (c) Rephrasing commands as evaluations, as in Bain's evaluativism. The authors say (c) inherits the problems of the "guise of the good" thesis.

**Initial takeaway.** This is the paper that made "imperative vs. indicative" a quantitative, testable-in-principle distinction for pain. The claim is comparative ("predominantly"). The model shows that *heterogeneous causes mapped onto unified protective acts* make a signal more about action than about the world.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Formal apparatus (§2)

A game has states `S`, messages `M`, acts `A`, a sender rule (a state × message matrix of probabilities), a receiver rule (a message × act matrix) and payoffs. Informational content follows Skyrms (2010, ch. 3):

$$
H(S)=-\sum_{i}\Pr(S_i)\log_2\Pr(S_i),\qquad H(S\mid M)=\sum_{j}\Pr(M_j)\,H(S\mid M=M_j)
$$

$$
I(S;M)=H(S)-H(S\mid M)\quad\text{(indicative)},\qquad I(A;M)=H(A)-H(A\mid M)\quad\text{(imperative)}
$$

*Manuscript note:* the printed `H(S)` (ms. p. 6) lacks the leading minus sign. This was verified on the rendered page and is a typo; the standard form is shown above.

Following Huttegger (2007) and Zollman (2011), messages are **predominantly imperative** iff `I(M;A) ≫ I(M;S)` and predominantly indicative iff the reverse (ms. pp. 8–9). If neither holds, they approximate Millikan's (1995) "pushmi-pullyu" representations. Footnote 4 gives Skyrms's pointwise measure for a single message–state pair, `log Pr(S_i|M_j)/Pr(S_i)`, whose weighted average is the mutual information.

The paper shows the two quantities can come apart:
- An identity receiver with a fully pooling sender (every row 1/3) gives `I(M;A) = log2 3` and `I(M;S) = 0` (ms. p. 8).
- Such configurations are not Nash equilibria. The pain game is introduced because its messages stay predominantly imperative *in* equilibrium (ms. p. 9).

| S1 severe | | 0 | **1** | 0 | −1 | **1** | 0 | −1 | −2 |
| S2 mild | | −1 | 0 | **1** | 0 | 0 | **1** | 0 | −1 |
| S3 none | | −2 | −1 | 0 | **1** | −1 | 0 | **1** | 0 |

This is Table 4 (ms. p. 11). Sender and receiver share payoffs: 1 for the optimal act and −1 per step away from it. Inflammation moves the optimum one column left.

Best-paying equilibrium (Tables 5–6, ms. p. 12):
- Receiver: identity (`M_k → A_k`).
- Sender, normal: `S1→M2`, `S2→M3`, `S3→M4`.
- Sender, inflamed: `S1→M1`, `S2→M2`, `S3→M3`.

**Reviewer derivation** of Fig. 1 in closed form, from the paper's stated assumptions (equiprobable states; `q` = probability of inflammation):
- Message probabilities: `Pr(M1) = q/3`, `Pr(M2) = Pr(M3) = 1/3`, `Pr(M4) = (1−q)/3`.
- The receiver is deterministic, so `H(A|M) = 0` and `I(A;M) = H(M)`.
- Given M2 the state is S1 (normal) with probability `1−q` or S2 (inflamed) with probability `q`; M3 is symmetric; M1 and M4 identify the state exactly.

Writing `h(q) = −q log2 q − (1−q) log2(1−q)` for the binary entropy,

$$
I(A;M)=H(M)=-\tfrac{q}{3}\log_2\tfrac{q}{3}-\tfrac{2}{3}\log_2\tfrac{1}{3}-\tfrac{1-q}{3}\log_2\tfrac{1-q}{3}=\log_2 3+\tfrac{1}{3}h(q)
$$

$$
H(S\mid M)=\tfrac{1}{3}h(q)+\tfrac{1}{3}h(q)=\tfrac{2}{3}h(q)\quad\Rightarrow\quad I(S;M)=\log_2 3-\tfrac{2}{3}h(q)
$$

$$
I(A;M)-I(S;M)=h(q)
$$

So the imperative surplus equals the binary entropy of inflammation:
- It is zero at `q = 0` or `1`, where both measures equal `log2 3 ≈ 1.585` bits.
- It peaks at `q = 1/2`: `I(A;M) ≈ 1.918` bits vs `I(S;M) ≈ 0.918` bits.

This matches the paper's verbal description (ms. p. 12) and Fig. 1's axis range of 0.8–2.0 bits.

*Reading note for the synthesis.* In this model "≫" amounts to at most about a 2:1 ratio, a surplus of at most 1 bit. "Predominantly" is therefore a modest comparative claim. The indicative measure is computed over the three stimulation states only; inflammation is a background condition, not part of `S`. That choice is exactly what §4.1 defends.

**Empirical motivation for the sender/receiver asymmetry.** Sensitisation is "largely a peripheral process, occurring primarily at the nociceptor and the spinal gate" (ms. p. 14; Woolf & Mannion 1999; Melzack & Wall 1965). The nociceptor "sees" inflammation; the receiver "only 'sees' the firing".

#### Argument reconstruction (§4: three indicativist moves)

| Move | What it does | Authors' reply |
|---|---|---|
| **4.1 Rearranging** | Redefine states as disjunctions, e.g. S*1 = severe-and-normal or mild-and-inflamed; all best equilibria then become full signalling systems | Brute disjunction collapses the imperative/indicative distinction for any imperative and misses generalisations (Fodor 1997). The only common property of the disjuncts is the appropriate protective response, which re-describes the act. Inflammation (endogenous, often chronic) and mechanical stimulation (external, brief) differ in etiology and time course (ms. pp. 14–16) |
| **4.2 Recarving** | Posit more messages (M1–M6), different under inflammation; perfect information about both (Tables 7–8) | Called an empirical question, but the evidence runs against it. *Peripheral physiology:* specificity theory's decline, Noordenbos's pattern view, gate control, and Wall & McMahon's (1985) microneurography finding of no simple fibre-to-quality mapping. *Phenomenology:* McGill descriptors do not track injury type ("Cuts are often described as burning, stabs as sore, fractures as hot", ms. p. 20; Melzack et al. 1982). Quality could still encode *kind of protective act*, which keeps the degeneracy of states onto acts (ms. pp. 16–20) |
| **4.3 Reinterpreting** | Rephrase commands as indicatives: "Things are such that you should protect your nail" | Either this is "British Imperativism" (polite imperatives), or it is evaluativism (Bain 2013: "this tissue damage is bad for you"). Evaluativism is a case of the *guise of the good* (Velleman 1992). On Schroeder's (2008) strong reading goodness must be *in the content*, which is too demanding for "babies and animals" (ms. p. 22). The weak reading (motivation *aims at* the good) is compatible with imperativism. Reasons can come from non-wilful imperatives (Martínez 2015), the body as minimal practical authority (Klein 2015) or epistemic authority (Bain 2011). The difference is *who deliberates*: the receiver for indicatives, the sender for imperatives (Lewis 1969; Huttegger 2007) (ms. pp. 20–24) |

**Closing thesis (ms. p. 24).** Sterelny (2003) argued that representations are useful because they are decoupled from particular actions. Imperatives play "a structurally complimentary role: they are 'decoupled' … from states of the world". "Neither imperatives nor indicatives are more primitive."

## 4. Bain (2017) — Evaluativist Accounts of Pain's Unpleasantness [tier: full]

**Citation:** Bain, D. (2017). Evaluativist accounts of pain's unpleasantness. In J. Corns (ed.), *The Routledge Handbook of Philosophy of Pain*, Routledge. DOI 10.4324/9781315742205-4
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Bain 2017 - Evaluativist accounts of pains unpleasantness.pdf`
**Version caveat.** This is an **author manuscript**: word-processed, 10 manuscript pages with endnotes. It cites "in preparation" and "forthcoming" works and refers to other handbook chapters by number. Citations are "ms. p."

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** A short handbook chapter that states, locates, motivates and stress-tests **evaluativism**, the view held by Helm (2001, 2002), Cutter & Tye (2011) and Bain (2013):
1. Being in pain = having an interoceptive experience that represents a bodily condition (a "disturbance", damage, or danger).
2. The pain's being **unpleasant** = its *additionally* "representing that condition as bad for you" (ms. p. 1).

**Key findings.**
1. **What kind of view it is.**
   - *Composite*: it leaves room for non-unpleasant pains, as under morphine.
   - *First-order*: the represented badness is "b-badness", the badness of the bodily condition, not of the pain.
   - *Content-based*, not functionalist.
   - *Cognitivist*: pains have truth-conditions. This is the contrast with imperativism's "Stop this bodily condition!".
   - *Phenomenological*.
2. **Rivals.** The second-order desire view, the "orthodoxy" (unpleasantness = wanting the pain not to occur), and the first-order desire view (wanting the represented bodily condition not to obtain).
3. **Motivations.**
   - It saves representationalism (Cutter & Tye).
   - Degrees of badness explain degrees of unpleasantness.
   - Most centrally for Bain, unpleasant pain is **itself** a motivating reason. It rationalises action "by dint of representing justifying reasons" (ms. p. 3), with no extra desire needed. Pains are "reason-constituting urges", between brute causes and desires.
4. **Extensions.**
   - Belief can change unpleasantness (Beecher's soldiers), read as cognitive penetration.
   - Pain asymbolia: patients who feel pain but are not bothered by it. They lack *care* for their body, and caring is a condition on representing threats as bad.
   - The view generalises to pleasure and emotion.
5. **Open challenges.**
   - What b-badness is: objective harm-aptness or subjective desire-frustration.
   - Whether the neutral content coheres ("bodily disturbances" is admitted to be "a promissory note", ms. p. 5).
   - How badness could be perceived (Aydede's causal worry).
   - The **normative ("messenger-shooting") objection**: representing badness is not itself bad.
   - A residual worry: evaluativism explains motivation to fix the body but not motivation to take painkillers.

**Initial takeaway.** This is a compact reference statement of evaluativism around 2017. It names the painkiller problem that Bain (2019) takes on at length.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### The four positions the chapter distinguishes

| View | Pain consists in | Unpleasantness consists in | Order | Truth-apt? |

| Evaluativism | interoceptive representation of a bodily condition | additionally representing that condition as bad for you | first | yes |
| First-order desire view (Jacobson; Aydede 2014; Aydede & Fulkerson) | same | an experience-based intrinsic desire that the represented condition not obtain | first | no |

#### Argument reconstruction — the case for evaluativism (§2)

1. **From representationalism** (Cutter & Tye 2011, pp. 96, 98–101). If phenomenal character is representational content, and two pains can match in descriptive content while differing in unpleasantness, then they must differ in some *non-descriptive*, evaluative content.
2. **From intensity.** b-badness comes in degrees, as brightness does, so more unpleasant pains represent conditions as worse (Cutter & Tye 2011, pp. 98, 104). Klein & Martínez ([§6](#6-klein--martinez-2018--imperativism-and-pain-intensity-tier-full)) reply to this.
3. **From motivation and rationalisation** (Helm 2002; Bain 2013).
   - (a) A visual experience of a wide boulder is motivationally inert without a desire. An unpleasant pain from a hand in hot water is "itself motivational" (ms. p. 2).
   - (b) Pain explanations are perspectival and normative, like belief–desire explanations, not like a volcano erupting.
   - (c) Best explanation: the pain "represents a good reason for doing so, namely your hand's bad state" (ms. p. 3).
   - (d) Imperativism does worse: "a child might command you to stand without your having any inclination to do so", and obeying a police officer is explained by a further desire (Bain 2011, 2013).
   - (e) Desire views either collapse into evaluativism (if desires are evaluative seemings, as for Oddie and Helm) or struggle to rationalise.
4. **Mere pain talk** ("my feet feel bad") is judged "not really probative".

#### Challenges and replies (§3)

| Challenge | Content | Replies canvassed |
|---|---|---|
| Nature of b-badness | If it is spooky, the naturalisation problem is only deferred | Objective: aptness to impede Darwinian proper functioning (Cutter & Tye 2011, 99–100), possibly too thin to justify. Subjective: desire-frustration (not the first-order desire view) or a disposition to cause unpleasantness (circularity worry, with McDowell's colour parallel). Helm's no-priority view; error theory |

| Perceiving badness (Aydede 2006) | On a tracking psychosemantics, extrinsic badness (the same cut is worse where bacteria abound) cannot cause the tracking state | Cutter & Tye: selection explains why cuts of this intrinsic type cause "H-states". Critics: the same H-state would occur in clean conditions. "The debate continues." Alternative functional-role psychosemantics; high-level perceptual contents are common |
| Normative / messenger-shooting objection (Aydede & Fulkerson; Brady 2015; Jacobson 2013) | Believing you are terminally ill is not itself bad, so why should *representing* bodily badness be? | (i) Deny that unpleasantness is non-instrumentally bad (Martínez 2015); evaluativists do not take this route. (ii) Tu quoque: the first-order desire view fails for central neuropathic pain, where the anti-damage desire is not frustrated. (iii) Badness via anti-unpleasantness desires (Cutter & Tye 2014), with the "Strangelove" cost. (iv) Perception differs from belief: in perception badness is *encountered* or *impressed* on you (Evans; McDowell). Normative explanation must end somewhere |
| Motivation for painkillers | Evaluativism explains body-directed but not pain-directed motivation | Anti-unpleasantness desires "exist and are what motivates our reaching for the aspirin" (ms. p. 7) |

## 5. Bain (2017) — Review of Klein, What the Body Commands [tier: short]

**Citation:** Bain, D. (2017). Review of *What the Body Commands* by Colin Klein. *Australasian Journal of Philosophy*. DOI 10.1080/00048402.2017.1355928
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Bain 2017 - Review of What the Body Commands by Colin Klein.pdf`. Deposited in the University of Glasgow "Enlighten" repository, 7 Nov 2017, under a cover sheet stating "author's final accepted version". Three manuscript pages. Bracketed page numbers below are Bain's citations to Klein's book.

**Claim.** The book is "original, empirically informed, clear, and often persuasive" (ms. p. 1), but its accounts of motivation, unpleasantness, location and quality face serious worries.

**Method / type.** Critical book review: summary followed by objections.

**Key result or argument.**
- **Summary.**
  - Klein's view is "an account not of pain's unpleasantness … but of pain per se" (ms. p. 1). Pain is a body-issued command to protect a part, with imperative *mood in the content*.
  - In the final chapter Klein "tentatively" treats unpleasantness as "commanding yourself not to have it [186–7]". Bain names the resulting view "**double imperativism**".
  - Bain credits Klein as "alone" in denying that pains are truth-apt, and alone in holding that even *non-unpleasant* pains motivate (postural adjustments).
  - The argument rests on pain's role in *recuperation*: people with congenital insensitivity die from "repeated, trivial, but unhealed" damage [29–30].
  - Replies Klein gives:
    - reasons come from accepting the body as a "practical authority" (Hobbes, Hart, Raz) [72–81];
    - asymbolia is a lost capacity to care about bodily integrity [143, 156];
    - intensity is urgency, a ranking over satisfaction worlds;
    - location is the part one is commanded to protect;
    - quality is the manner of protection ("withdraw … quickly" vs "treat … gingerly").
- **Worries.**
  - Escaping a scalding shower is defence, not recuperation.
  - Mild pains may be *mildly unpleasant*. Lobotomy reports may reflect loss of downstream anxiety rather than of unpleasantness.
  - "Acceptance of authority" is either trivial or over-intellectualises infant and animal pain.
  - Why do asymbolics not self-command? Not all intrusive urges are unpleasant ("the urge to stop running when you see a cliff edge").
  - Location via care predicts that asymbolic pains lack location, and that hunger is felt in the mouth.
  - Very fine-grained quality-commands would preclude the deliberation Klein allows.

**Relevance.** It documents, from a critic, the scope distinction that surveys tend to blur: pain versus pain's unpleasantness. It also records an early empirical challenge to the claim that neutral pains motivate.

## 6. Klein & Martinez (2018) — Imperativism and Pain Intensity [tier: full]

**Citation:** Klein, C. & Martínez, M. (2018). Imperativism and pain intensity. In D. Bain, M. Brady & J. Corns (eds.), Routledge edited volume (cited in 2014–2015 manuscripts as *The Nature of Pain*; the published title is not shown in the PDF). DOI 10.4324/9781351115865-2
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Klein and Martinez 2018 - Imperativism and pain intensity.pdf`
**Version caveat.** This is an **early author manuscript** (LaTeX, 21 manuscript pages). It cites Klein's book and Martínez's "Pains as Reasons" as "forthcoming", so it was written around 2014. That is before items 1–3 were published, even though the chapter appeared in 2018. It contains typos ("Gailure", "mutis mutandis"). The published text may differ. Citations are "ms. p."

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Pains vary in intensity. Critics suggested imperativism cannot explain this, since commands do not track magnitudes in the world (Cutter & Tye 2011; Pautz 2010). The authors answer in two steps: (1) imperatives come in degrees; (2) the degree is part of the imperative's *content*, which keeps imperativism intentionalist.

**Key findings.**
1. "Pass the salt now!" is a more intense command than "Please pass the salt at your earliest convenience!" (ms. p. 3). Their model is the Cold War **Autovon** telephone precedence system, where higher-precedence calls pre-empt lower ones.
2. **Three things intensity is not.**
   - Illocutionary force or politeness. This answers Cutter & Tye's charge that urgency would break intentionalism.
   - Temporal priority, which is extrinsic: the toe ache matters less "when the bear appears".
   - A tracked worldly magnitude. This answers Pautz's challenge about what makes one pain "twice" another.
3. **Ratio or order?** Whether pain intensity is a true ratio-scale magnitude is contested (Price et al. 1983 vs Hall 1981). The model is built to work even if intensity is only ordinal.
4. **Content model.** An imperative's content is its satisfaction worlds *plus* a ranking over all possible worlds. Intensity is defined by how imperatives rank satisfying themselves "at the expense of" each other, checked against third imperatives when needed.
5. **For pain.**
   - One pain is more intense than another when the rankings agree you should tend the first body part at the other's expense.
   - Ratios can be recovered from indifference points (standard gambles).
   - Comparisons may be cardinal *within* a modality (pain vs pain) but only ordinal *across* modalities (pain vs hunger).
   - Weighting rankings in aggregation could model individual differences in how much pain matters.

**Initial takeaway.** This paper supplies imperativism's formal account of intensity and separates *intensity* (inside the command) from *priority* (context-dependent). It also shows the early, ranking-based formal core shared with Martínez (2015).

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Content with a ranking (§3.2)

The satisfaction set alone cannot distinguish "Raise money for Oxfam!" read as *any amount* from the reading *the more the better* (ms. p. 11). So the content of imperative `i` is the pair

$$
i=\langle W_{sc},\ \succsim\rangle
$$

where `W_sc` is the set of satisfaction worlds and `≿` is a ranking over **all** possible worlds (`w_i ≿ w_j`: ranked at least as high). A simple imperative partitions worlds into two equivalence classes; a graded one partitions `W_sc` further.

The ad hoc alternative `⟨W_sc, d⟩`, with a bare intensity number `d`, is rejected as "under-motivated" (ms. p. 10).

Two observations drive the account:
- `≿` ranks worlds where *other* imperatives are satisfied.
- It may rank another imperative's satisfaction *above* its own. An Autovon "immediate" call should yield to a "flash" call.

#### Definitions (ms. p. 13), transcribed

- *At the expense of:* `i` is satisfied at the expense of `j` iff `i` is satisfied and `j` is not, **or** `i` is satisfied temporally before `j`.
- *Semi-preferable:* `i` is semi-preferable to `j` relative to some (possibly hypothetical) distinct imperative `k` iff
  1. both `i` and `j` rank all worlds where `i` is satisfied at the expense of `j` above worlds where `j` is satisfied at the expense of `i`; **or**
  2. `i` and `j` cannot be satisfied at each other's expense, but
     - (a) `i` ranks all worlds where it is satisfied at `k`'s expense above those where `k` is satisfied at `i`'s expense, and `j` does not; **or**
     - (b) `i` ranks some worlds where it is satisfied at `k`'s expense as not worse than the converse, and `j` ranks all worlds where it is satisfied at `k`'s expense as worse than the converse.
- **Intensity:** `i` is more intense than `j` iff there is some imperative relative to which `i` is semi-preferable to `j`, and none relative to which `j` is semi-preferable to `i`.

**Worked cases (from the paper).**
- *Clause 1, Autovon.* A flash call ranks its connection above a routine call's, and the routine call agrees. The pattern is consistent across levels, so the flash call is more intense.
- *Clause 2, prophet vs pastor.* Both command "Repent of your sins, and the sooner the better!", with identical `W_sc`. Take `k` = "move your car out of the fire lane". The prophet's imperative ranks repentance above `k`; the pastor's ranks `k` above immediate repentance. The prophet's imperative is semi-preferable and hence more intense, "by triangulating against other possible things you might do" (ms. p. 14).

**Consequences (§3.3).**
- *Incommensurability.* Two "mutually selfish" imperatives, each ranking its own satisfaction above the other's (Oxfam vs the department chair's "repaint your office"), are not semi-preferable either way, so neither is more intense. Priority can still be settled by extrinsic factors.
- *From order to ratios.* Suppose `≿` has indifference points: satisfying `i` is on a par with `n` units of some good `x`, and satisfying `j` with `2n` units. Then `j` is "twice as intense" as `i` (ms. pp. 15–16). Footnote 11: this holds when the rankings satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, so a cardinal function exists without implying a real magnitude underneath (Ellsberg 1954).

#### Application to pain (§3.4)

- One pain is more intense than another "just in case their world-rankings agree that one should tend to the body part involved in the first pain at the expense of the one in the second" (ms. p. 16).
- Intensity differences within one body part can be cashed out as "standard gambles against other valued goods".

**Cross-modal comparison** (paper's example, ms. p. 17; `∼` = ranked equally):

$$
\succsim_1:\ \neg\text{pain}_1\ \sim\ [\text{pain}_1\wedge\$2]\ >\ \neg\text{pain}_2\ \sim\ [\text{pain}_2\wedge\$1]\ >\ \neg\text{hunger}_1\ >\ \neg\text{hunger}_2
$$

$$
\succsim_2:\ \neg\text{pain}_1\ >\ \neg\text{pain}_2\ >\ \neg\text{hunger}_1\ \sim\ [\text{hunger}_1\wedge\$2]\ >\ \neg\text{hunger}_2\ \sim\ [\text{hunger}_2\wedge\$1]
$$

Both rankings place pain above hunger, but each has monetary (cardinal) structure within only one modality. So "cross-modality comparisons are merely ordinal, but intra-modality ones can be cardinal". This is offered as a possible explanation of why "some hunger is twice as intense as a pain" sounds odd.

**Aggregation (footnote 12).** Weighted Kendall-tau aggregation (Ailon et al. 2008), as in Martínez (2015). The weights "might be interpreted as marking individual differences in the importance of pain" (ms. p. 17).

**Naturalisation (§3.5).**
- Portner's (2007) "To-do list" semantics for imperatives, an action analogue of Stalnaker's common ground.
- Teleosemantics (Millikan 1984; Martínez 2010), with motor cortex as a candidate consumer.
- "A well-adapted biological agent thus needs both information about what to do and about how important it is to do" (ms. p. 19).

1. **§1 Introduction (ms. pp. 1–3).** Two content formulations: Martínez's "See to it that this bodily state does not exist!" and Klein's "Protect this body part (in this manner)!". Itch and hunger as other imperative sensations (Hall 2008). The objection that imperativism cannot handle intensity; the two-step plan. Footnote 2: perceptual "mandates" (Siegel) and practical perception (Bengson).

## 7. Bain (2019) — Why Take Painkillers? [tier: full]

**Citation:** Bain, D. (2019). Why take painkillers? *Noûs* 53(2): 462–490. DOI 10.1111/nous.12228
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Bain 2019 - Why take painkillers.pdf`
**Version caveat.** This is the **accepted manuscript**: a University of Glasgow Enlighten cover sheet ("peer reviewed version", deposited 7 Nov 2017), then a manuscript headed "Forthcoming in Noûs 2017". Citations are "ms. p." using the printed numbers; ms. p. 1 is PDF p. 2.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Ask someone why they took a painkiller: "I was in pain … it was unpleasant … unpleasant pain is a bad state to be in." Painkillers do not repair damage, so "We don't take painkillers to prevent bodily damage, but to prevent or reduce unpleasant pain" (ms. p. 1). Bain uses this to state two conditions that a theory of unpleasantness might have to meet:
- **Normative condition.** Whatever unpleasant pain consists in must be *non-instrumentally bad* for its subject (ms. p. 1).
- **Self-elimination condition.** It must be able to *itself* motivate action aimed at reducing the unpleasant pain (ms. p. 2).

Critics (Aydede, Brady, Fulkerson, Jacobson) say his evaluativism fails both.

**Key findings.**
1. **Three places where badness sits** in a rotten-tooth case: the damage (upstream), the anxiety and anger (downstream), and the unpleasantness in between, which is "also—and independently—bad for you" (ms. p. 1).
2. **Denying that unpleasantness is bad in itself fails.** This "instrumentalism" is Martínez's (2015) view: pain is bad because it intrudes on your plans. Bain's cases against it:
   - pleasures are intrusive too;
   - brief torture is unbearable though its intrusion is fleeting;
   - the worry "this pain might get worse" is not a worry about intrusion or emotion;
   - "Stan" on the beach: missing the sunset because of a harmless, emotionless stab of pain is slightly worse than missing it because of a rustle in the bushes.
3. **The critics' own desire views fail the normative condition.**
   - The *second-order desire view* posits anti-**pain** desires. When we cry "I want the pain to stop" we want the **unpleasantness** gone: morphine that removes the unpleasantness but leaves the pain satisfies that wish.
   - The *first-order desire view*'s anti-damage desire is not frustrated in illusory (central neuropathic) pain, yet that pain is still bad.
   - Subjective versions fail on thought experiments: a woman who knows the Müller-Lyer lines are equal; a man in a VR cliff-edge simulation.
   - The best repair of the first-order view turns out to *be* evaluativism.
4. **Evaluativism can meet the normative condition** in two ways.
   - (a) Anti-unpleasantness desires make unpleasantness bad. Bain calls this non-instrumental but extrinsic, and costly for "Coolman" (indifferent to his pain) and "Strangelove" (wants it).
   - (b) Preferred, the **perceptual strategy**: *perceiving* your body's state as bad for you is intrinsically bad. In perception badness is putatively *encountered* or *impressed* on you, unlike being told or believing.
5. **Self-elimination is denied, not met.** Painkiller-taking is "motivated not by your pain's unpleasantness per se, but only by desires (intrinsic and extrinsic) for that unpleasantness not to be occurring" (ms. p. 20).

**Initial takeaway.** For the field's history this paper does two things:
- It makes "what motivates relief-seeking?" a named test (the self-elimination condition).
- It gives the evaluativist answer: the drive to protect the body comes from the unpleasant experience itself, while the drive to get rid of the feeling comes from separate desires.

Barlassina & Hayward ([§8](#8-barlassina--hayward-2019--loopy-regulations-the-motivational-profile-of-affective-phenomenology-tier-full)) reject both conditions as constraints of the wrong kind, and reject the answer.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### The positions and strategies mapped

| # | Strategy | Account of unpleasantness | Account of its badness | Bain's verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Instrumentalism (Martínez 2015; similar to Klein 2015b) | imperative content | bad effects: intrusiveness on action plans; for Klein, compromised agency | fails: pleasures intrude; torture intolerability; worry about worsening; Stan case (ms. pp. 3–5) |
| 2 | Desire strategy (in SOD form) | anti-pain desire | narrow-scope Humeanism: if S wants not-x, x is pro tanto non-instrumentally bad for S | mislocates badness in pain per se; we lack anti-pain desires (morphine and substituted-itch cases) (ms. pp. 6–7) |
| 3 | Desire-frustration strategy (SOD / FOD) | anti-pain or anti-damage desire | wide-scope Humeanism: having an anti-x desire while x obtains is bad | frustration is not a feeling; the anti-desire-pill oddity; FOD fails Pain Illusion; "additionalist" (ms. pp. 6–9) |
| 4a | Believed desire-frustration (FOD) | anti-damage desire | you *believe* the desire frustrated | fails when God persuades you there is no damage (ms. pp. 9–10) |
| 4b | Experienced desire-frustration, narrow scope | anti-damage desire | you want not-x while experiencing x | Linda (Müller-Lyer) and Ed (VR cliff) need not be badly off (ms. pp. 10–12) |
| 4c | Experienced desire-frustration, wide scope → "Better FOD" → "Best FOD" | experience representing damage *as frustrating a desire of yours* → as *bad for you* | same | most promising, but Best FOD "is actually a version of evaluativism!" (ms. p. 13) |
| 5 | Additionalist second-order desire (Cutter & Tye 2014) | evaluative content | anti-unpleasantness desires (narrow-scope Humeanism) | non-instrumental but extrinsic; Coolman / Strangelove; babies (ms. pp. 14–17) |
| 6 | Perceptual strategy (Bain) | evaluative content | perceiving bodily badness is *intrinsically* bad | defended against five doubts (ms. pp. 17–20) |

**Definition used throughout (ms. p. 9).** A strategy is *additionalist* iff it explains the non-instrumental badness of unpleasant pain "in terms of something additional to what constitutes your unpleasant pain".

**Scope distinction (ms. p. 12)**, introduced as new:
- *Narrow scope:* you desire not-x, and you experience that x.
- *Wide scope:* you desire not-x, and you experience that x obtains *while you want it not to*.

This is kept separate from narrow- vs wide-scope Humeanism.

#### Argument reconstruction — the perceptual strategy (§8)

**Leibniz's Law argument (ms. p. 17).**
1. Your pain's unpleasantness is bad for you intrinsically.
2. Your pain's unpleasantness is a matter of undergoing a perceptual experience representing a damaged state of your body as bad for you. (Evaluativism.)
3. So undergoing such a perceptual experience is intrinsically bad for you.

Bain notes that circularity would arise only if the case for (2) relied on (3). It does not: his own case rests on motivation and rationalisation (Bain 2017).

**Five doubts and replies.**

| Doubt | Reply |
|---|---|
| Why think the experience is bad? | The Leibniz's Law argument above |
| Reductio: reject (2) because (3) is absurd | Even critics do not find (3) absurd; Brady calls it "natural and intuitive" (2015: 406) |
| (3) is less obvious than (1) | "… is obviously bad for one" is an intensional context; informative reductions need not preserve obviousness (compare Moore's open-question argument) |
| Evaluativists have not explained *why* the experience is bad | Neither have desire theorists explained why frustration is bad; "normative explanation presumably comes to an end somewhere" (ms. p. 18) |
| Normative contrast: why isn't a *judgement* with the same content bad? | This reduces to the general **phenomenal contrast** question (why seeing red differs from judging red), which every representationalist faces. Candidate answers: perception "impresses" circumstances on you (Evans 1982; McDowell 1994) or involves putative "encounter" |

**Further support Bain cites.**
- It captures Klein's "outside imposition" (2015b: 182–3, 193), but as impingement on *awareness* rather than agency.
- Imagined pain and depersonalisation-syndrome pain lack putative encounter and are reportedly not unpleasant (Sierra 2009).
- Messenger-shooting is answered: encountering badness is unlike being told about it.

#### The self-elimination condition (end of §8)

- Evaluativists "should simply deny that pain's unpleasantness is self-eliminating" (ms. p. 20).
- Unpleasantness-directed actions such as taking painkillers are motivated only by desires that the unpleasantness not occur. Actions aimed at the *damage* are motivated by the unpleasant experience itself (ms. p. 3; see also Bain 2017).
- Footnote 50: the second-order desire view also fails the condition, because its desires are anti-pain rather than anti-unpleasantness.
- *For the synthesis.* This is a specific evaluativist claim about **two motivational routes**. The body-protective route is intrinsic to unpleasantness; the relief-seeking route runs through separate desires.

## 8. Barlassina & Hayward (2019) — Loopy Regulations: The Motivational Profile of Affective Phenomenology [tier: full]

**Citation:** Barlassina, L. & Hayward, M. K. (2019). Loopy regulations: The motivational profile of affective phenomenology. *Philosophical Topics* 47(2): 233–261. DOI 10.5840/philtopics201947224
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Barlassina and Hayward 2019 - Loopy regulations - The motivational profile of affective phenomenology.pdf`. Published typeset version, journal pagination. The printer's stamp is dated 9/30/20, so the Fall 2019 issue was printed in 2020, and the paper cites Barlassina 2020 as "under review".
**Relation to Part A.** This is the second 2019 Barlassina & Hayward paper. It is a companion to the *Mind* paper "More of Me! Less of Me!" reviewed in Part A, and is Carruthers (2023)'s "[2019a]". The attribution check is at the end of Phase 2.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Pleasant and unpleasant experiences show a **loop**. Abe finds a brownie tasty, keeps eating, and gets more pleasure. Zoe's sprained ankle feels bad, she takes a painkiller, and the bad feeling goes. The authors ask three questions:
1. What makes an experience feel pleasant or unpleasant?
2. Why does that feeling motivate?
3. Why do the actions it motivates regulate the feeling itself?

They restrict themselves to theories that explain feelings through their *content* (intentionalism). Desire theories cannot say *why* we want a pleasant experience, and brain states or raw qualia cannot make actions intelligible.

**Key findings.**
1. **Normative questions are set aside.** They reject Bain's (2019) normative condition as a constraint on philosophy of mind. Whether unpleasantness *is* bad is a question for ethics; the question here is why it *feels* bad and moves us.
2. **Two ways to divide theories.**
   - Evaluativism vs imperativism.
   - World-directed (about the body or world) vs experience-directed: Klein's "higher-order" imperativism, and their own **reflexive imperativism**, on which an unpleasant experience commands "**Less of me!**" and a pleasant one "**More of me!**".
3. **Against world-directed theories** (world-directed evaluativism and imperativism):
   - *Not necessary.* Objectless misery feels bad yet evaluates or commands nothing about the world.
   - *Not sufficient.* Some hunger commands "get food" but is not unpleasant. A novice and an expert doctor both see an injury as bad, but only the novice finds the sight unpleasant.
   - *Thinking Otherwise Problem* (evaluativism only). After a habanero you *know* there is no mouth damage, yet the burning still drives you to gulp milk.
   - *"Hedo-motivational inversions"* from rat studies (Berridge and colleagues): liking and seeking come apart.
     - Rats with amygdala lesions show pleased taste reactions to salt yet refuse salt.
     - Rats given lateral-hypothalamus stimulation eat more while showing displeasure.
   - *Relief-seeking cannot be explained.* A command to fix the ankle cannot motivate a painkiller, and objectless moods still make you want them to end.
   - Martínez's "spam" account is called "utterly unbelievable" (p. 251). Bain's background-desire and "encounter" account is said to leave intentionalism.
4. **Reflexive imperativism answers all three questions.**
   - Unpleasantness *is* the self-directed command "Less of me!". Pure misery is that command with no other content.
   - The motivation to regulate the experience is built in. Motivation toward objects is derived: a decision system works out which action will satisfy "less of this".
   - When the decision system finds no action (misery) or is overridden (lesioned rats), feeling and object-directed motivation come apart.

**Initial takeaway.** This paper turned the painkiller question into an argument that valence is *about the experience itself*. It explicitly uses animal liking/wanting dissociations as evidence in a philosophy-of-mind debate. For a historian it marks the point where the philosophical dispute started to lean on affective neuroscience about liking versus wanting.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### The map of intentionalist theories (§2)

| | World-directed (first-order) | Experience-directed |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive representation | Tye 1995b: pain represents damage (judged not to explain *affect*; Aydede 2006) | — |
| Evaluative representation | Bain 2013, 2019; Carruthers 2018 (e.g. "backache … evaluates such a condition as bad", p. 239) | no evaluativist has taken this route (footnote 2 treats Bain's "dislike" theories as experience-directed *desire* views) |
| Imperative content | Martínez 2011: "Get less of this bodily damage!" (p. 239) | Klein 2015: *higher-order* "Get more/less of H!", with H a distinct experience. Barlassina & Hayward 2019: *reflexive* "Get more/less of E!" (pp. 240–241) |

The choice of targets (p. 242): nobody still holds descriptive representationalism about affect ("even Tye is now a world-directed evaluativist"). Higher-order imperativism was argued against in the *Mind* paper.

#### Argument reconstruction — against world-directed intentionalism (§3)

**Extensional adequacy** (the theory's content must be both necessary and sufficient for affect):
- *Necessity fails* (§3.1.1, pp. 243–244). Some world-undirected moods (Mendelovici 2013), e.g. waking up miserable, have affect but no world-directed content. Footnote 3: the claim is only that *some* moods lack such content.
- *Sufficiency fails* (§3.1.2, p. 244).
  - Against world-directed imperativism: all hunger plausibly commands "Get some food!", but not all hunger is unpleasant.
  - Against world-directed evaluativism: "Dr. Expert" and "Dr. Novice" share the content "That injury is bad", but only Dr. Novice's experience is unpleasant.

**Motivation** (§3.2):
- The authors state their own Humean doubt that *any* representational content motivates by itself, but grant the point to evaluativists "at least in this article" (p. 245).
- *Thinking Otherwise Problem* (pp. 245–246). Bain (2013: 84) appeals to cognitive impenetrability: the sensation keeps representing badness, as a stick in water keeps looking bent. The authors reply that the problem lies downstream: the decision-making system is isotropic (Fodor 1983). It should discount an evaluation known to be false, yet the unpleasant sensation still drives milk-drinking.
- World-directed imperativism is immune (§3.2.2). "Change the state of my mouth!" does not conflict with the belief "no damage".
- *Hedo-motivational inversions* (§3.2.3, pp. 247–248). If affect *is* world-directed evaluative or imperative content, and that content is intrinsically appetitive or aversive, affect and object-directed motivation should not come apart. The authors cite cases where they do (next subsection).

**The loop** (§3.3). World-directed theories model the loop as (a) a stimulus causes affect → (b) the affect intrinsically motivates more or less of the *object* → (c) action → (d) affect changes as a side effect. This leaves out experience-directed motivation, as when Zoe takes a painkiller "because she wanted to get rid of her unpleasant pain" (p. 249), or when one wants misery to end.
- *Against Martínez 2015* (§3.3.1): a dilemma. If spam means *unfulfillable*, a freshly sprained ankle is not spammy, yet Zoe wants the pain gone. If spam means merely *insistent*, the theory predicts we should want *less* of an orgasm (p. 251).
- *Against Bain 2019* (§3.3.2). Bain explains relief-seeking through background desires for things that are bad for us. The authors grant the desires but say:
  - the "intuitive argument" that unpleasant feelings are bad appeals to how they feel, not to content, and so is not intentionalist;
  - tokening an evaluative belief ("jumping off a cliff would be terribly bad") is not bad;
  - the "encounter" idea cannot distinguish belief from experience for an intentionalist, since "the belief *This is bad for me* and the experience *This is bad for me* have exactly the same content" (p. 252).

**Stock-take (pp. 252–253).** (i) Not extensionally adequate; (ii) the Thinking Otherwise Problem; (iii) hedo-motivational inversions; (iv) no experience-directed motivation.

#### The empirical cases the paper relies on (as reported by the authors, not re-analysed here)

| Case | Manipulation | "Liking" measure | "Wanting" / object-directed measure | Inversion type claimed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Central amygdala lesion rats (Flynn et al. 1991; Galaverna et al. 1993) | bilateral damage to the central nucleus of the amygdala | taste-reactivity facial expressions indicate pleasure to salt | reject solutions above 0.2% NaCl; normal water and food intake | pleasant experience + aversive motivation (p. 248) |
| Lateral hypothalamus stimulation (Berridge & Valenstein 1991) | electrical stimulation of the lateral hypothalamus | food not found more pleasant; "even though they find it unpleasant" | greatly increased feeding | unpleasant experience + appetitive motivation (p. 248) |
| Crisps anecdote (one author) | none | eating experience "disgusting" | keeps munching | unpleasant + appetitive (p. 248) |

Background: taste reactivity as a cross-species measure of hedonic impact (Berridge 2000); liking vs wanting (Berridge 1996). Footnote 5 notes that weaker dissociations, where changing pleasantness does not change motivational strength, are also common (Berridge et al. 2009).

#### Reflexive imperativism's answers (§4)

**(1) Grounds** (p. 253). For an experience `P` that feels pleasant and an experience `U` that feels unpleasant:

$$
P \text{ is pleasant} \iff P \text{ has content } \textit{More of } P!\qquad U \text{ is unpleasant} \iff U \text{ has content } \textit{Less of } U!
$$

Stated as extensionally adequate, though not proven by exhaustion. Their treatment of the problem cases:
- Neutral hunger has "Get some food!" without the reflexive layer. Unpleasant hunger adds a reflexive command, printed as "Less of this command!" (p. 254).
- Dr. Expert's experience "has lost this layer of content—probably due to desensitization" (p. 255).
- Pure misery is a state "whose only content is *Less of me!*" (p. 255).

**(2) Motivation** (§4.2).
- The experience-directed motivation is intrinsic. Object-directed motivation is instrumental, computed by the decision-making system as the means to the reflexive goal: Abe eats the brownie to get more of the experience, and "if … smart and self-controlled, he will savor" it (p. 256).
- *Habanero:* "Less of this sensation!" is unaffected by beliefs about the mouth.
- *Misery:* the decision system tries strategies, fails, and "gives up", so there is no object-directed motivation.
- *Lesioned rats:* the lesion "directly control[s] the rat's world-directed motivations", producing a prepotent "Don't eat salt!" regardless of the reflexive "More of this experience!" (p. 258).
- The authors say it "doesn't matter much if our models get the details right"; the point is that the view allows "a considerable degree of independence between affective phenomenology and world-directed motivation" (p. 258).

**(3) The loop** (§4.3, p. 259). The general structure:
- (A) S has a pleasant or unpleasant experience E;
- (B) E's affect motivates S for or against E itself;
- (C) S's decision-making computes the best non-mental action to satisfy that motivation;
- (D) if one is found, S acts and gets more or less of E.

"It is thus the very nature of affective phenomenology that makes its regulatory profile loopy." The conclusion inverts the world-directed picture: object-directed motivations "are ultimately in the service of the experience-directed motivations that caused them" (p. 260).

**A scope distinction that surveys blur.** "Imperativism" names at least three different targets in this batch:
- *pain per se*, commanding body protection (Klein 2015; Bain's review);
- *unpleasantness*, commanding removal of bodily damage (Martínez 2011, 2015);
- *unpleasantness*, commanding less of the experience itself (Barlassina & Hayward 2019).

Klein's view of unpleasantness is reported in two different ways by Bain in the same year: an evaluation of the pain (chapter footnote 5, citing book p. 185) versus a self-addressed command (review, citing pp. 186–7). Barlassina & Hayward call it "higher-order imperativism". The relevant chapter (14) is not in the preview PDF.

**Two positions on relief-seeking, stated neutrally.**
- *Martínez 2015:* it is extrinsic, from general avoidance of spammy requests.
- *Bain 2019:* the unpleasant experience itself motivates body-directed action, while relief-seeking comes from separate anti-unpleasantness desires.
- *Barlassina & Hayward 2019:* relief-seeking is the intrinsic motivation of unpleasantness, and body- or object-directed action is derived from it.
