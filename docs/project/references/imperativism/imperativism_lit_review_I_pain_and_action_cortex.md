# Imperativism field history — Batch I: Where pain meets action in cortex — Reference Review

**Topic folder:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/`
**Batch:** I of the multi-file field-history review (Part A: [[imperativism_lit_review_A_imperative_theories]]).
**Corpus:** 10 papers (human neuroscience, 2011–2023), reviewed one at a time in the order assigned, 2026-09-17. Two further items are listed by name only (no PDF held).
**Reviewer:** literature-reviewer

## What this batch is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question.** When something hurts, you want to pull away. Where in the brain does the hurt meet the pulling-away? The ten brain-science papers here (2011–2023) looked for that meeting point. Most of them studied a strip of cortex on the inner surface of each brain hemisphere called the **cingulate cortex**. They focused on its middle part, the **midcingulate cortex**, which contains small motor areas that can send commands toward the spinal cord.

**Why it matters for the field's history.** Philosophers asked whether pain's badness is a *command* to act (Part A of this review). Neuroscientists, largely independently, asked whether the brain's "pain" responses are really about sensation, about feeling bad, or about getting ready to act. This batch documents that second strand.

**What the papers claim, in order.**
- **A 2011 review** argued that bad feelings, pain and effortful self-control all activate the same midcingulate patch. It proposed that the patch uses information about punishment to choose actions when the right move is uncertain.
- **A Swedish group ran three studies.**
  - **2013:** the patch responded during pain only when the person also pressed a button, while another region (the insula) responded to pain either way.
  - **2020:** the same was true in people born with fewer pain-nerve fibres, who also reported a weaker urge to pull their hand away.
  - **2022:** the patch responded more to pain that was *coming* than to pain happening now. Its dependence on whether a button press could shorten that pain rests partly on exploratory tests.
- **Four papers came at the question from other angles.**
  - Movement and pain overlap in the same patch within single individuals (2015).
  - The patch's motor maps also handle reward feedback (2016).
  - Surgical lesions there impair recognising fear and anger in faces and performance on a colour-word control task, but pain was not tested (2016).
  - Watching other people's pain engages the region (2015) and makes an instructed button press more forceful (2017).
- **A 2023 paper** redrew the motor-cortex body map around a whole-body "action network". It links that network to pain only through citations.

**A gap the synthesis should note.** No paper in this batch measured how *unpleasant* a person's own pain felt alongside what they did about it.

## Reading conventions

- **Tiers.** `[tier: full]` entries have Phase 1 (plain overview), Phase 2 (design, sample, manipulation, measures, statistics as reported, and the inferential step from result to claim), and a section-by-section backbone appendix. `[tier: short]` entries are a structured summary read from the PDF. Every entry ends with a machine-readable **Field-history record**.
- **Page citations** use journal page numbers printed on the PDF, with these exceptions:
  - *Budell et al. 2015* (PLoS ONE) is cited by article page "N / 20".
  - *Koppel et al. 2022* is the Advance Access version, cited by its printed pages 1–17.
- **Statistics** are copied as reported. Where a PDF's reported value is internally inconsistent, the entry says so rather than correcting it.
- **No invented mathematics.** None of these papers is equation-driven. Two quantities that a paper describes only in words are rendered as display equations, each marked "reviewer's rendering of the paper's prose": the lesion regression in §6 and the d′ sensitivity score in §8.
- **One brain region, many names.** Across these papers the same stretch of medial frontal cortex is labelled differently. A rough concordance, as used by the papers themselves:

  | Label used | Papers | What it refers to |
  |---|---|---|
  | aMCC (anterior midcingulate cortex) | Shackman 2011; Misra & Coombes 2015; Procyk 2016; Tolomeo 2016 | Vogt's four-region model: the part of cingulate cortex above the corpus callosum, anterior to the anterior-commissure plane (Shackman p.155; Procyk p.467). |
  | "dorsal ACC" (dACC) | Bush et al. 2000, as described by Shackman; Procyk; Gordon | Mostly equivalent to MCC (Procyk p.467). |
  | mid-ACC / MCC | Perini 2013, 2020; Koppel 2022; Han 2017 | Same territory. |
  | "supracallosal ACC" / "cingulate motor area" | Budell 2015 | Same territory. |
  | RCZ (rostral cingulate zone; RCZa, RCZp) and CCZ (caudal cingulate zone) | Picard & Strick 1996, used by Shackman, Perini 2013, Procyk, Koppel | Human cingulate motor zones, proposed homologues of macaque rostral, ventral and dorsal cingulate motor areas (CMAr, CMAv, CMAd) (Procyk p.469). |
  | CON (cingulo-opercular network) | Gordon 2023 | A large-scale action-control network that includes dACC, anterior insula and SMA. |

- **Vocabulary.** "Pain" is used for the human phenomenon these papers study. For this project's artificial agents, the internal damage signal is **nociception**, never "pain".

## 1. Shackman et al. (2011) — The integration of negative affect, pain and cognitive control in the cingulate cortex [tier: short]

**Citation:** Alexander J. Shackman, Tim V. Salomons, Heleen A. Slagter, Andrew S. Fox, Jameel J. Winter & Richard J. Davidson, "The integration of negative affect, pain and cognitive control in the cingulate cortex," *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 12: 154–167, 2011. DOI 10.1038/nrn2994.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Shackman et al. 2011 - The integration of negative affect pain and cognitive control in the cingulate cortex.pdf` — published version.

**Claim.** The dorsal cingulate is not split into an "emotional" front and a "cognitive" back: negative affect, pain and cognitive control all consistently activate one region, the anterior midcingulate cortex (aMCC). The authors propose the **adaptive control hypothesis** — "aMCC uses information about punishment to control aversively motivated actions" (section heading, p.160).

**Method / type.** Review plus a new coordinate-based meta-analysis (activation likelihood estimation, ALE) of BrainMap studies: 380 foci from 192 experiments, "nearly 3,000 participants", covering negative-affect inductions, delivered pain (heat, cold, shock) and control tasks (Stroop, Go/No-Go, flanker) (p.155). Also draws on monkey tract-tracing, ERP work and computational control / reinforcement-learning models (Box 2).

**Key result or argument.**
- The only three-way overlap was a dorsal aMCC cluster (Talairach 0, 12, 42) near the rostral cingulate zone premotor area (p.157–158). Pain studies were *more* likely to activate the "cognitive" MCC than the "affective" ACC (odds 4.9); negative-affect studies were equally likely to activate either (odds 1.1, P = 0.64) (p.156).
- In monkeys this zone receives spinothalamic nociceptive input and projects to the spinal cord, motor cortices and the facial nucleus (p.157–159).
- Hypothesis: the shared function is "the need to determine an optimal course of action in the face of uncertainty" (p.161). Cited support includes MCC neurons that fire for anticipated pain and for instrumental escape from pain (p.161).
- Stated caveats: "a number of indirect inferences" (p.162); "not a new theory" but a synthesis (p.164); monitor versus controller unresolved (Box 2, p.160); the region may code salience rather than negative valence (Box 3, p.161).

**Place in the field's history.** Directly challenges the segregation model of Devinsky, Morrell & Vogt (1995) and Bush, Luu & Posner (2000), and became the reference point for the rest of this batch: Perini 2013 and Koppel 2022 frame MCC findings as "adaptive control", Misra & Coombes 2015 compare coordinates with it, and Tolomeo 2016 used its binary maps to define lesion overlap.

**Relevance.** It frames pain's unpleasant side and its link to action as sharing action-control machinery, which bears on evaluation-versus-action-guidance debates. The evidence is co-activation plus anatomy; the paper makes no claim about the content of subjective unpleasantness.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2011 · human-neuroscience
- Question it asked: Are negative affect, pain and cognitive control segregated into different cingulate subdivisions, or integrated in one?
- Position / finding in one line: All three consistently activate an overlapping aMCC region, proposed to use punishment information to bias aversively motivated action under uncertainty (adaptive control hypothesis).
- Responds to / builds on: Devinsky, Morrell & Vogt 1995 and Bush, Luu & Posner 2000 (segregation model, rejected); Vogt & Sikes 2009 (cingulate premotor pain model); Picard & Strick 1996 (cingulate motor zones); Yeung, Botvinick & Cohen 2004 and Botvinick 2007 (conflict monitoring); Rainville et al. 1997; Mobbs et al. 2010
- Measures (empirical only): intensity rating no · unpleasantness rating no · avoidance/escape behaviour no · desire/urge rating no · neural coordinate-based (ALE) meta-analysis of published fMRI/PET foci
- Dissociation reported (empirical only): none (meta-analytic co-localisation; partial specialisation noted — only negative affect consistently activated subgenual ACC; negative affect and pain, but not control, activated pregenual ACC and posterior MCC, p.163)
- Survey claim check: yes — "adaptive control hypothesis (aMCC integrates pain, negative affect and control for action)" → the paper proposes that "aMCC implements adaptive control by integrating information about punishment … in order to bias responding in situations where the optimal course of action is uncertain" (p.161); it is framed as a working hypothesis built on "indirect inferences" (p.162).
- PDF version: published

## 2. Perini, Bergstrand & Morrison (2013) — Where pain meets action in the human brain [tier: full]

**Citation:** Irene Perini, Simon Bergstrand & India Morrison, "Where Pain Meets Action in the Human Brain," *The Journal of Neuroscience* 33(40): 15930–15939, 2013. DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.3135-12.2013.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Perini et al. 2013 - Where pain meets action in the human brain.pdf` — published version.

### 2.1 Phase 1 — Foundational overview

#### Introduction

Pain studies in brain scanners (fMRI) ask people to lie still. Even so, pain reliably activates motor-related brain areas, including small motor areas inside the midcingulate cortex (the cingulate motor zones). The authors asked whether some of this "pain" activity is actually the brain preparing or holding back a movement. To find out, they gave people painful and non-painful heat and cold on one hand. On half the blocks, people pressed a button with the other hand when the stimulus was painful; on the other half, when it was not painful. Movement was therefore spread evenly across pain and no-pain trials, so its contribution could be separated.

The introduction states the motivating idea in explicitly motivational language. Pain "calls the shots" (*dolor dictat*), and subjectively the action component "is probably experienced as an imperative desire to escape or avoid the source of pain" (p.15930).

#### Key Findings

1. **Action, not pain, drove the midcingulate response.** A midcingulate cluster (probably the caudal cingulate zone, CCZ), motor cortex, thalamus and cerebellum responded when people pressed the button, whether or not the stimulus was painful. These regions did not respond to pain when no press was made (abstract, p.15930).
2. **The insula responded to pain regardless of action.** Bilateral anterior insula was the main region for the pain-versus-no-pain comparison, whether or not a press was made (p.15933).
3. **Pain sped responses.** Presses were faster during painful stimulation. Trial-by-trial CCZ activity correlated with faster presses, but the authors note this relation "was not specific for pain" (p.15937).
4. **Urge and intensity rose together.** In a separate group, continuous ratings of the "urge to move" the hand away rose faster for painful than non-painful stimuli, and faster for heat pain than cold pain. Intensity ratings behaved the same way, and the two ratings correlated in every condition (p.15932).
5. **A meta-analysis converged on the same area.** Across 305 published studies, pain, hand-action execution and hand-action preparation converged in bilateral MCC, SMA, thalamus, striatum and mid-insula. The fMRI cluster coincided with the left MCC peak (p.15936).

#### Initial Takeaway

Part of what earlier studies called midcingulate "pain" activation can be accounted for by making or withholding a voluntary movement. The authors do not conclude that the cingulate is irrelevant to pain. They argue that "generating and regulating voluntary action impulses are partly constitutive of cortical pain processing" (p.15937).

### 2.2 Phase 2 — Graduate-level deep dive

#### 2.2.1 Design, sample and manipulation

- **Scanner sample.** N = 18 right-handed healthy adults (9 male, 21–33 years); 3 T Philips Achieva (p.15930–15931).
- **Stimuli.**
  - A 30 × 30 mm thermode on the back of the **left** hand. Heat stimuli lasted 4 s; cold stimuli 6 s.
  - The experimenter applied the thermode by hand, already at target temperature. Painful stimuli were 2 °C beyond each person's pain threshold; non-painful stimuli used discrimination thresholds.
  - Mean pain threshold was 46 °C for heat (range 42–50) and 1 °C for cold (range −8 to 5) (p.15931–15932).
- **Task.**
  - A dot cue appeared 1 s before stimulus offset and stayed for 2 s. People pressed with the **right** hand.
  - In 2 of 4 runs they pressed only for painful stimuli; in the other 2, only for non-painful ones.
  - This gives a 2 × 2 × 2 within-subject design: noxiousness × temperature × press/no-press. There were 24 stimuli per run, with inter-stimulus intervals of 10–50 s (p.15931).
  - Stimulating and responding with different hands was meant to separate stimulus-related from response-related activation.
- **Behavioural sample.** A separate group, n = 15 (8 female), received the same stimulation outside the scanner (p.15931).

#### 2.2.2 Measures

- **Reaction time and accuracy.** Accuracy was at least 78%. Responses faster than 200 ms or slower than 800 ms were excluded (p.15932).
- **Continuous ratings (behavioural group only).**
  - Two separate trials: "urge to move" the hand away (visual analogue scale from 1 "no urge" to 10 "strong urge"), and stimulus intensity.
  - Each trace was fitted with a linear regression over 1–4 s after onset. Slopes were compared within subjects (p.15931).
  - There was no unpleasantness rating.
- **fMRI.**
  - Eight predictors (one per condition) modelled the 1-s window starting at cue onset, at the end of stimulation.
  - Whole-brain random effects used t > 3.22 (p < 0.005), cluster-corrected to family-wise α = 0.05 by Monte Carlo simulation (p.15931). The Figure 4 caption states p < 0.001.
  - Follow-up analyses: ROI ANOVAs, trial-wise ROI–RT correlations, and a split of participants by single versus double cingulate sulcus.
- **ALE meta-analysis (BrainMap).**
  - Cutaneous pain: 62 papers, N = 814.
  - Hand action execution: 234 papers, N = 4,019.
  - Hand action preparation: 9 papers, N = 92.
  - Maps were thresholded at FDR q < 0.05 and combined in a three-way conjunction (p.15931). The text reports "305 studies and 4,929 subjects"; the three subtotals sum to 4,925.

#### 2.2.4 The inferential step, and where it is thin

The argument runs in three steps.
1. **Observation.** MCC/CCZ activation appears with pressing, whatever the stimulus, and does not appear for pain without pressing.
2. **Inference.** Much of the MCC's usual "pain" response is therefore accounted for by an action factor. In the authors' words, "pain processing in several key areas, particularly the CCZ, is accounted for by a voluntary action factor, whereas their action-related responses are independent of pain" (p.15937).
3. **Interpretive extension (not tested).** Action impulses are "partly constitutive" of cortical pain processing. "part of what is perceived during acute pain may be a motivated urge related to potential courses of action for escape or avoidance" (p.15937).

Points a historian should record:
- **The urge link is indirect.** The urge ratings come from a different group, collected outside the scanner, and are not modelled against BOLD. Urge and intensity slopes correlated in all four conditions (r = 0.53–0.83), so these data do not separate a motivational urge from perceived intensity.
- **No unpleasantness measure was taken.**
- **The RT link is modest and not pain-specific** (r = −0.27; p.15937).
- **The null for pain in MCC is only a non-significant result.** There is no equivalence test. The modelled window is the last second of stimulation, around the cue.
- **The text is internally inconsistent** about which regions showed an action effect without a pain effect. Results list CCZ, thalamus, left striatum and cerebellum (p.15935). The Discussion says "the MCC cluster was the only region showing a main effect of action without a main effect of pain" (p.15936).
- **The meta-analytic three-way conjunction shows co-localisation, not shared function.**
- **No equations appear in the paper.** The rating analysis is a per-trace linear regression slope.

### 2.3 Appendix: Section-by-Section Backbone

1. **Abstract (p.15930).** Pain's influence on behaviour implies an action component. A motor task was crossed with painful and non-painful stimulation. MCC (CCZ), motor cortex, thalamus and cerebellum responded during action regardless of pain but not to pain without action. RTs were fastest during pain and correlated with CCZ. An ALE meta-analysis (reported N = 4,929) converged. Insula responded to pain regardless of action.
2. **Introduction (p.15930).** *Dolor dictat*. Acute pain modulates autonomic and skeletomotor output. In the scanner, people suppress movement, so pain responses may partly reflect motor processing (Farrell et al. 2005). MCC is consistently activated by pain (Shackman et al. 2011; Duerden & Albanese 2013) and contains cingulate motor areas with spinal projections and spinothalamic input (Dum & Strick 1992; Dum et al. 2009). Two alternatives are set up: pain and motor responses processed separately (Kwan et al. 2000), or action recruitment as part of the normal pain response.
3. **Materials and Methods (p.15930–15931).**
   - Participants and 3 T acquisition.
   - Thermal thresholding (discrimination and pain thresholds, heat and cold).
   - Design: 24 stimuli per block; press to painful or to non-painful stimuli by run; cue 1 s before offset; stimulation left hand, response right hand.
   - Urge-to-move and intensity ratings in a separate n = 15, with slopes over 1–4 s.
   - fMRI analysis in BrainVoyager QX: 8 predictors, 1-s window, t > 3.22, cluster correction.
   - ALE meta-analyses: pain, hand execution, hand preparation; FDR 0.05; three-way conjunction in SPM5.
4. **Results — Behaviour (p.15932).** Pain thresholds; RT ANOVA (faster for pain; noxiousness × temperature interaction; non-painful cold faster than non-painful heat); urge-to-move and intensity slopes (pain > non-pain, heat > cold; correlated).
5. **Results — fMRI main effects (p.15932–15933).** Pain: bilateral anterior insula and frontal operculum. Non-pain: somatosensory and parietal cortex. Action: MCC (putative CCZ), SMA, sensorimotor cortex, thalamus, striatum, cerebellum. Heat > cold: somatosensory and cingulate. Table 1.
6. **Results — Motor-related responses during pain (p.15933–15934).** Pain-press and non-pain-press contrasts and their conjunction (MCC, M1, thalamus, cerebellum). Table 2.
7. **Results — Correlations with behaviour in ROIs (p.15934).** Only the CCZ conjunction ROI correlated with RT. No correlation in pain-main-effect ROIs. Functional dissociation of CCZ versus anterior insula (Figure 3).
8. **Results — BOLD in pain-press ROIs (p.15935).** 2 × 2 × 2 ANOVAs: no interactions. Task effect without pain effect in CCZ, thalamus, left striatum and cerebellum. Temperature effects in thalamus, striatum and CCZ voxels (heat > cold).
9. **Results — Cingulate sulcal anatomy (p.15935–15936).** Single- versus double-sulcus split. Peak within the sulcus, consistent with the primate dorsal cingulate motor area (Picard & Strick 1996).
10. **Results — Meta-analysis (p.15936).** Conjunction clusters in bilateral MCC, SMA, left dorsal precentral gyrus, thalamus, caudate/putamen and mid-insula. The fMRI cluster coincides with left MCC.
11. **Discussion — Activations accounted for by voluntary action (p.15936).** The action regressors absorb motor variance during pain. These regions belong to the classic "pain neuromatrix". Cerebellar lobule differences between press conditions.
12. **Discussion — Cingulate motor zones and "adaptive control" (p.15936–15937).** Medial premotor fields and withdrawal and escape evidence (Piché et al. 2010; monkey escape studies). Cluster probably in CCZ. RT relation not pain-specific. A "motivated urge" for escape or avoidance may be part of what is perceived. Heat is more urgent than cold, and CCZ was higher for heat even without a press.
13. **Discussion — Activation regardless of voluntary action (p.15937).** Anterior insula responded to pain regardless of action and did not track RT. Posterior insula is uniquely linked to pain experience (stimulation and epilepsy evidence). Primary somatosensory cortex is inconsistent for pain.
14. **Conclusion (p.15937).** CCZ pain responses are accounted for by action. Action is nonetheless "integral to the experience of pain". Anterior insula is not tightly linked to voluntary action.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2013 · human-neuroscience
- Question it asked: Do medial premotor and cingulate regions activated by pain reflect processing of voluntary action rather than of pain as such?
- Position / finding in one line: MCC (putative caudal cingulate zone), motor cortex, thalamus and cerebellum responded to button presses regardless of pain and not to pain without a press, whereas anterior insula responded to pain regardless of action.
- Responds to / builds on: Shackman et al. 2011; Vogt & Sikes 2009; Vogt & Morecraft 2009; Kwan et al. 2000 (alternative that motor responses to pain are processed separately); Dum & Strick 1992; Dum, Levinthal & Strick 2009; Picard & Strick 1996; Farrell, Laird & Egan 2005; Morrison et al. 2007a,b; Piché, Arsenault & Rainville 2010

- Dissociation reported (empirical only): Under a press/no-press manipulation crossed with pain, MCC/CCZ BOLD moved with pressing but not with pain, while anterior insula BOLD moved with pain but not with pressing; RT correlated with CCZ but not insula activity. Urge-to-move and intensity ratings did not dissociate (both steeper for pain and for heat than cold; correlated r = 0.53–0.83).
- Survey claim check: partly — "a mid-cingulate/premotor region whose activity to pain scales with action-related processing" → the paper shows action-*dependence*, not graded scaling: these regions "did not respond to pain unless an action was performed" (p.15930), and CCZ's relation to faster responses "was not specific for pain" (p.15937).
- PDF version: published

## 3. Misra & Coombes (2015) — Neuroimaging evidence of motor control and pain processing in the human midcingulate cortex [tier: short]

**Citation:** Gaurav Misra & Stephen A. Coombes, "Neuroimaging Evidence of Motor Control and Pain Processing in the Human Midcingulate Cortex," *Cerebral Cortex* 25(7): 1906–1919, 2015 (Advance Access 23 January 2014; cited as "2014" by Koppel et al. 2022). DOI 10.1093/cercor/bhu001.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Misra and Coombes 2015 - Neuroimaging evidence of motor control and pain processing in the human midcingulate cortex.pdf` — published version.

**Claim.** The same aMCC region (with SMA and pre-SMA) is engaged by a precise grip-force task and by painful heat within the same individuals, and the medial wall is more active when both occur together — presented as the first "direct evidence in humans" of this overlap (p.1906).

**Method / type.** fMRI, N = 15 right-handed adults (8 female, 19–56 years). Four blocked 15-s conditions on the right hand: force only (visually guided grip at 15% of maximum), pain only (contact heat rated 5–7 of 10), force + warm, force + pain. After each block, a 0–10 rating from "no sensation" to "intolerable pain" (p.1907–1908). Group and single-subject conjunctions, split by paracingulate-sulcus anatomy.

**Key result or argument.**
- Pain did not change force output (amplitude F(2,28) = 3.284, P = 0.052), and adding force did not change pain ratings (t(14) = −1.608, P = 0.130) (p.1909).
- Force-only and pain-only overlapped in aMCC (MNI 0, 9, 48), SMA, pre-SMA, anterior insula and putamen (p.1910–1911). Force + pain exceeded every other condition only on the medial wall; insula and putamen did not increase (p.1910–1912).
- MCC overlap in 14 of 15 individuals, unaffected by sulcal pattern (p.1912–1913).
- Positioned against Kwan et al.'s (2000) segregation finding, which the authors attribute to method differences (p.1915). They concede the coupling effect may reflect dual-task attention: the study "was not designed to delineate cognitive or attentional processes from stimulus intensity processes" (p.1915).

**Place in the field's history.** First same-subject test of the overlap predicted by Dum, Levinthal & Strick's (2009) tracing and meta-analysis; supports Shackman 2011. Cited by Procyk 2016 (p.474) and Koppel 2022.

**Relevance.** Individual-level co-localisation of motor output and pain in aMCC. Nothing here addresses the motivational or affective side of pain: the motor task was unrelated to escape, and the single rating scale mixed sensation and pain intensity.

- Position / finding in one line: Yes — overlapping aMCC, SMA and pre-SMA activity at group level and in 14 of 15 individuals, with further medial-wall increases when force and pain co-occur.
- Responds to / builds on: Dum, Levinthal & Strick 2009; Kwan et al. 2000 (segregation, contested); Shackman et al. 2011; Amiez & Petrides 2014; Bush, Luu & Posner 2000; Vogt 2005; Büchel et al. 2002

## 4. Budell et al. (2015) — Mirroring pain in the brain: emotional expression versus motor imitation [tier: short]

**Citation:** Lesley Budell, Miriam Kunz, Philip L. Jackson & Pierre Rainville, "Mirroring Pain in the Brain: Emotional Expression versus Motor Imitation," *PLoS ONE* 10(2): e0107526, 2015. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0107526.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Budell et al. 2015 - Mirroring pain in the brain - Emotional expression versus motor imitation.pdf` — published open-access version.

**Claim.** Watching and then reproducing facial expressions of pain produces "mirror-like" responses (active both when observing and when executing) in supracallosal ACC/SMA, inferior frontal gyrus (IFG)/anterior insula and inferior parietal lobule (IPL). Expressing the pain's *meaning* relies more on IFG and superior temporal sulcus / middle temporal gyrus; copying the *movements* relies more on IPL. Conclusion: "while motor mirroring may contribute … interpreting these expressions in terms of pain content draws more heavily on networks involved in the perception of affective meaning" (p.1).

**Method / type.** fMRI, N = 23 (13 female, 18–33). 96 one-second clips of actors' pain faces at four intensities, each shown twice: *pain task* ("use your own face to express the amount of pain that you see") and *movement task* ("imitate the facial movements"), with execution delayed 3–5 s (p.3). Participants' faces were FACS-coded; the pain expressed in each clip was rated after scanning (p.4–5). A priori regions corrected at p < 0.05; figures shown at p < 0.001 uncorrected (p.6, 10).

**Key result or argument.**
- Produced facial pain rose with depicted pain equally in both tasks (F(3,63) = 120.02); imitation was more accurate in the movement task (76.1% vs 69.6%) (p.6, 8).
- Observation–execution overlap in the pain task included "supracallosal ACC (cingulate motor area)" (p.8). The pain-vs-neutral overlap included ACC/SMA in the pain task but not in the movement task (p.10).
- ACC and anterior insula responded to observed pain "only when subjects were attending to the meaning of the expressions" (p.14). The ACC/SMA response "may reflect motor readiness or priming" for withdrawal or expression (p.14–15, citing Perini et al. 2013). Effects on the observers' later actions were not tested (p.16).

**Place in the field's history.** Empathy-for-pain and pain-communication strand; follow-up of Budell, Jackson & Rainville (2010), not reviewed here. Brings the cingulate motor-area action account into social perception.

**Relevance.** No participant received painful stimulation — the pain was observed. The study concerns decoding pain's meaning, not the aversiveness of one's own pain.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2015 · human-neuroscience
- Question it asked: Do brain responses to others' facial expressions of pain reflect motor mirroring of the movements or processing of the expression's affective meaning?
- Position / finding in one line: Observation–execution overlap exists in ACC/SMA, IFG/anterior insula and IPL, but meaning-focused processing recruits IFG and STS/MTG more while movement-focused processing recruits IPL more.
- Responds to / builds on: Budell, Jackson & Rainville 2010; Iacoboni 2005 (fronto-parietal "core circuit" of imitation); Shackman et al. 2011; Dum, Levinthal & Strick 2009; Morrison et al. 2007a,b; Perini, Bergstrand & Morrison 2013; Kunz et al. 2011

- Dissociation reported (empirical only): The task instruction (express pain meaning vs imitate movement) shifted responses between IFG/STS and IPL, while the intensity of produced facial pain displays (FACS) did not differ between tasks; pain-versus-neutral responses in ACC and anterior insula appeared in the meaning task but not the movement task.
- Survey claim check: partly — "facial pain expressions drive motor mirroring in MCC" → the paper finds observation–execution overlap in what it calls "supracallosal ACC (cingulate motor area)" (p.8), but pain-related responses there appeared in the pain-meaning task rather than the movement-imitation task (p.10, 14), and its conclusion favours affective-meaning networks over motor mirroring (p.1).

## 6. Tolomeo et al. (2016) — A causal role for the anterior mid-cingulate cortex in negative affect and cognitive control [tier: full]

**Citation:** Serenella Tolomeo, David Christmas, Ines Jentzsch, Blair Johnston, Reiner Sprengelmeyer, Keith Matthews & J. Douglas Steele, "A causal role for the anterior mid-cingulate cortex in negative affect and cognitive control," *Brain* 139: 1844–1854, 2016. DOI 10.1093/brain/aww069.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Tolomeo et al. 2016 - A causal role for the anterior mid-cingulate cortex in negative affect and cognitive control.pdf` — published version.

### 6.1 Phase 1 — Foundational overview

#### Introduction

Brain scans show that the anterior midcingulate cortex (aMCC) is active during bad feelings, pain and effortful control (Shackman et al. 2011). Scans cannot show that a region is *needed*; damage to the region can. Natural damage confined to aMCC is very rare because of how its blood supply is arranged.

The authors therefore studied people who had undergone **anterior cingulotomy**. This operation makes small lesions in the white matter just beneath aMCC in both hemispheres, and is used in Dundee as a last-resort treatment for severe, treatment-resistant depression. The question was whether patients whose lesions overlapped more with Shackman et al.'s published aMCC maps performed worse on two tests:
- recognising negative emotions (fear, disgust, anger, sadness) in photographs of faces;
- a Stroop colour-word task, the standard test of overriding a habitual response.

#### Key Findings

1. **Negative emotion recognition.** More overlap between the lesion and the "negative affect" map predicted more errors recognising **fear, disgust and anger**. It did not predict errors for sadness, happiness or surprise.
2. **Stroop control.** More overlap with the "cognitive control" map predicted more **Stroop errors**.
3. **Total lesion size predicted nothing.** Only overlap with the grey-matter-derived maps did.
4. **The two maps were not interchangeable.** Overlap with the control map did not predict emotion errors, and overlap with the negative-affect map did not predict Stroop errors.
5. **The deficits were subtle.** They "were only detectable on neuropsychological testing and not clinically" (p.1852).

#### Initial Takeaway

The paper offers lesion evidence that aMCC contributes to recognising some negative emotions and to cognitive control. **Pain was not measured**, and none of the participants had a pain syndrome (p.1846). The title's "causal role" refers to negative affect (operationalised as facial-emotion recognition) and to cognitive control.

### 6.2 Phase 2 — Graduate-level deep dive

#### 6.2.1 Design and sample

- **Setting.** The Advanced Interventions Service, Dundee, a UK-wide tertiary referral centre for treatment-resistant depression (p.1846).
- **Two studies, 94 unique participants in total** (abstract, p.1844; p.1846). Five patients took part in both studies, giving 15 cingulotomy patients overall.

  | Group | Study | N | Notes |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Surgical patient group 1 (SPG1) | 1: facial emotion recognition | 12 | assessed before surgery and at long-term follow-up |
  | Control group 1 (CG1) | 1 | 38 | no history of mood disorder |
  | Surgical patient group 2 (SPG2) | 2: Stroop | 8 | cingulotomy only (no other neurosurgery) |
  | Non-surgical patient group 2 (NSPG2) | 2 | 20 | treatment-resistant depression, no surgery; matched on medication exposure |
  | Control group 2 (CG2) | 2 | 21 | recruited from friends and relatives |

- **Surgery.** A single bilateral lesion at least once. About half the remaining patients had two bilateral lesions, and one had three (p.1846).
- **Clinical measures.** Hamilton (HAM-17) and Montgomery–Åsberg (MADRS) depression scales, HAD anxiety subscale, SF-36 quality of life, estimated IQ (NART), and treatment-resistance staging (MGH-S) (p.1846).
- **Why Study 2 exists.** Stroop data were gathered in a separate cross-sectional study because "staff had stopped collecting Stroop data" in the longitudinal Study 1 (p.1846).

#### 6.2.2 Manipulation (natural) and measures

- **Lesion quantification (p.1848).**
  - T2-weighted MRI was acquired at least one year after surgery and segmented and normalised in SPM8.
  - Lesions were outlined blind to outcome in MRIcron.
  - Overlap volume was computed with Shackman et al.'s (2011) binary maps for negative affect and for cognitive control, obtained from NeuroVault.
- **Study 1 task.** The Ekman 60 Faces test: 10 faces × 6 basic emotions, scored as errors per emotion (p.1846–1847).
- **Study 2 tasks.** Classic colour-word Stroop, and an Emotional Face Stroop (e.g. a sad face above the word "happy"). The Stroop effect was defined as incongruent minus congruent errors (p.1848).
- **Four hypotheses (p.1845–1846).**
  - H1: negative-affect overlap predicts negative-emotion errors.
  - H2: cognitive-control overlap predicts Stroop errors.
  - H3: total lesion volume behaves like the grey-matter overlap.
  - H4: the two masks are interchangeable.

#### 6.2.3 The regression model

The paper specifies the model in prose (p.1848). In the reviewer's rendering of that prose, for patient *i*:

$$\text{Overlap}_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{Errors}_i + \beta_2\,\text{Severity}_i + \beta_3\,\text{Procedures}_i + \varepsilon_i$$

- *Overlap* is lesion volume inside the relevant mask.
- *Errors* is recognition errors for one emotion (Study 1) or Stroop-effect errors (Study 2).
- *Severity* is MADRS or HAM-17 at testing, entered in separate models.
- *Procedures* is the number of cingulotomies.

Two features matter for interpretation:
- **Direction of the model.** Lesion overlap is the *outcome* and test errors are a *predictor*, the reverse of the direction the causal claim runs.
- **Few degrees of freedom.** With 12 (Study 1) or 8 (Study 2) patients and three predictors, very few remain.

No correction for multiple comparisons was applied to the a priori tests. The authors justify this as one hypothesis per region of interest; Bonferroni correction was used for other tests (p.1848).

#### 6.2.5 The inferential step, and where it is thin

- **The chain.**
  - *Observation:* lesion-overlap volume covaries with specific test errors, after entering illness severity and number of operations.
  - *Inference:* aMCC "has a causal role in these processes" (p.1852).
  - *Extension:* since aMCC is associated with "the subjective experience of negative affect and pain", lesioning it "may be therapeutic" for intractable mood, anxiety and pain syndromes (p.1853).
- **What supports the inference.**
  - Surgical lesions are an intervention rather than a correlate of activity.
  - The effect is specific to the mask-defined overlap, not to total lesion volume.
- **What limits it.**
  - The evidence is a between-patient association within very small samples, with uncorrected per-emotion tests.
  - Illness severity itself correlated with errors (p.1849–1851).
  - Patients already made more disgust errors than controls *before* surgery (p.1849).
  - "Negative affect" is operationalised as recognising others' facial expressions, not as one's own experienced affect.
  - Pain, avoidance and unpleasantness were not measured, so the pain clause of the conclusion goes beyond the data.
- **Internal reporting inconsistencies in the PDF.**
  - The Discussion states that "only the overlap between ACING lesions and the negative affect map was associated with impairments in recognizing facial expressions of negative affect and Stroop effect errors, not total ACING lesion volume or when using the cognitive control mask" (p.1852). This contradicts the Results, where the cognitive-control mask predicted Stroop errors and the negative-affect mask did not (p.1851).
  - Several "r" values in the specificity tests exceed 1 (r = 1.6, 2.2, 3.6, 4.2; p.1851), so they are probably test statistics mislabelled as correlations.
  - "t = 1.6, P = 0.004" (p.1849) is also not internally consistent.
- **A reviewer observation for the synthesis.** H4 was *not* supported: the negative-affect and cognitive-control masks predicted different deficits. The authors read this as confirming the specificity of the masks. It also amounts to a partial dissociation within aMCC between emotion recognition and Stroop control, which fits uneasily with a strict single-function reading of the overlap in Shackman et al. 2011. Shackman et al. themselves anticipated finer-grained segregation (p.162).

### 6.3 Appendix: Section-by-Section Backbone

1. **Abstract (p.1844).** 94 volunteers (15 cingulotomy, 20 non-surgical treatment-resistant depression, 59 controls). Ekman 60 Faces and two Stroop tasks. Larger lesion overlap predicted fear, disgust and anger recognition errors (not sadness, happiness, surprise) and Stroop errors, but only with Shackman's masks. Closing speculation on therapeutic lesions in a region linked to negative affect and pain.
2. **Introduction (p.1845–1846).** aMCC is linked to cognitive control, negative affect and pain (Peyron et al. 2000; Vogt et al. 2003; Shackman et al. 2011); Vogt 2014 links it to punishment and unpleasantness. Natural lesions are rare, hence cingulotomy. Cingulotomy was first used for pain (Foltz & White 1962) and procedures vary between centres. Prior neuropsychology is small or single-case (Cohen et al. 1999; Ridout et al. 2007; Steele et al. 2008). Shackman's masks are adopted, and four hypotheses stated.
3. **Materials and methods — diagnosis, recruitment, surgery (p.1846).** Structured interview and rating scales; UK consent safeguards; groups SPG1/CG1 (Study 1) and SPG2/NSPG2/CG2 (Study 2); inclusion and exclusion criteria; no comorbid pain syndromes.
4. **Study 1 task (p.1846–1847).** Ekman 60 Faces (FEEST); negative emotions defined as fear, sadness, disgust, anger.
5. **Study 2 tasks (p.1847–1848).** Classic Stroop; Emotional Face Stroop with explicit meaning conflict; Stroop effect in errors.
6. **Neuroimaging (p.1848).** T2 at least one year post-operation; segmentation and normalisation; blind manual lesion outlining; overlap with the binary masks.
7. **Analyses (p.1848).** Study 1 between-group t-tests (pre- and post-surgery); H1 regression. Study 2 group × task ANOVA with covariates; H2 regression. H3 with total lesion volume; H4 cross-mask. Correction policy.
8. **Results — participants (p.1849).** Groups matched on sex, age and IQ; very high treatment resistance (MGH-S 15.9–17.3); SF-36 impairment; responder and non-responder quality-of-life scores.
9. **Results — Study 1 (p.1849–1850).** Severity decreased at follow-up. Between groups: disgust (pre), fear (post). Within patients: correlations with severity and number of procedures. H1 supported for fear, disgust and anger.
10. **Results — Study 2 (p.1850–1851).** Group differences in severity. Stroop effect by group. Errors correlated with severity within surgical patients. H2 supported.
11. **Results — specificity (p.1851).** H3: total lesion volume unrelated. H4: cross-mask relations absent.
12. **Discussion (p.1851–1853).**
    - Findings restated; sadness spared, consistent with Vogt et al. 2003 linking sadness to subgenual cortex.
    - aMCC–amygdala and aMCC–PAG links to fear.
    - Stimulation of anterior aMCC evokes foreboding (Parvizi et al. 2013); stimulation of posterior aMCC evokes involuntary stereotyped behaviours (Talairach et al. 1973).
    - Support for the adaptive control hypothesis; aMCC neurons encode conflict; the error-related negativity (ERN) and frontal-midline theta.
    - "Causal role" claim; deficits subclinical; possible trade-off between mood benefit and control cost.
13. **Limitations and summary (p.1853).** Small N, centre differences, medication exposure. Summary of H1 and H2. Speculation about lesions in a region associated with negative affect and pain being therapeutic.

## 7. Han et al. (2017) — Empathy for pain motivates actions without altruistic effects [tier: short]

**Citation:** Xiaochun Han, Kang He, Bing Wu, Zhenhao Shi, Yi Liu, Siyang Luo, Kunlin Wei, Xinhuai Wu & Shihui Han, "Empathy for pain motivates actions without altruistic effects: evidence of motor dynamics and brain activity," *Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience* 12(6): 893–901, 2017. DOI 10.1093/scan/nsx016.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Han et al. 2017 - Empathy for pain motivates actions without altruistic effects.pdf` — published open-access version.

**Claim.** Seeing others in pain makes observers act more vigorously even when the action helps no one, and acting reduces the brain's response to the observed pain. The authors read this as support for the "egoistic" account of empathy (Hoffman 1981; Piliavin et al. 1982) over a purely altruistic one (Batson et al. 1987, 1988) (p.893–894, 899).

**Method / type.** 24 three-second clips of faces receiving a needle (pain expression) or a cotton swab (neutral). *Experiment 1* (N = 30): press a force sensor through each clip "for no reason"; rate the model's pain, own unpleasantness and willingness to help (1–11) (p.894). *Experiment 2* (fMRI, N = 33): passive-viewing runs vs runs pressing a button on every clip; ROIs from an earlier study with the same clips (MCC/SMA, anterior insula/IFG, SII) plus whole-brain tests (p.895–896).

**Key result or argument.**
- Painful vs non-painful clips: pain 8.80 vs 2.04, unpleasantness 8.57 vs 2.58, willingness to help 7.71 vs 2.26 (p.895).
- Maximum force (0.740 vs 0.696 lbs; t(29) = 3.400, d = 0.62) and plateau force (d = 0.61) were higher for painful clips; ramp-up velocity was not. Within participants, unpleasantness predicted plateau force (slopes > 0, t(29) = 4.36) (p.895).
- Pressing reduced pain-vs-non-pain ROI responses in MCC/SMA (t(32) = 2.26, d = 0.39), left anterior insula/IFG and bilateral SII (p.896). In the direct whole-brain contrast only SII survived family-wise correction; SMA and insula/IFG passed only a lenient threshold; MCC is not listed (p.896; Table 2, p.898).
- Author caveats: raised arousal (footnote, p.899) and attention shifted to one's own hand (p.899–900).

**Place in the field's history.** Carries "pain calls for action" from first-hand pain into empathy research, citing Rainville et al. 1997 and Perini et al. 2013 (p.894, 899). The only paper in this batch relating an *unpleasantness* rating to motor vigour — but the unpleasantness is an observer's.

**Relevance.** Observers' unpleasantness and the vigour of an instructed movement covary; what changed was **how hard** people pressed, not **whether** they chose to act or avoid.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2017 · human-neuroscience
- Question it asked: Does seeing others in pain motivate one's own actions even when they cannot help, and do such actions reduce brain responses to the observed pain?
- Position / finding in one line: Viewing others' painful (vs non-painful) stimulation increased the force of an instructed button press, in step with self-rated unpleasantness, and pressing reduced MCC/SMA, anterior insula/IFG and SII responses to the observed pain.
- Responds to / builds on: Batson et al. 1987, 1988 (empathy–altruism hypothesis); Hoffman 1981 and Piliavin et al. 1982 (egoistic hypothesis); Singer et al. 2004; Lamm et al. 2011; Luo et al. 2014; Rainville et al. 1997; Perini, Bergstrand & Morrison 2013; Morrison et al. 2007; Preston & de Waal 2002

## 8. Perini et al. (2020) — Mutation carriers with reduced C-afferent density reveal cortical dynamics of pain–action relationship [tier: full]

**Citation:** Irene Perini, Marta Ceko, Leonardo Cerliani, Helene van Ettinger-Veenstra, Jan Minde & India Morrison, "Mutation Carriers with Reduced C-Afferent Density Reveal Cortical Dynamics of Pain–Action Relationship during Acute Pain," *Cerebral Cortex* 30(9): 4858–4870, 2020. DOI 10.1093/cercor/bhaa078.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Perini et al. 2020 - Mutation carriers with reduced C-afferent density reveal cortical dynamics of pain-action relationship.pdf` — published version of record (open access) with an institutional-repository cover page as PDF page 1. Journal pagination is printed and used below.

### 8.1 Phase 1 — Foundational overview

#### Introduction

A small group of families in northern Sweden carries one copy of a rare mutation (R221W) in the gene for nerve growth factor. Carriers are born with fewer **C-fibres**, the thin, slow nerve fibres that carry much of the skin's pain signal. They can still tell painful from non-painful stimuli, but they tend to under-react to pain.

This makes them a natural experiment. The pain signal entering the brain is weakened, while the ability to recognise it as pain is kept. The authors used the 2013 button-press design with one change: on some trials pain was the "signal" to press, on others it was "noise" to ignore. They then compared 12 carriers with 12 matched controls.

#### Key Findings

1. **Normal thresholds, worse task, weaker urge.** Carriers' pain thresholds matched controls'. Carriers made more errors deciding when to press, and their urge to pull the hand away from painful heat rose less steeply.
2. **Insula tracked task accuracy.** In carriers, the better someone separated signal from noise, the more their right anterior insula responded during pain.
3. **Midcingulate followed the action requirement.** In controls, the midcingulate cortex responded when a press was required, even for non-painful warmth, replicating 2013.
4. **Insula–cingulate coupling tracked the urge in controls only.** Coupling between insula and midcingulate cortex during pain rose with each control's urge to withdraw. Carriers showed no such relation.
5. **Carriers leaned on primary sensorimotor cortex.** Carriers showed more activity there during pain and action, which the authors interpret as compensation.

#### Initial Takeaway

The authors argue that the carriers' apparent "pain indifference" reflects a weaker translation of the pain signal into appropriate action and motivational urgency, not a failure to perceive pain (p.4866). On their account, an insula–midcingulate pathway links noticing a relevant stimulus to selecting an action and to the felt urge to escape it.

### 8.2 Phase 2 — Graduate-level deep dive

#### 8.2.1 Design, sample and manipulation

- **Participants.** 12 heterozygous R221W carriers (7 female, mean age 36.2 ± 15.4) and 12 controls individually matched on sex, age and education (p.4859).
  - About 50 adult carriers are known, mostly in far-northern Sweden; carriers were identified by pedigree and confirmed by DNA sequencing (p.4859–4860).
  - Earlier data from 3 homozygous carriers enter only one correlation.
- **Natural manipulation.** Reduced peripheral C-nerve-fibre density (C-NFD). In an earlier sample it was measured by corneal confocal microscopy. Heterozygotes' C-NFD range overlaps the healthy range at its upper end (p.4859, 4863).
- **Experimental manipulation.** A 2 × 2 design, adapted from Perini et al. 2013 (p.4861):
  - *Stimulus:* noxious heat at the temperature rated 8 on a 0–10 scale, or innocuous warmth; 4-s plateau on the back of the left hand; 48 trials in 2 runs.
  - *Response:* press or no press, with the right hand.
  - *Instruction:* in half the trials press only if the stimulus is painful ("pain signal"); in the other half press only if it is non-painful ("pain noise"). The cue appeared 1 s before offset.

#### 8.2.2 Measures

- **Signal-detection scoring.** Hits, misses, false alarms and correct rejections, giving task sensitivity (d′) and criterion (p.4861). The paper defines d′ in words as "subtracting the Z-transformed false alarms from the Z-transformed hits, divided by the group's SD". Reviewer's rendering of that prose:

$$d'_i = \frac{z(\text{hit rate}_i) - z(\text{false-alarm rate}_i)}{\mathrm{SD}_{\text{group}}}$$

  The division by a group SD departs from the textbook definition, and the paper does not say which SD is meant. The criterion formula is not given.
- **Urge to move.** Measured after scanning, on 6 hot and 6 warm stimuli: continuous rating of the urge to move the hand away (1 "no urge" to 10 "high urge"), summarised as a regression slope (p.4861).
- **Task fMRI.**
  - 3 T GE scanner, FSL FEAT.
  - The **higher-level model was fixed-effects** (random-effects variance forced to zero), justified by individual matching and expected carrier covariance (p.4861). Threshold Z > 3, cluster p = 0.01.
  - Analyses: conjunction as in 2013; within-carrier regression of d′ on BOLD, restricted to atlas-defined insula; psychophysiological interaction (PPI) connectivity from the resulting insula seed, with d′ or urge slope as covariates (p.4861–4862).
- **Other imaging.** Resting-state connectivity (CONN; group ICA in GIFT) and cortical thickness in insula (FreeSurfer) (p.4863).
- **Not measured.** No pain-intensity or unpleasantness ratings were collected during the task.

#### 8.2.4 The inferential step, and where it is thin

- **Claim 1: under-reaction is an action problem, not a perception problem.** Thresholds are normal, while task accuracy and urge are reduced. The authors infer that any "pain indifference" reflects "an individually variable impairment in translating nociceptive afferent signaling into situation-appropriate behavioral responses, rather than … an impairment in the ability to perceive and report nociceptive stimulation" (p.4866).
  - The perception side rests on thresholds and a calibration temperature only, with no intensity or unpleasantness ratings during the task.
  - The group difference in sensitivity (d′) is a trend (P = 0.06).
  - Carriers also erred more on non-painful signal trials, which the authors attribute to general signal-to-noise uncertainty (p.4866).
- **Claim 2: anterior insula separates relevant from irrelevant stimulation.** The anterior insula "lifted 'signal' from 'noise'" (p.4867). This rests on a regression within 12 carriers; controls were at ceiling.
- **Claim 3: the insula–midcingulate axis carries motivated action.** The two regions jointly produce "motivated, subjectively 'hot' representations", with insula signalling relevance and midcingulate cortex "selecting and prioritizing voluntary actions" (p.4867).
  - The urge covariate was measured outside the scanner, after the session, as a per-person slope.
  - The evidence is a group-by-covariate PPI under a fixed-effects model, which limits generalisation beyond these 24 people.
- **Claim 4: midcingulate cortex is premotor, not pain-specific.** "its predominantly premotor role was not specific for pain". The authors note in the same passage that MCC shows "pain-specific, yet generalizable" patterns in Kragel et al. 2018 (p.4867).
- **Speculation flagged by the authors.** Carriers might "evaluate pain on a linear intensity scale" rather than against "a motivation- or behavior-centered threshold" (p.4867). A transgenic mouse model of the mutation showed a selective deficit in pain-related fear conditioning (Testa et al. 2019; p.4867).
- **Internal inconsistency.** Results say the significant C-NFD–d′ correlation required pooling the 7 heterozygotes with 3 homozygotes (p.4864). The Discussion describes it as "a subset of seven R221W carriers (including three homozygotes)" (p.4866).

## 9. Koppel et al. (2022) — Prediction and action in cortical pain processing [tier: full]

**Citation:** Lina Koppel, Giovanni Novembre, Robin Kämpe, Mattias Savallampi & India Morrison, "Prediction and action in cortical pain processing," *Cerebral Cortex*, 2022, Advance Access, 1–17. DOI 10.1093/cercor/bhac102.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Koppel et al. 2022 - Prediction and action in cortical pain processing.pdf` — published open-access Advance Access version (CC BY), paginated 1–17 rather than final issue pages. Page citations below use those printed pages.

### 9.1 Phase 1 — Foundational overview

#### Introduction

Knowing that something painful is about to happen lets you act to avoid it. This study separated three things that are normally bundled together in real pain: what hurts now, what is predicted to hurt next, and whether you can do anything about it.

On each trial:
1. A short first stimulus (**S1**), painful or not, signalled whether a second stimulus (**S2**) would be very painful or not.
2. During S1, people had to press a button quickly on *every* trial.
3. On half the trials, a fast enough press (within 450 ms) cut the upcoming S2 from 4 seconds to 1 second. On the other half, the press did nothing.
4. An instruction screen said in advance which kind of trial it was.

The hypotheses were preregistered. The authors expected the insula to track predicted pain, and the midcingulate cortex to track whether action could change the outcome.

The introduction frames the question in action terms: "pain is in large part an action problem rather than a sensation per se" (p.1).

#### Key Findings

1. **Pain now did not register; pain coming next did.** Across the whole brain, there was no difference between painful and non-painful S1. There was a clear difference by what S1 predicted: predicted pain raised activity in insula, ACC, MCC, striatum and thalamus.
2. **The preregistered action tests were null.** No main effect of action appeared in MCC, and no difference between effective and ineffective action for predicted pain.
3. **Exploratory tests suggested action dependence.** When current pain predicted more pain, right ACC and MCC were more active *only* on trials where the press could shorten S2. On ineffective trials, only left insula was.
4. **MCC tracked response speed; insula did not.** People pressed faster when the press mattered (322 vs 340 ms). Trial types with faster responses had more MCC activity; insula activity did not follow response time.
5. **The insula was also modulated by action.** Contrary to the preregistered expectation, insula responses to predicted pain changed with whether action was effective.

#### Initial Takeaway

The authors conclude that cingulate and insular "pain" responses are organised around "consequence-level" terms, meaning what the stimulus implies for behaviour, rather than the sensory stimulus itself (p.15). Two cautions apply. The strongest action-dependence evidence comes from exploratory contrasts. The study collected no ratings of pain or unpleasantness.

### 9.2 Phase 2 — Graduate-level deep dive

#### 9.2.1 Design, sample and manipulation

- **Sample.** 40 recruited from a Linköping student pool. 3 withdrew for scanner discomfort and 7 were excluded for technical problems, leaving **N = 30** (17 male, 24.33 ± 3.25 years). Two participants contributed one run only (p.2–3).
- **Preregistration.** OSF 8p7rq, with hypotheses per contrast (p.2). Deviations are disclosed in-line: head coil, EPI count, AFNI motion-censoring thresholds, and regressors of no interest (p.3–4).
- **Stimuli.** 3 × 3 cm thermode on the left forearm, applied manually at target temperature (p.3).
  - "Painful" (S1) = heat pain threshold, 42–47 °C, mean 44.86.
  - "Very painful" (S2) = pain tolerance limit, 45–49 °C, mean 47.86.
  - Non-painful = 32 °C baseline.
- **Design.** 2 × 2 × 2 within subjects: current S1 (pain / non-pain) × predicted S2 (very painful / non-painful) × action (effective / ineffective). 96 trials in 2 blocks of 48, Latin-square ordered.
- **Trial timeline (Fig. 1, p.3–4).**
  1. Instruction screen, 3 s ("painful"/"nonpainful"; "response button: enabled"/"disabled").
  2. Fixation, 2 s.
  3. S1, 2 s. The cue dot appears 1 s after onset for 450 ms; a press is required on every trial.
  4. Inter-stimulus interval, 4–6 s.
  5. S2: 1 s if action was effective and the press landed within 450 ms, otherwise 4 s.
  6. Inter-trial interval, 3.56–16.86 s.

#### 9.2.2 Measures

- **Behaviour.** Response times. Only responses between 200 and 800 ms were modelled (93.6% of responses); 85.9% of responses fell inside the 450-ms window (p.5).
- **Not collected.** No ratings of pain intensity, unpleasantness or urge in the main study.
- **fMRI acquisition and model.**
  - 3 T Siemens Prisma, 64-channel coil, multiband EPI with TR 901 ms. Analysis in AFNI.
  - The GLM modelled a 1-s window from S1 onset: 8 condition predictors plus regressors of no interest for the cue, the rest of S1, the inter-stimulus interval and S2.
  - Whole-brain 2 × 2 × 2 ANOVA (3dmvm) with voxel p = 0.002 and cluster-level family-wise α = 0.05 (3dClustSim with the ACF smoothness model) (p.4).
- **ROIs (p.5).**
  - *MCC:* 10-mm sphere at the Perini et al. 2013 conjunction peak (MNI −1, 8, 40), grey matter only, 146 voxels.
  - *Left and right insula:* from the S2 main effect of pain.
  - *ACC:* from the S1 main effect of prediction; used only for time-course plots.
  - ROI β-values went into 2 × 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVAs and correlations with response times.
- **Exploratory analyses.** Split t-tests; an S2-window ANOVA; ROI time courses across cue, S1 and S2; unsmoothed group maps (p.7–9).

#### 9.2.4 The inferential step, and where it is thin

- **Claim 1: the prediction outweighs the current stimulus.** No main effect of current pain, alongside a robust effect of predicted pain, is read as "the current stimulation's meaning as a predictive cue had a stronger and more selective influence on pain processing than its meaning as an acute signal" (p.11).
  - The authors acknowledge they cannot rule out that S1 pain, at threshold temperature for 2 s, was simply not strong enough (p.15).
  - They argue against this because non-painful S1 that predicted pain was directionally modulated.
- **Claim 2: cingulate pain prediction depends on action.** This is stated in the abstract ("activation in ACC and MCC depended on whether a meaningful action was performed").
  - The preregistered tests for it were null (p.5–6).
  - The support comes from exploratory split contrasts (Table 2) and a trend-level ROI interaction (P = 0.075).
  - The Discussion is explicit that "no cingulate region was sufficiently action-selective to be activated above a corrected threshold for the whole-brain main effect of meaningful action in S1" (p.9, 11).
- **Claim 3: MCC is tied to motor output, insula is not.** RT correlations appear in the MCC ROI but not in insula (p.7, 13). These are across-participant correlations of condition β-values with RTs, not trial-wise.
- **Claim 4: a rostrocaudal gradient in the cingulate.** From an exploratory look at *unsmoothed* maps: rostral cingulate codes predicted pain regardless of action, caudal cingulate only on effective-action trials (p.9, 12–13). This is proposed as a cingulate control hierarchy, with the predicted-pain cluster placed in RCZa (p.12–13).
- **Other interpretive context the paper supplies.**
  - Intracranial stimulation of human ACC produces reported "feelings of urgency rather than pain sensation" (Bancaud et al. 1976; Hsieh et al. 1994; p.12).
  - The authors reconcile higher insula/ACC activity on controllable trials with reports that *uncontrollable* pain raises these regions (Salomons et al. 2004) and with contrary controllability findings (Limbachia et al. 2021) (p.13–14).
- **What the design cannot show.**
  - The "action" factor is the *consequence* of an action required on every trial, not a choice whether to act.
  - No subjective pain, unpleasantness or urge was rated, so the paper's measures cannot separate the sensory and affective sides of pain.
- **No equations in the paper.**

15. **Discussion — cortical processing of predicted outcomes (p.14–15).** S2 analysis: pain effects without cingulate; action effects in pre-SMA/SMA as feedback monitoring; selectivity emerges in S2; caveat on S1 intensity; medial prefrontal interaction.
16. **Conclusion (p.15).** Processes separated in time; ACC/MCC and AI modulated by predicted rather than current pain and by action possibility; "consequence-level" processing.

- Position / finding in one line: ACC/MCC and insula responded to predicted upcoming pain rather than to current pain; action-dependence of cingulate prediction responses appeared in exploratory contrasts, and MCC (but not insula) activity tracked response speed.

## 10. Gordon et al. (2023) — A somato-cognitive action network alternates with effector regions in motor cortex [tier: short]

**Citation:** Evan M. Gordon, Roselyne J. Chauvin, Andrew N. Van, … Damien A. Fair & Nico U. F. Dosenbach, "A somato-cognitive action network alternates with effector regions in motor cortex," *Nature* 617: 351–359, 2023. DOI 10.1038/s41586-023-05964-2.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Gordon et al. 2023 - A somato-cognitive action network alternates with effector regions in motor cortex.pdf` — published open-access version (34 PDF pages including Methods and Extended Data).

**Claim.** Primary motor cortex is not one continuous foot-to-face map (Penfield's "homunculus"). Foot, hand and mouth zones — each organised in concentric distal-to-proximal rings — **alternate with three "inter-effector" regions**. These connect to each other and to the cingulo-opercular action-control network, have thinner cortex, lack movement specificity, and are more active when planning than when executing movement. The authors name the system the **somato-cognitive action network (SCAN)** within an "integrate–isolate" model (p.351, 356).

**Method / type.** Precision functional mapping in 7 highly sampled adults (172–1,813 min of resting-state and 353 min of task fMRI each, plus diffusion, thickness and myelin measures) (p.352); movement and action-planning task batteries in 2 of them (p.354–355); replication in UK Biobank, ABCD, HCP and WU120 group data; newborn, infant, child and perinatal-stroke cases; macaque fMRI; re-analysis of cortical-stimulation data (p.352, 356).

**Key result or argument.**
- In all 7 individuals, inter-effector regions connected more strongly to the cingulo-opercular network than effector regions did (paired t > 4.75, P < 0.01 FDR), and more strongly to middle insula ("known to process pain"), thalamic CM/VIM nuclei, putamen and cerebellum (p.352).
- Abdominal and eyebrow movements co-activated the inter-effector regions, which were more active in planning than in execution (p.355).
- SCAN is linked to projections toward the adrenal medulla and to allostatic regulation (p.356).
- Pain appears only by citation: the connected network is "critical for action … physiological control, arousal, errors and pain" (p.351, citing Wager et al. 2013), and SCAN integration serves an organism pursuing its "goals through movement while avoiding injury" (p.358). No pain stimulus or rating was used.

**Place in the field's history.** Reframes the Penfield homunculus (1937/1950), building on concentric monkey motor zones (Kwan et al. 1978), ethological action maps (Graziano 2016) and the cingulo-opercular network (Dosenbach et al. 2006). A history anchor for the SCAN label, which the survey used without citation.

**Relevance.** SCAN offers a candidate route from action-control regions (dorsal ACC, insula) to whole-body motor and autonomic output. Any claim that it mediates pain-driven action goes beyond this paper's data.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2023 · human-neuroscience

- Position / finding in one line: Effector-specific foot, hand and mouth zones alternate with three inter-effector regions linked to the cingulo-opercular action-control network and to whole-body action planning — the somato-cognitive action network (SCAN).
- Responds to / builds on: Penfield & Boldrey 1937; Penfield & Rasmussen 1950; Kwan et al. 1978 (concentric motor zones); Graziano 2016 (ethological action maps); Dosenbach et al. 2006 (cingulo-opercular network); Dum, Levinthal & Strick 2016 (adrenal medulla); Gordon et al. 2017; Newbold et al. 2020, 2021; Wager et al. 2013 (cited for pain); Jensen et al. 2022 (preprint)

- Dissociation reported (empirical only): none relevant to pain (effector regions were movement-specific and inter-effector regions were not; inter-effector regions were more active in planning than execution)
- Survey claim check: n.a. — the survey names SCAN but cites no source → this is the defining paper; its link to pain is indirect, through the connected network being described as critical for "action … physiological control, arousal, errors and pain" (p.351, citing Wager et al. 2013); no pain task was run.

## Batch notes for the synthesis

These notes are hand-off facts for the cross-paper synthesis, not a synthesis. Each is grounded in the entries above.

1. **Citation chain inside the batch** (from the papers' own reference lists):
   - Shackman 2011 is cited by Perini 2013, Misra & Coombes 2015, Budell 2015, Procyk 2016, Tolomeo 2016, Perini 2020 and Koppel 2022.
   - Perini 2013 is cited by Budell 2015, Han 2017, Perini 2020 and Koppel 2022.
   - Misra & Coombes is cited by Procyk 2016 and Koppel 2022.
   - Procyk 2016 is cited by Koppel 2022.
   - Gordon 2023 cites none of the other nine.
2. **Two readings of "the midcingulate responds to pain".**
   - *Integration:* one region serves pain, negative affect and control (Shackman 2011); motor control and pain co-localise within individuals (Misra & Coombes 2015).
   - *Action-dependence:* the pain response is contingent on action requirements or consequences and is not pain-specific (Perini 2013, 2020; Koppel 2022). Perini 2020 notes the counter-evidence of pain-specific MCC patterns (Kragel et al. 2018; p.4867).
   - These readings are not mutually exclusive, and no paper here adjudicates between them.
3. **The insula story shifts across the Swedish line.**
   - 2013: pain regardless of action.
   - 2020: tracks behavioural relevance of pain.
   - 2022: modulated by action consequences, contrary to the preregistered hypothesis.
4. **The measures gap.**
   - First-hand unpleasantness was never rated.
   - "Urge to move away" was rated in Perini 2013 and 2020, but outside the scanner. In 2013 it did not dissociate from intensity.
   - Han 2017's unpleasantness rating is an observer's.
   - This matters for any claim in the synthesis about valence *versus* action.
5. **Causal evidence is thin.** The only intervention study (Tolomeo 2016, cingulotomy) did not measure pain. Every other study is correlational fMRI with modest samples (12 per group to 33).
6. **Misattribution risks flagged in the entries.**
   - The survey's "scales with action-related processing" (Perini 2013) overstates a contingency as a gradient.
   - "Motor mirroring in MCC" (Budell 2015) inverts that paper's conclusion.
   - "Causal cingulotomy evidence" (Tolomeo 2016) is not evidence about pain.
   - Procyk 2016 is a reward-feedback paper.
   - Gordon 2023's pain link is by citation only.
