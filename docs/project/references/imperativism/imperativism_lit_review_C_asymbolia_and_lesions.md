**Topic folder:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/`
**Batch:** C of the imperativism field-history corpus. There are 3 papers with PDFs, all philosophy of mind and science, reviewed one at a time in publication order on 2026-09-17. Three clinical source papers are listed but have no PDF.
**Reviewer:** literature-reviewer
**Companion files:** [[imperativism_lit_review_A_imperative_theories]] (Part A: the imperative and evaluative theories of pain and valence: Klein 2007, Carruthers 2018/2023, Barlassina & Hayward 2019, Barlassina 2020).

## What this part is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question.** Is the *hurt* of pain the same thing as the *feeling* of pain? Could someone feel a pinprick as painful and still not mind it at all, with no urge to pull away? If so, pain would have at least two separable parts: a sensation, and an unpleasantness that pushes you to act. Several theories of pain depend on whether those two parts can come apart. That includes the "imperative" theory in Part A, on which pain *is* a bodily command.

**The evidence everyone argues over.** A rare brain-lesion condition called **pain asymbolia** was first described by Schilder and Stengel in 1928. It usually follows damage around the insular cortex. Patients who are pricked, pinched or burned may say "oh, that hurts" while smiling. They do not withdraw, and they may offer another body part for testing. In 2007 the philosopher Nikola Grahek made it the leading example of "pain without painfulness".

**What the three papers claim.**
- **Klein (2015)** says asymbolics have not lost part of their pain. They have lost the general capacity to *care about their body*: they also ignore threats, oncoming lorries and verbal menaces. Pain is still a command, but the patient no longer treats it as binding.
- **Griffith & Kind (2023/2024)** argue that the clinical reports do not show that asymbolics feel *pain* at all. On their reading, asymbolia may be a symptom of several different disorders rather than one syndrome, so philosophers should stop using it as evidence, at least for now.
- **Duval & Klein (2025/2026)** reply that those objections are weak. They argue that the real open question is how to *classify* asymbolia: as a rare, specific deficit, or as the extreme end of common experiences like depersonalization. For all we know, asymbolia "might still be pain".

**Where it stands.** The exchange is unresolved as of 2025. All three papers agree that the clinical record is small, old and ambiguous. The three foundational clinical reports (Foltz & White 1962, Berthier et al. 1988, Ploner et al. 1999) are discussed here only as the reviewed papers describe them; no PDFs were available.

## Reading conventions

- **Page citations are journal pages** in all three papers. All three PDFs are typeset publisher versions with printed pagination.
  - *Klein 2015*: *Mind* 124(494), pp. 493–516.
  - *Griffith & Kind*: *Philosophy of Science* 91(3), pp. 561–578.
  - *Duval & Klein*: *Philosophy of Science* 93(1), pp. 221–229.
- **Dates.** The manifest keys use first-online years. Griffith & Kind was first published online on 4 December 2023, in the 2024 volume. Duval & Klein was first published online on 4 July 2025, in the 2026 volume.
- **No mathematics.** All three are conceptual papers with no equations. Each "Phase 2" is an **argument reconstruction**: numbered premises, the models being compared, and which premise each objection attacks.
- **Vocabulary.**
  - *PA*: pain asymbolia.
  - *Standard Interpretation (SI)*: Griffith & Kind's name for the view that asymbolics feel real pain that lacks an affective or motivational component.
  - *Motivationalism*: the view that motivational force is intrinsic to pain.
  - *Double dissociation*: two patients or conditions impaired in opposite ways, the classic neuropsychological evidence that two functions are separable.
  - *Painfulness / hurt / suffering*: the unpleasantness of pain. Grahek's word is "painfulness"; Griffith & Kind use "suffering" (p. 563, fn. 4).
- **Clinical sources seen second-hand.** The case reports (Schilder & Stengel 1928, 1931; Hemphill & Stengel 1940; Rubins & Friedman 1948; Berthier et al. 1988; Ploner et al. 1999) are cited only as quoted in the three reviewed papers. They were not checked against the originals.

## 1. Klein (2015) — What Pain Asymbolia Really Shows [tier: full]

**Citation:** Colin Klein, "What Pain Asymbolia Really Shows," *Mind* 124(494): 493–516, 2015. DOI 10.1093/mind/fzu185.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Klein 2015 - What pain asymbolia really shows.pdf`. This is the published OUP typeset version (24 PDF pages, with a library download stamp).

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

"Motivationalists" hold that pain's power to move us is part of what pain *is*. Pain asymbolia looks like a clean counterexample. Asymbolics say they feel pain but do not act on it, and no competing motive (duty, masochism) explains why. Grahek (2007) read the condition as pain whose *affective/motivational component* has been knocked out, leaving a "blunt, inert sensation" (quoted p. 497). Klein argues that this reading mischaracterises what the patients have lost.

#### Key Findings

1. **Two models of asymbolia.**
   - The **Degraded Input (DI)** model (Grahek) says the *sensation* has changed: pain has lost its motivational part.
   - The **Lost Capacity (LC)** model (Klein) says the *person* has changed: they "no longer care about the physical integrity of their bodies" (p. 498).
2. **The clinical record fits LC better.** Asymbolics are indifferent to *any* immediate bodily threat, not just to pain. Examples from the case reports:
   - matches struck near the face, loud noises and flashes (Hemphill & Stengel 1940);
   - threats with a hammer or knife (Schilder & Stengel 1928);
   - verbal menaces (five of Berthier et al.'s six patients);
   - a lorry sounding its horn behind a patient who heard it (p. 501).

   Yet one patient reacted strongly to being called a liar and a thief (p. 501–502). So the deficit is specific to threats to the body, not a general loss of emotion.
3. **DI makes pain's sensory side pointless.** If only the affective part were missing, asymbolics should still *know* they are being damaged. They should then protect themselves by ordinary reasoning, as people born insensitive to pain learn to do. They do not (pp. 502–503).
4. **A weaker motivationalism survives.** Klein separates three versions:
   - "ambitious": pain always motivates. Asymbolia refutes it.
   - "lazy": pain motivates normal people. This is trivial.
   - "modest": pain always has the property that motivates, but motivation also depends on the agent.

   Modest motivationalism is compatible with asymbolia under LC (pp. 505–507).
5. **The imperative account fills in the motivating property.** A pain is a bodily command. Commands move us only if we accept the source's authority, and we accept the body's authority because we care about our bodily integrity. Asymbolics "have ceased to treat such bodily commands as binding" (p. 510).
6. **Link to depersonalization.** Asymbolia may be a severe form of depersonalization, in which sensations feel as if they are not one's own. Morphine pain may be a drug-induced version of the same thing (pp. 510–512).

#### Initial Takeaway

Klein moves the question from "what part of pain is missing?" to "what has happened to the patient's relationship with their own body?" On this view asymbolia leaves pain's content intact and does not refute the claim that pain is intrinsically motivating in the modest sense. Two further consequences follow. The view makes a testable prediction: indifference to pain should always come with broader indifference to bodily threat. It also ties the condition to a much more common psychiatric phenomenon, depersonalization.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Technical Analysis

**Dialectical target.** Grahek's *Feeling Pain and Being in Pain* (2007) treats pain as a composite of sensory-discriminative, emotional-cognitive and behavioural components that "can become disconnected" (Grahek p. 2, quoted p. 497). Klein recasts Grahek's argument as a **double dissociation** (fn. 4, p. 497):
- Asymbolia is pain without painfulness.
- Ploner, Freund & Schnitzler (1999) is painfulness without pain. Their patient had an S1/S2 lesion and felt a "clearly unpleasant" sensation he "wanted to avoid" without pain sensation.

Klein then attacks both halves. He is sceptical of the Ploner half (fn. 4, p. 498) for two reasons:
- The patient did report pain in the affected hand, only at a much higher threshold.
- "Unpleasant" need not mean *pain* unpleasantness. The same worry applies to Hardcastle's reading of fentanyl tooth-pulp data.

**Neural framing.** Both sides accept posterior insula damage as the lesion site (p. 499). DI inherits Geschwind's (1965) serial "disconnection syndrome" picture, in which limbic processing fails to attach to sensory processing. Klein argues that this feed-forward picture is outdated: the insula integrates interoceptive and motivational signals with reciprocal connections (Craig 2003; Singer et al. 2009), and that picture inspires LC (p. 500).

**Immediate vs. distant threats.** Schilder & Stengel's patient still asked to eat and to use the bathroom. This looks like an objection to LC. Klein answers that eating and toileting respond to *distant* threats, which habit can handle. Pain signals *immediate* threats, which cannot be met by habit, so a loss of care shows most clearly there (p. 499). In fn. 6 he concedes an alternative: care for immediate and distant threats might dissociate. He reports that he finds nothing in the clinical literature that decides between the two.

**Reflexes (fn. 9, p. 504).** Some asymbolics lack even reflexive withdrawal. Klein explains this with top-down modulation of spinal reflexes: descending control via the periaqueductal grey and brainstem, loss of pain reflexes in catatonia and schizophrenia, and primitive reflexes that reappear after cortical damage. He calls the explanation speculative.

#### Argument Reconstruction

**Argument 1 — Against DI (abductive, §2.3).**

- P1. DI says only the affective/motivational component of pain is missing; the sensory component, which informs about damage, is intact.
- P2. Someone who knows they are being damaged and cares about their body is motivated by practical reasoning, even without a motivating sensation. Klein's two illustrations are an anaesthetised arm being hammered out of sight, and people with congenital insensitivity to pain, who learn to avoid injury (pp. 502–503).
- P3. So DI predicts asymbolics should still protect themselves once they know of a threat.
- P4. They do not. They ignore threats in every modality, including language and a lorry horn (pp. 500–501).
- P5. DI can absorb P4 only through ad hoc additions. One option is many separate modality-specific deficits. The other is a single conduction deficit, which cannot explain why only *threat*-related input is affected (p. 501).
- C1. LC, a single lost capacity to care about bodily integrity, is the better explanation. DI also leaves pain's sensory side "wholly epiphenomenal" (p. 503).

**Argument 2 — Asymbolia does not refute motivationalism in every form (§3).**

- Ambitious: "Necessarily, if an agent feels pain they are motivated by it." Asymbolia refutes it even under LC (p. 506).
- Lazy: "If a typical agent in normal circumstances feels pain, they will be motivated by it." It is compatible with asymbolia, but too weak to be interesting (p. 505).
- Modest: "Pains motivate in virtue of some property p, and pains intrinsically and necessarily have p" (p. 505). Motivation is a *two-place relation* between pain and agent, and it can fail through a change in the agent. Klein's analogy (pp. 506–507) contrasts chlorine trifluoride, which ignites almost anything, with a lit match, which needs oxygen and dry tinder but is still an ignition source.
- LC supplies the background condition that keeps "modest" from collapsing into "lazy": a *unified* capacity to care about the body. This yields a falsifiable prediction: "any agent who is indifferent to felt pain should be as asymbolics actually are: possessed of a collection of deficits" (p. 507).

**Argument 3 — Imperative content is the best candidate for p (§4).**

- Being a representation of bodily damage is not p. Such a representation lacks the right direction of fit; learning of damage by another route need not motivate (p. 508).
- Being an affective state or desire is not p either. Asymbolics recognise pains without the usual affect (p. 508).
- Imperative content qualifies. Authoritative commands give "content-independent reasons for action" (Hart), and they motivate without further deliberation (p. 509).
- Accepting an authority can lapse without any change in the command's content (p. 509). The issuing authority for pain is the body, accepted because we care about bodily integrity.
- So asymbolics still recognise pain's command, but "have ceased to treat such bodily commands as binding" (p. 510). Klein's analogy is a bystander who hears a police officer shout "Stop or I'll shoot!" and recognises it as a command without realising that *he* has a reason to stop (p. 510).

**Revision of Klein (2007).** Footnote 12 (p. 508) says the content is now "commands to protect, rather than negative commands to cease movement". Klein says he once thought negative commands were needed for cases like asymbolia; he now finds this account more satisfying. This is a documented change of position between Part A's Klein (2007) and this paper.

**§5 Depersonalization.** The DSM-IV description of depersonalization includes sensory anaesthesia and lack of affective response. Klein cites several parallels:
- Sierra's (2009) patients: "as if it was somebody else's pain" (p. 511).
- Insula damage producing disownership syndromes such as asomatognosia and somatoparaphrenia (p. 511).
- Schizophrenia, where pain thresholds are reportedly normal, so "indifference" rather than "insensibility" (Guieu et al. 1994; p. 512).
- Morphine as drug-induced depersonalization (p. 512).

The upshot for theories of consciousness is that one kind of unity, felt *ownership* of sensations, can fail (p. 513).

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- The LC argument rests on **associations of deficits** from a handful of 1928–1988 case reports. Klein anticipates Shallice's warning about associations (fn. 11, p. 507) and presents the riskiness as a virtue. Griffith & Kind (entry 2) and Duval & Klein (entry 3) both return to this evidential base.
- The imperative account of *why* asymbolics fail to act is a candidate filling for p. Klein does not claim to have established it (p. 510: "A full defence of imperativism about pain is beyond the scope of this paper").
- The paper does not use or report new data.

### Appendix: Section-by-Section Backbone

**Abstract (p. 493).** Asymbolics feel pain but act indifferent. Grahek takes this as a counterexample to motivationalism. Klein argues asymbolics "have lost a general capacity to care about their bodily integrity", which is about the pain–subject relationship, not pain's nature. The paper also links asymbolia to depersonalization.

**§1 Pain and motivation (pp. 493–496).** The paper defines motivationalism and quotes Schilder & Stengel (1928, pp. 147–148): the patient smiles, says "Oh, pain, that hurts", and offers her hand again. Asymbolia is "arguably the cleanest" counterexample (p. 494). Stoics and masochists have overriding motives. Lobotomised patients still withdraw from pinprick (Melzack & Wall 1982), and morphine reports are unreliable. Striking behaviour: self-stabbing, a severe burn, no defence against surprise pricks (p. 495). The motivationalist "tent" includes attitude/evaluation views and imperative views (Klein 2007, Hall 2008, Martínez 2010). Working assumption: pain's biological function is to preserve bodily integrity (p. 496, fn. 2).

**§2.1 Degraded input model (pp. 496–498).** This is the composite view (Dennett 1978, Hardcastle 1997, Price 2000, Grahek 2007). DI says the asymbolic's sensation lacks its motivational push, so motivationalism is false. It is recast as a double dissociation with Ploner et al. (1999). Footnote 4 raises doubts about the Ploner half.

**§2.2 Lost capacity model (pp. 498–500).** LC says asymbolics cannot care about their bodies in the way pain requires. It predicts widespread deficits for threats however they are learned of. Reply to the eating/toileting worry: the distinction between immediate and distant threats, plus habit. Both sides accept posterior insula damage. DI reflects Geschwind's serial disconnection model, which is now disfavoured. LC explains *why* pains motivate: because we care about our bodies.

**§2.3 Evidence for a lost capacity (pp. 500–505).** Indifference to non-pain threats (matches, noises, hammer, knife, magnesium flare, verbal menaces, lorry). Two readings of Grahek's "plurimodal deficit" fail: multiple deficits (ad hoc) or one conduction deficit (does not explain threat-specificity). Thought experiment of the anaesthetised arm; comparison with congenital insensitivity. DI makes the sensory component epiphenomenal. Footnote 9 covers reflex suppression through descending modulation. Conclusion: no clean dissociation, and LC wins abductively.

**§3 Modest motivationalism (pp. 505–507).** Ambitious, modest and lazy versions defined. The match vs. chlorine trifluoride analogy. LC supplies the background condition, a unified capacity to care, and a falsifiable prediction. Footnote 11 addresses Shallice on associations.

**§4 Motivation and command (pp. 507–510).** Candidates for p: damage representation (rejected), affective state (rejected), imperative content (accepted). The body's authority rests on care. Asymbolics recognise commands but do not treat them as binding (police analogy). Footnote 12 revises the 2007 negative-imperative content to protective commands.

**§5 Asymbolia and depersonalization (pp. 510–513).** The patient experiences detachment from the pain. Supporting links: the insula and bodily self-awareness (Craig 2002; Karnath & Baier 2010); Sierra's depersonalization patients; schizophrenic indifference to pain; morphine. Footnote 15 notes asymbolics' laughter and rationalisations (Ramachandran 1998; Hemphill & Stengel 1940). Unity of consciousness: felt ownership can fail. Footnote 20 notes that Bain's *Australasian Journal of Philosophy* reply (2014) addressed an earlier version of this paper.

## 2. Griffith & Kind (2023) — Pain Asymbolia is Not Pain [tier: full]

**Citation:** Trevor Griffith and Adrian Kind, "Pain Asymbolia is Not Pain," *Philosophy of Science* 91(3): 561–578, 2024 (first published online 4 December 2023). DOI 10.1017/psa.2023.167.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Griffith and Kind 2023 - Pain asymbolia is not pain.pdf`. This is the published Cambridge University Press version, open access under CC-BY (18 PDF pages).

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

Since Grahek (2007), many philosophers have treated pain asymbolia as real pain with something missing: painfulness, the capacity to care, or both. Griffith & Kind call this the **Standard Interpretation (SI)** and list its adopters: Fink 2011, Corns 2014, Bain 2014, de Vignemont 2015, Klein 2015, Geniusas 2017, Bradford 2020 and Gerrans 2020, with Park 2023 as a partial adopter. The use it is put to: if asymbolics have pain minus a part, then ordinary pain must be a *compound* of parts. Griffith & Kind argue that SI is unsupported by the clinical record and incoherent on its own terms.

#### Key Findings

1. **The clinical testimony is inconsistent** (§3).
   - Some patients say it hurts. One says "Well it hurts, but I don't know what that really is. Maybe my heart hurts" in response to a leading question (p. 566).
   - Two of Schilder & Stengel's 1931 patients "could remember that the pain had been inflicted, but that they did not feel it" (p. 566).
   - Two of Rubins & Friedman's patients "stated repeatedly that the pinprick did not hurt even after prolonged application and to the point of drawing blood" (p. 567).
   - Aphasia and word deafness commonly co-occur, which undermines verbal report.

   The authors' conclusion: it is more faithful to say that asymbolics experience "noxious stimuli without painfulness (i.e., suffering)" than "pain without painfulness" (p. 568).
2. **SI commits a mereological fallacy** (§4). SI holds three claims that cannot all be true: "1) pain is complex, 2) PA is not complex, 3) PA is pain" (p. 569). If pain is essentially complex, its isolated parts cannot themselves be pain.
3. **SI collapses into subjectivism** (§5). To count PA as pain, SI must make the bare phenomenal component sufficient for pain. That is subjectivism, the simple view of pain that Grahek's composite view was designed to avoid (pp. 570–571).
4. **Klein's and Bain's alternatives are improvements but under-motivated** (§6).
   - Klein's imperativist reading is "more plausible than the standard interpretation". But asymbolics "do not respond to pain as if they are being commanded", and the imperative content itself might have changed (p. 573).
   - Bain's evaluative model is judged "the most compelling" because it is stated conditionally, but it leaves unclear whether PA pain is still pain (p. 574).
5. **Deflation** (§7). Pain asymbolia may be "not a syndrome at all, but … merely a symptom" that accompanies several distinct neuropsychiatric conditions (p. 575). Only three things are undisputed (p. 575):
   - patients discriminate types of noxious stimuli;
   - they perceive the normal pain threshold;
   - they lack normal avoidance behaviour.

#### Initial Takeaway

The paper is a sceptical audit of the evidence base behind a famous philosophical example. Its message is not that pain has no parts, or that some particular theory of pain is wrong. It is that asymbolia, as currently documented, cannot bear the weight philosophers have put on it, and should be set aside "at least until further evidence becomes available" (abstract, p. 561). The authors call explicitly for more clinical research (p. 577).

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Technical Analysis

**Scope qualifier.** The two conceptual arguments (§§4–5) target SI *combined with essentialism about pain*, meaning that pain has necessary and sufficient conditions. Philosophers with a family-resemblance view (Fink 2011, 2012; Coninx 2020) are challenged only by the evidential argument of §3 (pp. 562–563). This qualifier becomes the pivot of Duval & Klein's reply (entry 3).

**The case for SI as reconstructed (§2).** SI has two empirical premises and one methodological one.
- *Testimony*: patients say it hurts.
- *Thresholds and neuroanatomy*: Berthier et al. (1988) found normal pain thresholds but much higher tolerance, and insular/parietal opercular lesions in all patients. This is backed by monkey electrophysiology on nociceptive neurons in granular insula and area 7b (Robinson & Burton 1980).
- *Methodology*: introspection cannot fix the necessary features of pain (Fink's rule against "phenomenological foot-stomping", p. 565).

Griffith & Kind accept the neurobiology and the methodological rule. They dispute the reading of the testimony.

**Threshold detail (p. 564).** Berthier et al. used electric shock.
- Mean pain threshold was 3.9 mA in 5 controls and 5.1 mA in 3 asymbolics, which the authors note is "over 25% higher".
- Mean pain endurance (tolerance minus threshold) was "over 600% greater" in asymbolics.

The standard reading treats the threshold as not significantly different. Griffith & Kind flag the gap "for the sake of completeness".

#### Argument Reconstruction

**Argument E — The evidential argument (§3).**

- E1. SI needs clinical evidence that asymbolics experience *pain*. Grahek calls it "unequivocal" and "incontestable" (p. 565).
- E2. Verbal report is the main evidence, and it is (a) elicited by leading questions, (b) confused about where the pain is, (c) contradicted by patients who deny feeling pain while bleeding, and (d) compromised by frequent aphasia and word deafness (pp. 566–568).
- E3. "Partial asymbolia" (an "ouch" with no withdrawal) does not help SI. The more pain is felt, the less the patient has PA, which would make PA a disruption of pain perception itself (p. 567).
- C_E. The evidence supports "noxious stimuli without painfulness", not "pain without painfulness". The difference matters philosophically because conflating noxious stimulation with pain experience begs the question (p. 568).

**Argument M — The mereological argument (§4), essentialist framing.**

- M1. SI (with Grahek) holds that pain is essentially complex: sensory, affective and behavioural components (Grahek p. 73, quoted p. 569).
- M2. On SI, PA isolates a simple component.
- M3. SI also holds that PA is pain.
- M4. An essentially complex kind cannot be instantiated by one of its simple parts. Predicating "is pain" of a part is a mereological fallacy (p. 568).
- C_M. The triad M1–M3 is inconsistent: "either asymbolics feel pain, or pain is complex" (p. 569).
- *Reply to "it's merely verbal"*: inferring "pain is complex" from "PA is simple and not pain" is invalid, because the part–whole relation was what the argument set out to establish (pp. 569–570).

**Argument S — Collapse into subjectivism (§5).**

- S1. If PA is pain, it satisfies the necessary and minimally sufficient conditions for pain.
- S2. PA contains only the phenomenal/sensory element.
- S3. So that element is necessary and sufficient for pain, which is subjectivism.
- S4. Subjectivism makes pain simple, contradicting SI's thesis that pain is complex.
- C_S. SI is incoherent. Denying S1 instead cuts PA off from pain, and then PA says nothing about pain (pp. 570–571).

**§6 on the alternatives.**
- *Klein.* Griffith & Kind describe Klein's pain as a homeostatic sensation with imperative content "Keep B from E (with priority P)!", issued by the body, with "hurt/suffering" as a secondary motivation. Pain is thus simple (a "pure-imperative" model) and escapes the mereological charge (p. 572).
  - Objection: the evidence that asymbolics recognise an imperative is at best mixed. They "laugh, they extend their hand, they thank the experimenter". Their pain could be an *altered* imperative ("Subject B to E!") rather than an intact one they no longer obey (p. 573).
  - They add that "imperativism ultimately benefits from adopting a skeptical attitude towards PA" (p. 573).
- *Bain (2014).* Bain accepts Klein's lost-care diagnosis but rejects unitary imperativism: on Klein's view, he argues, asymbolics should still grimace and withdraw. On Bain's evaluative model, unpleasantness requires an evaluative layer that represents bodily damage as bad; asymbolia removes that layer, so the sensations are not unpleasant (pp. 573–574).

**§7 Deflation.** Two sources are used to show the heterogeneity of the cases.
- Schilder & Stengel's (1931) ten patients: six with sensory aphasia; five recovered from asymbolia as their aphasia resolved; two had frontal rather than parieto-temporal lesions (p. 575).
- A muteness analogy: loss of language and loss of the larynx both produce muteness but are different conditions. Likewise Berthier's cases may show an inability to make sense of pain concepts, while Schilder & Stengel's 1928 case may show an altered pain experience (pp. 575–576).

This also blocks the subjectivist from using PA as "purest pain" (p. 576). The authors stress that "we have not taken any position on whether pain in general is simple or complex" (p. 577).

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- **Possible citation conflation.** The paper attributes to "Klein 2015" (the *Mind* paper, its only Klein entry) three things this reviewer could not find in the *Mind* article (entry 1):
  - the content schema "Keep B from E (with priority P)!";
  - the label "pure-imperative";
  - a quotation cited to page "(142)".

## 3. Duval & Klein (2025) — Pain Asymbolia Is Probably Still Pain [tier: full]

**Citation:** Alexandre Duval and Colin Klein, "Pain Asymbolia Is Probably Still Pain" (Discussion Note), *Philosophy of Science* 93(1): 221–229, 2026 (first published online 4 July 2025). DOI 10.1017/psa.2025.10098.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Duval and Klein 2025 - Pain asymbolia is probably still pain.pdf`. This is the published Cambridge University Press version, open access under CC-BY-NC-SA (9 PDF pages).

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

This is a short reply to Griffith & Kind (entry 2). Duval and Klein say they themselves favour a different reading of asymbolia (Klein's lost-care view, entry 1), not the Standard Interpretation. They nonetheless argue that Griffith & Kind's reasons for rejecting SI are weak, and that the whole dispute turns on an unaddressed question: *what kind of clinical category is pain asymbolia?*

#### Key Findings

1. **The mereological argument faces a dilemma** (§2). If its target is SI itself, SI can avoid essentialism. It can treat "pain" as a **homeostatic property cluster (HPC) kind**: a kind whose members typically share properties because of stabilising mechanisms, with no strictly necessary ones. By analogy, a human on dialysis without kidneys is still human. Pain's evolutionary, genetic and developmental mechanisms make that treatment natural (p. 223). If the target is SI *plus* essentialism, the argument succeeds, but it cannot justify rejecting SI or removing PA from philosophy (pp. 223–224). The same move answers the subjectivism argument (fn. 4).
2. **Rarity is not decisive** (§3.1). In neuropsychology a single rigorously shown dissociation can establish a point, while associations of deficits (such as PA with aphasia) are the weaker evidence (p. 225). A second, non-dissociationist route also exists, which looks for commonalities across more frequent cases:
   - morphine pain;
   - depersonalization (up to 70% of people have at least one episode, per Sierra 2009);
   - schizophrenia, autism and Cotard's delusion (p. 225).
3. **Ambiguity can be handled** (§3.2).
   - Comorbidities are expected under both approaches. Lesions are large, and apraxia that worsens for self-preservation actions is what the care-lack view predicts (p. 226).
   - Varied testimony is expected in patients whose experience has changed.
   - Patients who *never* call anything "pain" may simply have been misclassified (p. 227).
   - They also dispute Griffith & Kind's translation "It hurts indeed" (fn. 8, p. 226).
4. **The core issue is taxonomy** (§3.3). The authors identify two clinical traditions going back to the nineteenth century:
   - a German one: asymbolia as a rare, specific deficit in processing pain's meaning, which fits SI;
   - a French one: asymbolia as one symptom in a cluster, the tip of an iceberg of common experiences, which fits the alternatives.

   Griffith & Kind's "symptom, not syndrome" conclusion places them in the French tradition (p. 227).
5. **Verdict.** None of the criticisms undermines SI or the alternative readings on which asymbolics feel pain, so "for all we know, pain asymbolia might still be pain" (p. 228).

#### Initial Takeaway

The latest move in this corpus does not settle whether asymbolics feel pain. It relocates the disagreement. How to read the case reports depends on a prior decision: is asymbolia a clean single dissociation, or the extreme of a spectrum? That decision has not been made. As of 2025 the philosophical use of asymbolia is contested but not abandoned.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Technical Analysis

**What is conceded.** The authors "fully agree" that PA is rare and that the case reports are ambiguous. They also grant Griffith & Kind "many valid philosophical points against Grahek's analysis" (p. 224). They grant that the Ploner et al. (1999) "painfulness without pain" case "has not been terribly convincing (it strikes many as a report of poorly localized pain, rather than of untethered affect)" (p. 224), which echoes Klein 2015 fn. 4. The dispute is over what follows from these concessions.

**Textual point about Griffith & Kind's target (fn. 3, p. 222).** Griffith & Kind announce that their target is SI combined with essentialism (G&K p. 562). Yet on p. 574 they summarise the mereological argument as showing "the interpretation itself is guilty", and "essentialism" does not occur in that passage. Duval & Klein build their dilemma on this shift.

#### Argument Reconstruction

**Argument D — Dilemma against the mereological argument (§2).**

- Horn 1 (target is SI itself):
  - D1. SI can be stated without essentialism by taking "pain" to name an HPC kind (Boyd 1999a,b).
  - D2. For an HPC kind, one can consistently hold both (i) pain typically comprises a sensory-discriminative and an affective-motivational component, and (ii) genuine instances can lack one of them (p. 223).
  - D3. Then "PA is pain" and "pain is (typically) complex" are consistent, and there is no mereological fallacy.
- Horn 2 (target is SI + essentialism):
  - D4. The argument is sound: the conjunction is false.
  - D5. SI can still be true if essentialism is false. So the argument cannot license rejecting SI, reinterpreting PA, or removing PA from the philosophy of pain (pp. 223–224).
- C_D. "G&K's mereological argument is either unsound or it cannot be used for the purposes that G&K have in mind for it" (p. 224).

**Argument R — Rarity does not defeat the case (§3.1).**

- R1. Asymbolia is meant as one half of a double dissociation. This is implicit in Grahek and explicit in Hardcastle 1997, 1999 (fn. 5).
- R2. On strict neuropsychological method, one rigorously shown case can establish a dissociation (Shallice 1988), so rarity alone is not a defeater.
- R3. Associations such as PA with aphasia carry *less* weight, since lesions ignore functional boundaries and co-occurrence can reflect anatomical proximity or shared blood supply (p. 225).
- R4. Independently, a commonality-based approach draws on frequent but messy phenomena, namely morphine pain and depersonalization, where rarity is not a problem (p. 225).
- C_R. Rarity does not justify setting aside the view that asymbolics feel pain.

**Argument A — Ambiguity is manageable (§3.2).**

- A1. Comorbidities are predicted by both the dissociationist and the care-lack approaches (p. 226).
- A2. Testimony: some asymbolics are free of aphasia and call their experiences pains. Variation is expected when experience has changed. Most subjects gave some verbal or non-verbal admission of pain in some context, such as Berthier et al.'s threshold tests (p. 227).
- A3. For the few who never do, reclassification (not PA; cf. Bain 2014) is available, and "mistakes in categorization occur, but they do not undermine the category itself" (p. 227).
- C_A. Ambiguity does not force rejection.

**Argument T — Taxonomy is prior (§3.3).**

- T1. The two interpretive strategies map onto two clinical traditions: German (specific deficit) and French (symptom cluster). Klein & Duval 2023 is cited for this history.
- T2. If the single-dissociation taxonomy were settled, rarity and ambiguity would simply "come with the territory". If the spectrum taxonomy were settled, PA would already be primarily a symptom (pp. 227–228).
- C_T. The difficulty of the literature is "symptomatic of a larger issue about taxonomization", not something peculiar to asymbolia (p. 228).

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- A discussion note of this length leaves some moves only sketched. The authors say so for the subjectivism reply (fn. 4). The HPC-kind proposal is stated, not developed; for example, it does not say which mechanisms would count as the "homeostatic" ones for pain experience rather than for pain behaviour.
- The authors have a stake in one side: they favour the Klein (2015) reading (fn. 2, p. 222). The note defends *any* interpretation on which asymbolics feel pain, not SI specifically.
- There is a typographical slip: Berthier et al. is cited in text as "1998" (pp. 221, 227), while the reference list gives the correct 1988.
- The note, like the two papers it follows, uses no new clinical data.

### Appendix: Section-by-Section Backbone

**Abstract & §1 Introduction (pp. 221–222).** Grahek: asymbolia as the case "in which severe pain is not experienced as unpleasant at all" (Grahek p. 38), perhaps "pure pain". G&K's claim that the literature rests on a shaky foundation. SI defined in G&K's words. The authors favour a distinct interpretation (Klein 2015a, 2015b; Klein & Duval 2023). Plan: answer G&K's two core moves, then taxonomy. Footnote 1 lists similar critiques (Coninx 2020; Park 2023).

**§2 Griffith and Kind's mereological argument (pp. 222–224).** The inconsistent triad quoted. Dilemma over the target. HPC kinds (Boyd; species example; kidneys/dialysis). Pain's evolutionary, genetic and developmental homeostatic mechanisms (Sneddon et al. 2014; Mogil & Max 2006; Baccei & Fitzgerald 2006). Both horns are problematic. Footnote 4 extends this to subjectivism.

**§3 A taxonomic debate (p. 224).** G&K's second move is rarity plus ambiguity. The authors agree with both premises but deny that the conclusion follows.

**§3.1 The rarity of pain asymbolia (pp. 224–225).** Double dissociation logic (Caramazza 1984, 1986; Shallice 1988). The Ploner half is unconvincing. Single cases suffice; associations are weaker. PA without aphasia exists. The non-dissociationist approach via morphine pain and depersonalization (70% lifetime episode estimate), schizophrenia (Bonnot et al. 2009), autism (Hoffman et al. 2023) and Cotard's delusion (Klein & Duval 2023): "one looks not for purity but for commonalities" (p. 225).

**§3.2 The ambiguity of the case reports (pp. 226–227).** Comorbidity ambiguity: predicted by both approaches; footnote 7 says aphasia reflects anatomical proximity. Testimony ambiguity: the Rubins & Friedman denial quote acknowledged. Footnote 8 gives the German original and disputes the "It hurts indeed" translation. Variability is expected; most subjects admitted pain somewhere; misclassification explains the rest.

**§3.3 Coming back to taxonomy (pp. 227–228).** German vs. French clinical traditions (Klein & Duval 2023). G&K's closing view places them in the French tradition (G&K p. 575 quoted). The debate over interpretation cannot be separated from the debate over taxonomy.

**§4 Conclusion (p. 228).** The core question is categorisation, which shapes how reports are used as evidence. G&K's title claim is doubted: "for all we know, pain asymbolia might still be pain."

**Points of agreement across all three papers:**
- The clinical base is small, old, and confounded by aphasia and large lesions.
- The Ploner et al. (1999) "affect without sensation" half of the double dissociation is weak (Klein 2015 fn. 4; Duval & Klein p. 224; Griffith & Kind do not rely on it).
- Asymbolics can discriminate noxious stimuli and do not avoid them.

**Points of live disagreement:**
- Is what asymbolics experience *pain*?
- Is asymbolia a specific deficit or a symptom?
- Does its unpleasantness remain while failing to motivate (Klein as read by Bain), vanish (Bain), or is its imperative content altered (a suggestion from Griffith & Kind)?
