# Imperativism field history, batch E1: Separating How Strong Pain Is from How Bad It Feels in the Human Brain (1997–2014) — Reference Review

**Topic folder:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/`
**Batch:** E1 of the imperativism field-history corpus. Five human studies, each reviewed from its PDF, one at a time and in date order, 2026-09-17.
**Reviewer:** literature-reviewer
**Companion files:** [[imperativism_lit_review_A_imperative_theories]] (part A: the philosophy debate: Klein 2007, Carruthers 2018/2023, Barlassina & Hayward 2019, Barlassina 2020).

## What this batch is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question these studies asked.** Pain has two sides that usually move together: **how strong** it feels (its intensity, where it is, what it is like) and **how bad** it feels (its unpleasantness). Since Melzack and Casey's 1968 model, researchers have suspected that different brain systems handle each side. A burn that grows stronger usually also grows nastier, though, so an ordinary experiment cannot tell which brain area tracks which side.

**What the five papers did.** Each found a way to pull the two sides apart in healthy volunteers and then looked at the brain:

- **Rainville et al. (1997)** used hypnotic suggestion to make hot water feel *more or less unpleasant* while it felt about equally strong. Brain activity in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC, a frontal midline region) followed unpleasantness. Activity in primary somatosensory cortex (S1, the body-map region) did not.
- **Hofbauer et al. (2001)** did the reverse: they suggested the heat felt *stronger or weaker*. S1 changed and the ACC did not. Unpleasantness ratings also changed, however.
- **Zubieta et al. (2001)** measured the brain's own opioid (morphine-like) release during sustained jaw-muscle pain. People with more release in some regions reported less sensory pain; people with more release in other regions reported less affective pain.
- **Kulkarni et al. (2005)** skipped hypnosis. They asked people to attend either to *where* a laser pulse landed or to *how unpleasant* it was. Different brain networks were engaged.
- **Tiemann et al. (2014)** temporarily lowered brain dopamine with a special amino-acid drink. Laser pulses felt more unpleasant but not more intense.

**Why it matters to the field's history.** These are the experiments most often cited for the claim that pain's unpleasantness has its own brain machinery, apart from pain's sensory side. Philosophical theories that treat unpleasantness as a separate component of pain lean on this claim (see part A). Every study here measures *felt unpleasantness*, or a questionnaire score for pain's emotional side. **None of the five measured avoidance, escape or desire.** Several have caveats that matter for how they are cited. Hofbauer's intensity suggestions also moved unpleasantness. Zubieta's evidence is correlational. Tiemann's key effect is p = .048. Details are in each entry and in [Batch-level notes for the synthesis](#batch-level-notes-for-the-synthesis).

## Reading conventions

- **Page citations** use the journal pagination printed on each PDF: Rainville pp. 968–971, Hofbauer pp. 402–411, Zubieta pp. 311–315, Kulkarni pp. 3133–3142. Tiemann's PLoS ONE article is cited by its article pages 1–8.
- **All five PDFs are published versions** downloaded from the publishers. The Rainville and Zubieta files are *Science* page-range downloads. Their first page opens with the tail of the article that preceded them in the issue, which is not part of these reports.
- **Brain-region abbreviations.**
  - S1 / S2: primary / secondary somatosensory cortex.
  - ACC: anterior cingulate cortex. pACC: perigenual ACC. aMCC / pMCC: anterior / posterior mid-cingulate cortex.
  - IC: insular cortex. OFC: orbitofrontal cortex. M1: primary motor cortex. NAcc: nucleus accumbens. PAG: periaqueductal grey.
- **Method abbreviations.**
  - PET: positron emission tomography.
  - rCBF: regional cerebral blood flow, the PET index of local neural activity in papers 1, 2 and 4.
  - LEP: laser-evoked potential (EEG).
  - MPQ: McGill Pain Questionnaire.
  - VAS / NRS: visual-analogue / numerical rating scale.
- **"Lateral" and "medial" pain systems.**
  - *Lateral*: the lateral thalamus → S1/S2 route, traditionally linked to the sensory-discriminative side of pain.
  - *Medial*: the medial thalamus → ACC, prefrontal cortex and amygdala route, traditionally linked to the affective-motivational side.
- **Statistics are quoted as reported**, including a few internal inconsistencies, which are flagged rather than corrected.
- **No invented mathematics.** Equations appear only where the paper itself gives one (Zubieta's binding measure, Kulkarni's contrast definitions).
- **Vocabulary.** "Pain" is the correct word for these human studies. Where this project's artificial agents are mentioned, their signal is **nociception**.

## 1. Rainville et al. (1997) — Pain Affect Encoded in Human Anterior Cingulate But Not Somatosensory Cortex [tier: full]

**Citation:** Pierre Rainville, Gary H. Duncan, Donald D. Price, Benoît Carrier, M. Catherine Bushnell, "Pain Affect Encoded in Human Anterior Cingulate But Not Somatosensory Cortex," *Science* 277(5328): 968–971, 1997. DOI 10.1126/science.277.5328.968.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Rainville et al. 1997 - Pain affect encoded in human anterior cingulate but not somatosensory cortex.pdf`. Published version (science.org download, 5 PDF pages; page 1 begins with the end of the preceding article; the last page is a publisher cover sheet).

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

By the mid-1990s brain scans had shown that painful heat activates several cortical areas at once: S1, S2, the insula and the ACC. The old idea, from Melzack & Casey (1968), was that pain has a *sensory* side (location, quality, intensity) and an *affective* side (unpleasantness). Neurosurgery offered a hint about where the affective side lives. Patients whose cingulate cortex had been cut sometimes said they still felt pain but were less bothered by it. No experiment in healthy people had yet tested which area does what, because intensity and unpleasantness normally rise and fall together. Rainville and colleagues used **hypnotic suggestion** as a tool: they told hypnotised volunteers that the same hot water would feel more (or less) unpleasant, then measured brain blood flow with PET.

#### Key Findings

1. **Unpleasantness changed; intensity did not change significantly.**
   - Unpleasantness ratings: about 81/100 under "more unpleasant" suggestions vs. 45/100 under "less unpleasant".
   - Intensity ratings: 78 vs. 71, not significantly different.
2. **Only the ACC followed the change.** Painful heat activated S1, the insula and the ACC in both suggestion conditions. Only the ACC was significantly more active when the pain was made more unpleasant. S1 was, if anything, slightly (non-significantly) *less* active.
3. **ACC activity tracked unpleasantness ratings.** The model removed differences between people, intensity ratings and session. With those removed, ACC blood flow correlated with unpleasantness (r = 0.42). The insula and S1 did not.
4. **The authors' conclusion:** pain unpleasantness is specifically encoded in the ACC, with "at least a partial segregation of function between pain affect and sensation" (p. 970).

#### Initial Takeaway

This was the first direct experimental evidence in healthy humans that a limbic frontal region (the ACC) tracks how bad pain feels, separately from how strong it feels. It became the founding citation for "pain affect lives in the ACC". The authors themselves described the segregation as partial and the regions as highly interactive.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Design

| Element | As reported |
|---|---|
| Participants | 8 healthy volunteers (3 female, 5 male, aged 19–53). **Preselected** from a larger tested group for moderate-to-high hypnotic suggestibility (Stanford Scale Form A) *and* for robust modulation of unpleasantness (note 9, pp. 970–971). |
| Sessions | 11 sessions: 5 participants once, 3 participants twice. 12 PET scans per session. |
| Stimulus | 75-s passive immersion of the left hand in 35 °C ("neutral") or 46.5–47.5 °C ("painfully hot") water. The hot temperature was tuned per person to a pain rating of 40–80/100. A tonic stimulus was chosen because tonic pain "has a stronger affective component than phasic pain" (note 10, p. 971). |
| Conditions | Alert control → hypnosis control → hypnotic suggestion for increased (+UNP) or decreased (−UNP) unpleasantness. **Order of the three states was fixed** "because of possible residual effects of hypnotic suggestions". Only hot/neutral order within controls and +UNP/−UNP block order were counterbalanced (note 9). |
| Suggestions | Adapted from Kiernan et al. (1995) and previously validated for separating sensory and affective pain (note 7). |
| Ratings | After each scan: intensity and unpleasantness on separate 0–100 scales. The intensity anchor was "no burning, pricking, stinging sensation" → "extremely intense sensation". The unpleasantness anchor was "not at all unpleasant" → "extremely unpleasant". Ratings above 100 were allowed (note 11). |
| Imaging | H₂¹⁵O PET (Siemens ECAT HR+), 40-s frame; MRI for Talairach normalisation (notes 12–13). |
| Analysis | For each state, subtract neutral from painfully hot. Directed searches in right (contralateral) S1, S2, ACC and IC. Threshold t = 2.55 (p < .05 one-tailed, corrected for a 4-resel search volume; note 15). Then volume-of-interest (VOI) comparisons +UNP vs. −UNP, and an ANCOVA regression of rCBF on unpleasantness. |

#### Measures and key statistics (as reported)

- **Hypnosis alone did nothing detectable.** Alert vs. hypnosis control: intensity 77.6 ± 14.7 vs. 75.0 ± 14.0; unpleasantness 61.4 ± 28.8 vs. 54.8 ± 25.8, both non-significant. S1, S2, IC and ACC were pain-activated in both control states (Table 1, p. 969).
- **Suggestion effect on ratings.** Unpleasantness 81.4 ± 14.6 (+UNP) vs. 45.0 ± 25.8 (−UNP), ANOVA P < 0.001. Intensity 78.0 ± 14.6 vs. 71.2 ± 18.2, "statistically not significant" (p. 969).
- **Pain-evoked peaks in the suggestion conditions (Table 1).**
  - S1: t = 3.84 (+UNP) and 4.61 (−UNP).
  - ACC: t = 6.11 and 4.42.
  - IC: t = 4.50 and 4.66.
  - S2: "No peak with t > 2.50" in either condition.
- **VOI contrast +UNP vs. −UNP.** Significant only in ACC (P < 0.02). S1 rCBF was nonsignificantly lower in +UNP (p. 969). VOI radii of 10–20 mm gave similar results (note 16).
- **ANCOVA.** Residual rCBF after removing between-person variability, perceived intensity and session, correlated with unpleasantness:
  - ACC: r = 0.419, P = 0.005.
  - IC: r = 0.245, P = 0.134.
  - S1: r = −0.224, P = 0.149 (p. 970).
  - The ACC correlation peak lay within the ACC region activated in both suggestion conditions (Fig. 2).

#### Inferential step (from result to claim)

1. The suggestions produced a *perceptual* dissociation. Unpleasantness moved significantly; intensity did not.
2. Of the three regions activated during suggestion, only the ACC differed between the two suggestion conditions.
3. ACC activity covaried with unpleasantness after intensity was statistically removed; S1 and IC did not.
4. Therefore ACC activity is "consistent with the encoding of perceived unpleasantness" (abstract, p. 968). The authors go further: "direct evidence of a specific encoding of pain unpleasantness in the ACC" (p. 970).
5. **Functional speculation** (p. 970). ACC output might "command immediate behavioral reactions" through nearby motor and attentional ACC zones and connections with the midbrain PAG. The level of ACC activation is proposed as "determinant in the individual's emotional and behavioral reactions to pain". No behaviour was measured.
6. **Hedge** (p. 970). Sensory features "are major factors in determining unpleasantness", and the regions are "highly interactive". The claim is "at least a partial segregation".

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- **Small, selected sample.** N = 8, chosen for strong suggestibility *and* for already showing the desired rating dissociation. The rating dissociation is therefore partly built into the sample.
- **"Intensity fixed" means "not significantly different".** The 6.8-point intensity difference (78.0 vs. 71.2) goes the same way as the unpleasantness change. With N = 8 this is not evidence of equivalence. The ANCOVA does partial out intensity at the scan level.
- **Fixed order.** The suggestion conditions always came last, so time-in-scanner, habituation and fatigue are confounded with the suggestion states compared with the controls. The +UNP vs. −UNP comparison itself was counterbalanced.
- **S2 was absent** in both suggestion conditions. The authors offered attention/effort or habituation as explanations. Hofbauer et al. (2001) later argued against habituation (see entry 2).
- **Regional scope.** The directed search covered four regions with a one-tailed corrected threshold, so the paper does not test other candidate areas (e.g., prefrontal, amygdala).
- **What "affect" means here.** Affect is operationalised as a verbal unpleasantness rating only. The motivational half of Melzack & Casey's "affective-motivational" dimension (escape, avoidance) was not measured. The behavioural link in point 5 above is proposal, not data.

### Appendix: Section-by-Section Backbone

*Science* Reports have no section headings. The backbone follows the paragraph order of the paper.

**Abstract (p. 968).** Multiple pain-activated cortical regions; hypnotic suggestions altered unpleasantness "without changing the perceived intensity"; PET showed changes in ACC, "whereas primary somatosensory cortex activation was unaltered"; links frontal-lobe limbic activity to pain affect "as originally suggested by early clinical lesion studies".

**¶ Background (p. 968).** Affect vs. sensory dimensions (Melzack & Casey 1968); separate pathways postulated (Fields 1987); frontal lobotomy / cingulotomy patients feel pain but find it less distressing (Foltz & White 1962; Hurt & Ballantine 1973; Corkin & Hebben 1981); S1/S2 as sensory candidates from anatomy; prior imaging shows ACC, rostral insula, S1, S2 activation (Talbot 1991; Jones 1991; Casey 1994; Coghill 1994); monkey nociceptive input to these regions. Contribution of each region "largely unknown and untested".

**¶ Approach (pp. 968–969).** Hypnosis as "a cognitive tool"; suggestions to increase and decrease unpleasantness without changing intensity (note 7); PET during alert control, hypnosis control, +UNP, −UNP; 35 °C vs. 47 °C hand immersion; post-scan ratings.

**¶ Imaging methods (p. 969; notes 12–16).** H₂¹⁵O PET; MRI co-registration to Talairach space; hot − neutral subtraction; Worsley statistics; directed searches in right S1, S2, ACC, IC; corrected threshold.

**¶ Control results (p. 969).** Alert control reproduces S1, S2, IC, ACC activation; hypnosis control likewise; hypnosis induction had no significant effect on either rating.

**¶ Suggestion results (p. 969; Table 1).** Pain-related activation in S1, ACC, IC under both +UNP and −UNP; no S2 peak (possible attention demand or habituation). Ratings: unpleasantness differs (P < 0.001), intensity does not. VOI comparisons: only ACC greater in +UNP (P < 0.02); S1 nonsignificantly lower.

**¶ Regression (pp. 969–970; Fig. 2).** ANCOVA removing subject, intensity and session: ACC r = 0.419 (P = 0.005); IC and S1 non-significant.

**¶ Interpretation (p. 970).** ACC modulation "closely parallels a selective change" in unpleasantness; "direct evidence of a specific encoding of pain unpleasantness in the ACC"; ACC as recipient of "a highly modifiable pain pathway"; proximity to motor and attentional ACC and connections to PAG "fight or flight" regions might let it "command immediate behavioral reactions".

**¶ Caveat (p. 970).** Regions are interconnected and interactive; sensory features largely determine unpleasantness (Price 1988); "at least a partial segregation of function".

**Notes 9–16 (pp. 970–971).** Participant selection and session structure; stimulus rationale (tonic > phasic for affect); rating scales; PET acquisition; MRI and normalisation; statistical threshold; VOI procedure.

## 2. Hofbauer et al. (2001) — Cortical Representation of the Sensory Dimension of Pain [tier: full]

**Citation:** Robert K. Hofbauer, Pierre Rainville, Gary H. Duncan, M. Catherine Bushnell, "Cortical Representation of the Sensory Dimension of Pain," *Journal of Neurophysiology* 86(1): 402–411, 2001. DOI 10.1152/jn.2001.86.1.402.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Hofbauer et al. 2001 - Cortical representation of the sensory dimension of pain.pdf`. Published version (journals.physiology.org download, 10 pages).

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

This is the companion to Rainville et al. (1997), by the same Montreal group with almost the same method. The earlier study changed *unpleasantness* by hypnotic suggestion and found the ACC followed. This one changed *intensity*: hypnotised volunteers were told the heat would feel stronger or weaker. The aim was to see whether S1 now follows while the ACC does not. That pattern would complete a "double dissociation", the strongest form of evidence that two brain areas do different jobs.

#### Key Findings

1. **Intensity suggestions worked, but not cleanly.** Intensity ratings changed strongly. **Unpleasantness ratings also changed**, and the two ratings were highly correlated (r = 0.81).
2. **S1 followed the intensity suggestion.** Pain-evoked S1 activity was significantly greater under "stronger" than under "weaker" suggestions. S2 showed a similar, non-significant trend.
3. **The ACC did not follow.** Its activity did not differ between the two intensity-suggestion conditions.
4. **The authors combine this with the 1997 study** to claim a double dissociation: somatosensory cortex for the sensory dimension, the ACC for the affective one.
5. **But they reject a simple split.** Here unpleasantness changed as a *by-product* of intensity, and the ACC did not change significantly. They conclude that the ACC matters most when unpleasantness is driven by cognitive factors "associated with the meaning of pain", and that the "cortical representation of pain affect depends on the specific factors that contribute to this dimension" (p. 409).

#### Initial Takeaway

The two Montreal studies together are usually cited as "S1 = intensity, ACC = unpleasantness". This paper supports that summary at the level of *which region is modulated by which suggestion*. Its own data and conclusion also argue against a strict one-region-per-dimension mapping.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Design

| Element | As reported |
|---|---|
| Participants | 10 healthy right-handed volunteers (4 male, 6 female; aged 20–35, mean 24.2). **Selected** from 22 "for their ability to modulate pain sensation reliably". Stanford Hypnotic Susceptibility Scale-A scores ranged 1–10 (mean 6.9), a wider range than in 1997 but not significantly different (p. 403). |
| Stimulus | 60-s immersion of the left hand in 35 °C (warm) or 46.0–47.5 °C water. The hot temperature was set per person to a pain intensity of 40–80/100 (p. 403). |
| Conditions (Table 1, p. 403) | 12 scans. Alert control (2 warm, 2 painful) → hypnosis control (2 warm, 2 painful) → +pain-intensity (2 painful) → −pain-intensity (2 painful). The order of states was fixed; stimulus order within controls and suggestion-block order were reversed in half the subjects. |
| Ratings | After each scan: intensity and unpleasantness on separate 0–100 magnitude-estimation scales, with the same anchors as in 1997. Warmth was rated if the stimulus was not painful (pp. 403–404). Heart rate was recorded 1 min before and 1 min during each scan. |
| Imaging | H₂¹⁵O PET (ECAT HR+), first 40 s; 14-mm smoothing; Talairach normalisation (p. 404). |
| Analyses | (i) Directed searches for pain-related rCBF (painful − warm) in right S1, S2, ACC and IC, within 15 mm of the mean 1997 coordinates, at t = 2.5 corrected. (ii) Direct contrast +intensity − −intensity in the same regions. (iii) Whole-brain global search at t = 4.5 (p. 404). |

#### Measures and key statistics (as reported)

- **Controls.** No difference in intensity or unpleasantness between alert and hypnosis control (P > 0.05). S1, S2, ACC and IC were pain-activated in both. Hypnosis 47 °C vs. alert 47 °C showed no difference (pp. 404–405).
- **Ratings across the four painful conditions** (values shown only in Fig. 1):
  - Intensity: F(3,21) = 22.89, P < 0.001.
  - Unpleasantness: F(3,21) = 11.58, P < 0.001.
  - Intensity–unpleasantness correlation: r = 0.812, P < 0.001 (p. 405).
  - *Reviewer note:* error df = 21 over four conditions implies 8 complete rating sets, although N = 10. The paper does not explain this.
- **Heart rate.** Rose with noxious stimulation (P < 0.005). No effect of condition (P > 0.19; Table 2).
- **Direct contrast +intensity vs. −intensity (Table 5, p. 406).**
  - S1: t = 2.91, significant.
  - S2: t = 2.28, n.s.
  - ACC: t = 1.43, n.s.
  - Insula: mixed; middle insula +3.00, rostral insula −2.62.
- **Each suggestion condition vs. the hypnosis-control painful condition.** S1 showed no significant change, so the authors cannot say whether S1 went up, down or both (p. 406). S2 was significantly lower under −intensity (t = −2.6).
- **Global search (Table 4).** S1 reached whole-brain significance only under +intensity (t = 5.04). Also right putamen (+intensity) and frontal peaks (−intensity).

#### Inferential step (from result to claim)

1. The suggestion targeted intensity, and S1 activity differed between +intensity and −intensity while ACC activity did not.
2. In 1997, suggestion targeted unpleasantness, and ACC activity differed while S1 activity did not.
3. Crossing the two studies gives "a double dissociation of cortical modulation", indicating "a relative specialization of the sensory and the classical limbic cortical areas" (abstract, p. 402; p. 407).
4. **Complication.** Unpleasantness also changed here, but ACC did not change significantly. The authors therefore separate:
   - *primary* modulation of affect (direct suggestion, 1997), which "may involve both direct and indirect modulatory influences on ACC";
   - *secondary* modulation (affect following sensation, as here), which "may involve only a subset of modulatory circuits without a predominant (significant) influence on ACC activity" (p. 407).
   - This fits a successive-stage model in which unpleasantness is "highly (but not exclusively) dependent on pain sensation" (Wade et al. 1996; Price 2000).
5. **Conclusion** (p. 409). The results "speak against a simple dichotomous description of brain areas underlying pain sensation and pain affect".

#### Further points from the Discussion (pp. 407–409)

- **Lesion support for S1.** Ploner et al. (1999) described a patient with S1/S2 stroke who had no pain sensation but "an ill-localized and ill-defined unpleasant feeling", i.e., "pain affect was present in the absence of pain sensation" (p. 407).
- **Against the touch explanation.** The stimulus was tonic and stationary, which argues against Peyron et al.'s (1999) view that S1 pain activity reflects the touch of a moving probe (p. 407).
- **S2 in 1997.** S2 was activated throughout this study, so its absence in the 1997 suggestion conditions was probably not habituation. Instead it may reflect "the reduced emphasis on pain sensation" (pp. 407–408).
- **Insula.** Its role in intensity coding is "complicated". Pain asymbolia after insular lesions ("pain sensations appear to be normal but behavioral and physiological responses … are atypical") is cited as evidence the insula conveys affect (p. 408).
- **ACC and sensation.** The ACC "may contribute, to some extent, to the sensory aspects". Evidence cited: intensity-coding ACC neurons in rabbit and human (Sikes & Vogt 1992; Hutchison et al. 1999), and intensity changes after cingulotomy (Davis et al. 1994) (p. 409).

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- **No clean rating-level dissociation.** The survey's "reverse" framing suggests a mirror-image dissociation. At the rating level there is none: unpleasantness moved with intensity (r = 0.81). The dissociation is between *which suggestion* modulates *which region*, across two different samples tested in different studies.
- **The S1 effect is modest.** It is t = 2.91 in a directed search. Neither suggestion condition differed from the control painful condition on its own. S1 was whole-brain significant only in the +intensity condition.
- **Absence of evidence.** The ACC null (t = 1.43) is not a demonstration that the ACC was unchanged. The two studies were never analysed together in a single model testing a region × suggestion-type interaction.
- **Limits shared with 1997.** Small preselected sample and fixed state order.

### Appendix: Section-by-Section Backbone

**Abstract (p. 402).** PET before/after hypnotic suggestions to modulate intensity; 10 volunteers; four conditions; noxious stimuli activate contralateral S1, S2, ACC, IC; intensity modulation changes S1, "in contrast to our previous study" where unpleasantness modulation changed ACC; "double dissociation" → "relative specialization".

**Introduction (pp. 402–403).** Pain's two axes: sensory-discriminative and affective-motivational (unpleasantness plus behavioural and autonomic reactions) (Melzack & Casey 1968; Fernandez & Turk 1992; Price et al. 1987). S1/S2 anatomy and neuron coding support a sensory role. ACC/IC are limbic; medial thalamic nociceptive input to ACC; nociceptive ACC neurons in humans, monkeys, rabbits; IC and autonomic/homeostatic functions (Craig). Dimensions normally correlate ("as pain becomes more intense, it usually becomes more unpleasant, motivates more vigorous escape or avoidance behaviors"). Clinical dissociations (myocardial infarction vs. heartburn). The hypnotic paradigm (Rainville et al. 1999) and its 1997 result; the present aim is the sensory dimension.

**Methods (pp. 403–404).**
- *Subjects*: N = 10, selected from 22; SHSS-A.
- *Stimulation*: 60-s water immersion.
- *Experimental design*: Table 1, fixed state order.
- *Hypnotic induction and suggestion*: adapted from Bourassa & Leclerc 1991 and Kiernan et al. 1995; SHSS-A subtests between scans.
- *Psychophysical and physiological measurement*: rating scales, heart rate, ANOVA.
- *Scanning*: PET/MRI parameters.

**Discussion (pp. 406–409).**
- *General*: distributed nociceptive network; double dissociation across the two studies; secondary affect modulation without significant ACC change; successive-stage model; correlation precludes regression.
- *Role of somatosensory cortices*: lesion evidence (Greenspan 1999; Ploner 1999); S1 disputed (Peyron 1999) and rebutted by the stationary stimulus; attention and suggestions modulate S1; S2 absence in 1997 not habituation; S2 may code intensity.
- *Role of ACC in the affective dimension*: consistent with cingulotomy and Tölle et al. 1999; location differences between studies; ACC contribution "may be most determinant when pain unpleasantness is highly dependent on cognitive factors associated with the meaning of pain" (p. 408); Price 2000; somatosensory areas may contribute to emotion (Adolphs 2000; Damasio 2000).
- *Role of IC*: positive and negative differences; cold-intensity coding (Craig 2000); pain asymbolia; autonomic control; anterior shift possibly anticipatory.
- *Possible participation of ACC in the sensory dimension*: intensity-coding neurons; cingulotomy and capsulotomy effects.

## 3. Zubieta et al. (2001) — Regional Mu Opioid Receptor Regulation of Sensory and Affective Dimensions of Pain [tier: full]

**Citation:** Jon-Kar Zubieta, Yolanda R. Smith, Joshua A. Bueller, Yanjun Xu, Michael R. Kilbourn, Douglas M. Jewett, Charles R. Meyer, Robert A. Koeppe, Christian S. Stohler, "Regional Mu Opioid Receptor Regulation of Sensory and Affective Dimensions of Pain," *Science* 293(5528): 311–315, 2001. DOI 10.1126/science.1060952.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Zubieta et al. 2001 - Regional mu opioid receptor regulation of sensory and affective dimensions of pain.pdf`. Published version (science.org download, 6 PDF pages; page 1 begins with the end of the preceding article; the last page is a publisher cover sheet).

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

The brain has its own painkillers, the endogenous opioids. They act on the same μ-opioid ("mu") receptors that morphine targets. Before this study, human evidence for their release during pain came mostly from chronic-pain patients. Zubieta and colleagues used a PET tracer, [¹¹C]carfentanil, that sticks to free mu receptors. When the brain releases its own opioids, fewer receptors are free and the tracer signal drops. They compared sustained jaw-muscle pain with a placebo condition in healthy people. They then asked whether *how much* opioid activity a person showed in a given region predicted how intense or how emotionally unpleasant that person's pain was.

#### Key Findings

1. **Sustained pain triggered opioid release** in several regions: thalamus, amygdala, hypothalamus, insula, cingulate and prefrontal cortex. Receptor availability fell by roughly 4–12%.
2. **More opioid activity went with less pain, and the regions differed by questionnaire subscale.**
   - People with more activation in the nucleus accumbens, thalamus and amygdala (on the side of the pain) gave lower *sensory* pain-questionnaire scores.
   - People with more activation in the dorsal ACC, thalamus and nucleus accumbens gave lower *affective* scores.
3. **The authors' conclusion.** Some regions regulate "primarily sensory (e.g., ipsilateral nucleus accumbens and amygdala) or affective (e.g., anterior cingulate)" components, while the thalamus regulates both (p. 314).

#### Initial Takeaway

This was the first direct human measurement of a specific neurotransmitter system during pain, and it tied opioid activity to both dimensions of pain. The "different regions for different dimensions" message rests on correlations *between people*. The regions also overlap: the thalamus and one nucleus accumbens appear in both maps.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Design

| Element | As reported |
|---|---|
| Participants | 20 healthy right-handed volunteers (13 men, 7 women; aged 20–30, mean 24 ± 2). Non-smokers, no medical or psychiatric history, no psychotropic or hormonal medication. Women scanned in the follicular phase with ovulatory cycles confirmed (note 20). |
| Manipulation | Each person scanned twice, 2 h apart: sustained pain vs. placebo, both "applied in the masseter (jaw) muscles". Order was double-blind, randomised and counterbalanced. The challenge started 20 min after tracer injection and lasted 20 min. Pain intensity was "maintained constant (40 to 60 visual analog scale units)" by the method of ref. 23 (Zhang et al. 1993) (p. 311). The side of the challenge was counterbalanced right/left (note 31). |
| Ratings | Pain intensity every 15 s (VAS). After the scan, the McGill Pain Questionnaire (MPQ) sensory and "pain-specific affective" subscales (p. 311). |
| Imaging | [¹¹C]carfentanil PET (Siemens ECAT EXACT), bolus plus infusion, 70 min. Binding potential computed from 20–70 min by modified Logan analysis with occipital cortex as reference region (note 24). |
| Analyses | (i) Hypothesis-driven VOIs (9-mm spheres) in anterior and ventrolateral thalamus and amygdala, bilaterally (Table 1). (ii) Voxel-wise SPM99 pain vs. placebo (Table 2). (iii) Voxel-wise correlation of the placebo-minus-pain change in binding with MPQ sensory and affective scores (Figs. 2–3). |

**Binding measure (note 24, p. 314).** The Logan slope is the distribution volume ratio (DVR), which the paper relates to receptor availability as

$$
\mathrm{DVR} \;=\; \frac{B_{\max}}{K_d} + 1, \qquad \text{binding potential} \;=\; \frac{B_{\max}}{K_d} \;=\; \mathrm{DVR} - 1 .
$$

Here `B_max` is receptor density and `K_d` the tracer's dissociation constant. A *reduction* in binding potential from placebo to pain is read as "activation of the μ-opioid receptor system". The paper names two possible mechanisms: endogenous ligand competing with the tracer, and/or receptor internalisation (p. 311).

#### Measures and key statistics (as reported)

- **VOI analysis (Table 1, paired t, df = 19).** Significant reductions:
  - Ipsilateral amygdala: 1.90 → 1.77 (−5.1 ± 13.4%), t = 2.05, P = 0.05.
  - Contralateral ventrolateral thalamus: 1.04 → 0.96 (−7.1 ± 10.5%), t = 2.93, P = 0.009.
  - Other VOIs non-significant.
- **Voxel-wise pain vs. placebo (Table 2).**
  - Contralateral anterior thalamus: z = 5.06 (−7.2%).
  - Contralateral lateral thalamus: z = 4.83 (−11.3%).
  - Ipsilateral amygdala: z = 4.55 (−6.0%).
  - Contralateral hypothalamus: z = 4.61 (−3.9 ± 33.1%).
  - Significant after cluster-size correction: cingulate cortex z = 3.49 (−10.7%), contralateral anterior insula z = 3.97, prefrontal BA 8/9 bilaterally.
  - No region showed increased availability.
- **Sensory correlations (Fig. 2, p. 313).** MPQ sensory scores correlated negatively with activation in ipsilateral thalamus (z = 8.66), ipsilateral nucleus accumbens (z = 6.89) and ipsilateral amygdala (z = 4.92). PAG was sub-threshold (z = 4.15; p. 312).
- **Affective correlations (Fig. 3, p. 313).** MPQ pain-specific affective scores correlated negatively with activation in:
  - dorsal ACC, (−8, −3, 46): z = 4.11, P < 0.0001 corrected;
  - thalamus: ipsilateral z = 5.76, contralateral z = 4.19;
  - ipsilateral nucleus accumbens: z = 4.44, P < 0.001.
  - Contralateral nucleus accumbens (P = 0.09) and cerebellar vermis (P = 0.08) did not reach significance.
- **No test compared sensory and affective correlations directly.** The paper does not report whether the ACC–affective correlation is reliably stronger than an ACC–sensory correlation, or the reverse for the amygdala. It also does not report the correlation between the two MPQ subscales.

#### Inferential step (from result to claim)

1. Sustained pain lowered free mu-receptor availability in specific regions, read as endogenous opioid release.
2. Across the 20 people, larger regional release went with lower MPQ scores. So release is "associated with reductions in the sensory and affective ratings" (abstract, p. 311). This is read as antinociceptive regulation.
3. The regions whose release correlated with sensory scores were not identical to those correlating with affective scores, giving "distinct neuroanatomical involvements" (p. 311).
4. So the endogenous opioid system is "involved in the attenuation of sensory and pain-specific affective responses". Some regions are specialised (amygdala and NAcc → sensory; ACC → affective); the thalamus regulates both (p. 314).

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- **Correlation between people, not manipulation.** The two dimensions were not dissociated experimentally. Intensity was *held* at 40–60 VAS by design, so the variation being explained is individual differences in questionnaire scores.
- **The labels do not match the maps exactly.** The ipsilateral nucleus accumbens is called "primarily sensory" (p. 314) but also appears in the affective map (z = 4.44). The thalamus appears in both.
- **What "affective" means here.** It is the MPQ affective subscale (word descriptors such as fearful or exhausting), not an unpleasantness rating on a scale. It is not comparable one-to-one with the rating scales in entries 1, 2, 4 and 5.
- **Direction of inference.** "Regulation" is a causal word, but the design shows association only. The paper's own caveat covers individual variability, not causality.
- **The placebo condition** is not described in detail in the main text beyond "placebo administration".

## 4. Kulkarni et al. (2005) — Attention to Pain Localization and Unpleasantness Discriminates the Functions of the Medial and Lateral Pain Systems [tier: full]

**Citation:** B. Kulkarni, D. E. Bentley, R. Elliott, P. Youell, A. Watson, S. W. G. Derbyshire, R. S. J. Frackowiak, K. J. Friston, A. K. P. Jones, "Attention to pain localization and unpleasantness discriminates the functions of the medial and lateral pain systems," *European Journal of Neuroscience* 21(11): 3133–3142, 2005. DOI 10.1111/j.1460-9568.2005.04098.x.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Kulkarni et al. 2005 - Attention to pain localization and unpleasantness discriminates medial and lateral pain systems.pdf`. Published version (Wiley Online Library download, 10 pages). The file name shortens the title; the full title includes "…discriminates the functions of the…".

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

The Montreal studies (entries 1–2) used hypnosis, in two different groups of people. The Manchester/London group wanted to show the same division of labour "under more natural conditions" and "within a single experiment". They made two changes.

- **Sensory measure: location, not intensity.** Their argument was that intensity is encoded all over the brain's pain network, so intensity is not a pure sensory marker.
- **Manipulation: attention, not hypnosis.** Volunteers received identical laser heat pulses on the forearm. In some scans they focused on *where* each pulse landed. In others they focused on *how unpleasant* it was.

The laser heats the skin without touching it, so only pain and temperature nerve fibres are activated.

#### Key Findings

1. **Attending to location** increased pain-related activity in S1 and inferior parietal cortex. Both belong to the lateral, sensory system.
2. **Attending to unpleasantness** increased pain-related activity in:
   - the perigenual ACC and orbitofrontal cortex (both sides);
   - the amygdala, hypothalamus and posterior insula;
   - primary motor cortex (M1) and the frontal pole.
   - These are mainly medial-system, emotional and neuroendocrine regions.
3. **Unpleasantness ratings tracked cingulate activity.** Across scans, ratings of painful pulses correlated with activity in anterior cingulate / anterior mid-cingulate cortex, M1 and upper brainstem.
4. **Perceived pain did not differ between tasks.** The authors state that perceived pain was matched across tasks, so the brain differences come from attention alone.

#### Initial Takeaway

The paper presents itself as "the first clear demonstration, within a single experiment, of a major division of function within the neural pain matrix" (abstract, p. 3133). What it separates is *what the person attends to and reports*, not what the person feels. Pain intensity and unpleasantness were not dissociated perceptually. It also proposes, without testing it, that M1 activity during attention to unpleasantness reflects a system "involved in avoidance and/or escape responses" (p. 3139).

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Design

| Element | As reported |
|---|---|
| Participants | 17 right-handed healthy **male** volunteers (mean age 28, range 26–33), free of pain and of medical or psychiatric symptoms (p. 3134). |
| Stimulus | CO₂ laser on the left dorsal forearm, which "selectively activate[s] A-delta and C-fibre nociceptive afferents, sparing the touch-sensitive A-beta fibres" (p. 3134). There were four marked sites, 7 cm apart vertically and 3 cm horizontally, spaced so that localisation accuracy was ≥ 75%. Pulses moved pseudo-randomly between sites; subjects were blind to location. Energies were set per person to NRS level 7 (noxious) and level 3 (innocuous) on a 0–10 scale where 4 = just painful and 7 = moderate pain. |
| Tasks | *Attend to unpleasantness*: rate each pulse on a 0–4 scale (0 none, 1 pleasant warmth, 2 neutral, 3 moderately unpleasant, 4 strongly unpleasant). *Attend to location*: press the button for which of four sites was stimulated. *Control*: press buttons in sequence. Motor demands and number of response options were matched. Subjects were told not to rate intensity per stimulus (p. 3134). |
| Block ratings | After each block: retrospective average pain NRS, percentage of stimuli perceived as painful, and task confidence. |
| Factorial structure | 2 (noxious, innocuous) × 3 (unpleasantness, location, control), each block twice → 12 scans (Table 1, p. 3134). |
| Imaging | H₂¹⁵O PET (ECAT HR+, 3-D), 90-s frames, 14–16 stimuli per scan; SPM99; 16-mm smoothing; global normalisation; random-effects analysis; small-volume correction with regional masks (p. 3135). |

**Contrasts as defined in the paper (Table 1, p. 3134).** Number the cells 1 = unpleasantness/noxious, 2 = unpleasantness/innocuous, 3 = location/noxious, 4 = location/innocuous, 5 = control/noxious, 6 = control/innocuous. Then:

$$
\text{increase under attention to unpleasantness} \;=\; (1 - 2) - (3 - 4),
$$

$$
\text{increase under attention to location} \;=\; (3 - 4) - (1 - 2) \;=\; -1 \times \big[(1 - 2) - (3 - 4)\big],
$$

$$
\text{main effect of pain} \;=\; (1 + 3 + 5) - (2 + 4 + 6).
$$

Each interaction compares the *pain-related* response (noxious minus innocuous) between the two attention tasks. A region that is merely more active during one task, whether the pulse is painful or not, therefore cancels out.

#### Measures and key statistics (as reported)

- **Behaviour (p. 3135; Fig. 1).**
  - Unpleasantness: 3.4 ± 0.5 (noxious) vs. 1.8 ± 0.5 (innocuous), P < 0.01.
  - Localisation accuracy: 86.7 ± 9.8% (noxious) vs. 67.2 ± 18.2% (innocuous), P < 0.01.
  - Retrospective NRS: 4.9 ± 0.7 (painful blocks) vs. 2.4 ± 0.6 (non-painful blocks).
  - Percentage painful per task: 77.3 ± 10% (unpleasantness), 75.8 ± 5% (location), 77.9 ± 8% (control), P > 0.1.
  - Authors: "the perceived pain intensity (retrospective pain ratings) was matched across all three tasks" (p. 3135). *Reviewer note:* per-task NRS means are not reported; only the percent-painful figures are shown per task.
- **Main effect of pain.** Ipsilateral thalamus, bilateral ACC (BA 24), left anterior and right posterior insula, bilateral basal ganglia and S2, right S1, precuneus, SMA, cerebellum and upper brainstem (corrected P < 0.05). Pain in the control task alone (5 vs. 6) activated ACC, posterior insula/S2, basal ganglia and cerebellum, but not thalamus or S1 (p. 3135).
- **Unpleasantness > location interaction (Table 2, p. 3137; small-volume-corrected P).**
  - Right pACC: Z = 3.74, P = 0.01. Left pACC: Z = 3.38.
  - OFC (both sides): Z = 3.54, P = 0.03.
  - Left frontal pole: Z = 3.54, P = 0.03.
  - Right amygdala: Z = 3.34, P = 0.03.
  - Left hypothalamus: Z = 2.56, P = 0.05.
  - Left M1: Z = 3.40.
  - Left posterior insula: Z = 2.99, P = 0.01.
- **Location > unpleasantness interaction (Table 2).**
  - Right S1 (39, −30, 51): Z = 3.54, P = 0.05.
  - Inferior parietal cortex (46, −54, 58): Z = 4.45, P = 0.007. *Reviewer note:* Table 2 labels this "(L)", but x = +46 is right-hemisphere in MNI space, and the abstract and Fig. 2 caption say right.
  - Left precuneus, left M1 and right cerebellum did not reach significance.
- **Correlation with unpleasantness ratings (Table 3, p. 3137).**
  - Noxious scans: right and left aMCC (BA 32/24; ±4, 30, 20) Z = 3.68 / 3.54; left M1 Z = 4.68; upper brainstem Z = 3.35.
  - Innocuous scans: bilateral pMCC Z = 2.75; right M1 Z = 2.60.
  - *Reviewer note:* Table 3 labels the noxious peak aMCC, while the Fig. 3 caption says the correlation "was maximal within bilateral perigenual cingulate cortices" and the Discussion says "perigenual ACC extending into the aMCC".

#### Inferential step (from result to claim)

1. The stimuli were physically identical, and pain perception (percentage painful) did not differ between tasks. So differences in the *pain-related* response between tasks reflect attentional set (p. 3135).
2. Attending to location boosted lateral-system regions (S1, parietal). Attending to unpleasantness boosted medial, limbic and neuroendocrine regions.
3. Therefore there is "a broad division of function between components of the lateral and medial pain systems, confirming the primary hypothesis" (p. 3138). This was shown "without recourse to hypnosis", which "might result in a level of conflict between instructions and perceptions that results in confounding activations" (p. 3138).
4. **Conclusion** (p. 3140). pACC, aMCC and associated structures "are most likely to be concerned with the processing of the affective components of pain", while S1 and inferior parietal cortex process the "sensory-discriminative component".

#### Further points from the Discussion (pp. 3138–3140)

- **Differs from Rainville et al. (1997).** Rainville's unpleasantness-correlated cingulate region lay more posteriorly (in pMCC by Vogt's scheme). Faymonville et al. (2000) found hypnosis reduced both dimensions via the same MCC region, suggesting "the MCC possibly has a role in both affective and sensory processing" (p. 3138).
- **Links to other work.** pACC activation is linked to its high opioid-receptor density and to placebo/opiate analgesia overlap (p. 3139). Amygdala, OFC and hypothalamus are discussed in terms of fear, aversive conditioning and a parabrachial–hypothalamus–PAG loop "thought to have a major role in aversive behaviours" (Bester et al. 1999) (p. 3139).
- **M1.** M1 was active only in the unpleasantness task and correlated with unpleasantness, "despite the fact that subjects did not withdraw from the stimuli at any time". The authors read this as "a possible role of M1 in an affective-motivational system that may be involved in avoidance and/or escape responses" (p. 3139).
- **Bentley et al. (2004), as described here.** The group's own laser-evoked-potential study found a larger N300 during attention to location than to unpleasantness, "with no effect on the later P450 peak generated in the cingulate cortex" (p. 3140). *This review did not read Bentley 2004 (no PDF); this is Kulkarni et al.'s own description of it.*

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- **What is manipulated is attention, not perception.** Neither intensity nor unpleasantness was shown to change between tasks. The paper separates brain responses associated with *judging* location vs. *judging* unpleasantness. That differs from the Rainville/Hofbauer logic, where the percept itself was altered.
- **Task demands differ in kind.** Rating one's own affect is a self-referential evaluation; localising is a spatial discrimination. Some differences (OFC, frontal pole) may reflect evaluative vs. spatial judgement in general. The pain × task interaction contrast removes only task effects that are the same for noxious and innocuous stimuli.
- **Borderline sensory effect.** The key sensory-side result (right S1) is P = 0.05 after small-volume correction. The significance threshold was stated as corrected P < 0.01 in Methods but P < 0.05 in Results and captions.
- **Intensity matching is loosely documented.** The noxious stimuli, calibrated at NRS 7, were rated 4.9 on average in the scanner, around "just painful" on this scale.
- **The avoidance/escape interpretation of M1 is conjecture.** No withdrawal, avoidance or escape was measured.
- **Sample.** All-male (N = 17).

### Appendix: Section-by-Section Backbone

**Abstract (p. 3133).** Pain matrix; previous division of function shown only under hypnosis in two groups on separate occasions; PET, CO₂ laser, attention to unpleasantness or location; location → contralateral S1 and inferior parietal; unpleasantness → pACC, OFC, amygdala, hypothalamus, posterior insula, M1, frontal pole; "first clear demonstration, within a single experiment".

**Introduction (pp. 3133–3134).** Pain as multidimensional (Melzack 1968); lateral vs. medial division from post-mortem and neurosurgical observation (Bowsher 1957; Albe-Fessard 1985); lateral = lateral thalamus, S1, S2, somatotopic; medial = medial thalamus, ACC, prefrontal, amygdala, polysynaptic, non-somatotopic (Treede 1999); cingulotomy (Foltz & White 1962) and S1 lesions (Ploner 1999); prior imaging via hypnosis and attention (Rainville 1997; Peyron 1999; Tölle 1999; Hofbauer 2001); intensity encoded throughout the matrix (Coghill 1999; Derbyshire 1997; Büchel 2002), so localisation chosen as "a purer index of sensory-discriminative processing".

**Materials and methods (pp. 3134–3135).**
- *Ethics and participants*: N = 17 males.
- *Stimulus*: CO₂ laser.
- *PET procedure*: training session; four sites; level-3 and level-7 energies; three tasks × two intensities × two repeats; rating scales; block ratings; 2 × 3 design.
- *Image acquisition*: MRI; PET parameters.
- *Data analysis*: SPM99, random effects, small-volume correction; correlation analyses of affective ratings with rCBF, separately for noxious and innocuous scans.

- *Behavioural results*: unpleasantness and localisation differ by stimulus; retrospective NRS; percent painful equal across tasks.
- *rCBF responses*: main effect of pain; pain without instructions.

- *Correlation analysis*: noxious → ACC/aMCC, M1, brainstem; innocuous → pMCC, M1 (Table 3, Fig. 3).

**Discussion (pp. 3137–3140).**
- *General*: clear division of function; first without hypnosis; thalamic laterality differences.
- *Cingulate cortex*: pACC/aMCC/pMCC subdivisions (Vogt 2003); ACC in affect (Foltz; Derbyshire 1994; Tölle 1999; Singer 2004 empathy); pACC in strongly aversive pain; contrast with Rainville 1997 and Faymonville 2000; possible hypnosis confounds; attentional top-down effects (Bantick 2002; Valet 2004); Mesulam's motivational map; opioid-receptor density.
- *OFC, amygdala, insula, M1 and hypothalamus*: emotion, reward, aversive conditioning (Rolls 1999); avoidance-learning circuit; amygdala nociceptive neurons and fear pathways; hypothalamus as autonomic output; parabrachial–hypothalamus–PAG aversive loop; posterior insula; M1 without movement → possible avoidance/escape system.
- *Primary somatosensory and inferior parietal cortices*: S1 somatotopic modulation; S1 activity variably reported; spatial attention enhances early laser-evoked potentials (Valeriani 2000; Legrain 2002; Schlereth 2003; Bentley 2004); inferior parietal and spatial orienting.

**Conclusions (p. 3140).** pACC/aMCC → affective; S1/inferior parietal → sensory-discriminative; therapeutic appeal of reducing unpleasantness while preserving localisation.

## 5. Tiemann et al. (2014) — Dopamine Precursor Depletion Influences Pain Affect Rather than Pain Sensation [tier: full]

**Citation:** Laura Tiemann, Henrik Heitmann, Enrico Schulz, Jochen Baumkötter, Markus Ploner, "Dopamine Precursor Depletion Influences Pain Affect Rather than Pain Sensation," *PLoS ONE* 9(4): e96167, 2014. DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0096167.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Tiemann et al. 2014 - Dopamine precursor depletion influences pain affect rather than pain sensation.pdf`. Published version (open-access typeset article, 8 pages).

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

Dopamine is best known for its role in reward and motivation, but several lines of evidence also link it to pain:

- People with Parkinson's disease (low dopamine) often have pain and can be more pain-sensitive.
- People with schizophrenia have been reported to be less sensitive to pain.
- Some chronic pain conditions show altered dopamine signalling.

Two explanations had been proposed. Dopamine might dampen the incoming pain signal directly, or it might act "indirectly" on the cognitive and emotional side of pain. No study had compared these sides directly. Tiemann and colleagues temporarily lowered brain dopamine in healthy men with a drink of amino acids lacking dopamine's building blocks (tyrosine and phenylalanine). They then measured three things:

- the sensory side: single-pulse intensity ratings and EEG responses;
- the cognitive side: how much pain disrupted a visual reaction-time task;
- the affective side: unpleasantness ratings.

#### Key Findings

1. **The drink worked as intended.** Blood levels of the dopamine precursors fell sharply (brain-availability ratio of tyrosine −88%), and mood and motor speed did not change.
2. **Pain sensation was unchanged.** Pain thresholds, intensity ratings of 75 identical laser pulses, rating variability, habituation and EEG responses to pain did not differ from the control drink.
3. **Pain's interference with the attention task was unchanged.**
4. **Unpleasantness rose.** Unpleasantness ratings were higher under depletion (5.6 vs. 4.7 on 0–10, p = .048), while intensity ratings were not.
5. **Bigger depletion, bigger rise.** People whose tyrosine availability dropped more showed a larger increase in unpleasantness (r = .50).

#### Initial Takeaway

Lowering dopamine made brief laser pain feel worse but not stronger, and left the EEG pain response unchanged. The authors suggest dopamine's pain-relieving effects may work on pain affect, possibly by shifting "the motivation to endure or avoid pain" (p. 7), rather than on the incoming pain signal. The key affective result is a single post-task rating per condition at p = .048, and the authors list this as a limitation.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Design

| Element | As reported |
|---|---|
| Participants | 28 healthy **male** volunteers enrolled. 6 did not complete (4 regurgitated part of the drink, 1 vasovagal response after the blood draw, 1 personal reasons), leaving **22** (mean age 25, range 20–39). EEG data from 3 subjects (paradigm 1) and 1 subject (paradigm 2) were excluded for quality; the behavioural results were unchanged by these exclusions (pp. 1–3). |
| Manipulation | Double-blind within-subject crossover, one week apart, order balanced. **APTD** (acute phenylalanine and tyrosine depletion: amino-acid mixture lacking phenylalanine and tyrosine) vs. **BAL** (balanced mixture with all essential amino acids). Low-protein diet the day before and overnight fast. Testing 4–6 h after ingestion, "to coincide with maximum effects" (p. 2). |
| Manipulation checks | Serum amino acids at baseline and 5 h; ratios of tyrosine and phenylalanine to the long neutral amino acids (LNAA) as a marker of brain availability; tryptophan/LNAA to rule out serotonin effects; 7-item side-effect questionnaire; 16-item mood VAS (alertness, calmness, contentment); finger tapping (p. 2). |
| Stimulus | Tm:YAG laser (1960 nm, 1-ms pulse, 5-mm spot) on the right hand dorsum; site shifted slightly after each pulse (p. 3). |
| Paradigm 1 (sensory) | Pain threshold by method of limits. Then 75 identical 600-mJ pulses (interval 8–12 s), each rated for intensity on NRS 0–10, 3 s after the pulse; subjects told intensity "might vary". 64-channel EEG: laser-evoked potentials (N2/P2) and time-frequency responses (theta, alpha, gamma regions of interest) (p. 3). |
| Paradigm 2 (cognitive + affective) | Visual reaction-time task with interfering laser pulses; intensity individually set to NRS 5. **At the end of the task**, one VAS 0–10 rating each of intensity ("no pain" → "worst tolerable pain") and unpleasantness ("not unpleasant" → "highly unpleasant"). EEG gamma analysis (p. 3). |
| Statistics | Paired t-tests; repeated-measures ANOVAs; Pearson correlations; false-discovery-rate correction across electrodes and time/frequency points for EEG; α = .05 (p. 3). |

#### Measures and key statistics (as reported)

- **Depletion check (p. 4).**
  - Serum under APTD vs. morning baseline: tyrosine −78 ± 6%, phenylalanine −72 ± 11%. Levels after APTD were lower than after BAL: tyrosine t = 9.2, phenylalanine t = 11.6, both p < .001.
  - Tyrosine/LNAA: −88 ± 6% (APTD) vs. −40 ± 19% (BAL), interaction F(1,21) = 102.4.
  - Phenylalanine/LNAA: −84 ± 9% vs. −35 ± 10%, F(1,21) = 318.3.
  - Tryptophan/LNAA: no effect of time or condition.
- **Side effects, motor function and mood (p. 4).** Mild nausea was common (18 subjects at 30 min) but not linked to either mixture. Tapping did not differ (t = .2, p = .81). Mood showed no condition effects or interactions (F df 1,16 and 2,32; calmness interaction p = .06).
- **Paradigm 1 (p. 4).**
  - Thresholds: 291 ± 53 (APTD) vs. 283 ± 42 mJ (BAL), p = .47.
  - Mean intensity ratings: 4.1 ± 2.0 vs. 4.0 ± 1.9, t = .2, p = .85.
  - Variability p = .8; habituation slopes p = .8.
  - Laser-evoked potentials: no FDR-significant difference; N2P2 amplitude t = .1, p = .91.
  - Change in N2P2 did not correlate with change in intensity (r = .1) or unpleasantness (r = .01).
  - Time-frequency: pain increased theta and gamma and decreased alpha, with no difference between conditions.
- **Paradigm 2 (pp. 4–6).**
  - Pain slowed reaction times (F(1,21) = 9.2, p = .006). No condition effect (p = .1) and no pain × condition interaction (F = 1.8, p = .2). No condition effects on visual or pain-induced gamma.
  - **Unpleasantness: 5.6 ± 2.3 (APTD) vs. 4.7 ± 2.0 (BAL), t = 2.1, p = .048.** Intensity ratings n.s. (t = .9, p = .23). Stimulus energy n.s. (440 ± 87 vs. 470 ± 111 mJ).
  - Change in unpleasantness correlated with the decrease in tyrosine/LNAA (r = .501, p = .018), but not with phenylalanine decrease (r = .2) or phenylalanine/LNAA (r = .2). Change in intensity did not correlate with tyrosine/LNAA (r = .2, p = .31) (Fig. 5).
- *Reviewer note on reporting inconsistencies* (quoted as printed, not corrected):
  - The stimulus-energy comparison is given as "t = 1.2, p = .2" on p. 3 but "t = 1.2, p = .38" on p. 6.
  - BAL tyrosine change is "+6 ± 37%, t = 2.9, p = .4" on p. 4. A t of 2.9 with about 21 df would not give p = .4.
  - Intensity "t = .9, p = .23" on p. 6 is also atypical for that df.
  - None of these affects the direction of the main claims, but they lower confidence in the precision of the reported statistics.

#### Inferential step (from result to claim)

1. APTD reduced dopamine-precursor availability (confirmed by blood chemistry) without detectable change in mood, motor speed or serotonin-precursor ratio.
2. Every sensory index was unchanged: threshold, single-trial intensity, variability, habituation, evoked potentials and oscillations.
3. Unpleasantness rose, and the rise scaled with depletion strength. This dose-response relation is offered as evidence of dopamine specificity (p. 6).
4. Therefore "a selective influence of DA neurotransmission on pain affect, whereas pain sensation remained largely unchanged" (p. 6). Analgesic effects of dopamine "might be mediated by indirect effects on pain affect rather than by direct effects on ascending nociceptive signals" (abstract, p. 1).
5. **Speculative mechanism** (pp. 6–7). Dopamine is involved in reward motivation, and pain and reward share neurobiology (Leknes & Tracey 2008: a "common currency for emotion"). So "dopamine may, thus, influence the motivation to endure or avoid pain", and this bias "is subserved by a modulation of the affective component of pain perception" (p. 7).

#### Limitations stated by the authors (p. 7)

- Unpleasantness was measured only post hoc in paradigm 2, not trial by trial.
- The pain was phasic only; effects may differ for tonic pain.
- Possible effects on other catecholamines.
- No baseline mood ratings.
- Dopamine effects depend on time scale.
- The sample was all male.

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- **The affective effect is fragile.**
  - It rests on one retrospective rating per condition, significant at p = .048.
  - There was no correction for the number of behavioural outcomes tested (thresholds, mean, variability, habituation, reaction time, intensity, unpleasantness).
  - The supporting correlation (r = .50, N = 22) is with tyrosine/LNAA only; the parallel phenylalanine index did not correlate.
- **Asymmetric measurement.** Intensity was measured sensitively (75 single-trial ratings) in paradigm 1. Unpleasantness was measured once, after a different task, in paradigm 2. The "sensation unchanged" claim is therefore much better powered than the "affect changed" claim.
- **No neural correlate** of the unpleasantness change was found. The authors propose higher-level, non-time-locked processes (p. 7).
- **Conflicting literature, which the paper acknowledges.** Becker et al. (2013) found no thermal-pain effect of APTD or sulpiride. Nandhagopal et al. (2010) found that L-Dopa in Parkinson's disease *increased* unpleasantness, the opposite of the direction the authors would expect (p. 6).
- **The motivational link is not tested.** The "endure or avoid" interpretation is a hypothesis; no avoidance, escape or willingness-to-endure measure was taken.

## Batch-level notes for the synthesis

These notes are observations across the five entries, for the later cross-paper synthesis. They do not favour any theory.

1. **What "affect" was in practice.**
   - Four studies used a verbal *unpleasantness* rating (0–100, 0–4 or 0–10 scales).
   - Zubieta used the MPQ affective subscale.
   - **None measured avoidance, escape, approach, desire or willingness to endure.**
   - Motivational interpretations appear only as the authors' speculation: Rainville's ACC output that might "command immediate behavioral reactions" (p. 970); Kulkarni's M1 "avoidance and/or escape" (p. 3139); Tiemann's "motivation to endure or avoid pain" (p. 7).
2. **Four different dissociation strategies, of different strength.**
   - *Perceptual dissociation by suggestion*: Rainville, clean at the rating level; Hofbauer, not clean at the rating level.
   - *Individual-difference correlation*: Zubieta.
   - *Attentional set with perception held roughly constant*: Kulkarni.
   - *Pharmacological manipulation*: Tiemann, a rating dissociation without a neural correlate.
   - Only Rainville reports a significant rating dissociation *together with* a region-specific neural modulation in the same data.
3. **The "double dissociation" spans two studies.** Rainville (N = 8) and Hofbauer (N = 10) are separate samples. Hofbauer's intensity suggestions changed unpleasantness too (r = 0.81). The authors themselves conclude against "a simple dichotomous description" and propose that the ACC's role in affect depends on *why* unpleasantness changes.
4. **Where "affective" cingulate activity sits varies across these papers.**
   - Rainville's ACC is described by Kulkarni as more posterior (pMCC in Vogt's scheme).
   - Kulkarni's effects are perigenual/aMCC.
   - Zubieta's affective-opioid correlation is dorsal ACC (−8, −3, 46).
   - A synthesis that says "the ACC" should note that these are not the same subregion.
5. **Small, selected or single-sex samples.** Rainville used N = 8 selected for suggestibility and for showing the effect; Hofbauer N = 10 selected for modulating sensation. Kulkarni (N = 17) and Tiemann (N = 22) studied men only.
6. **Reporting inconsistencies** were found in Kulkarni (hemisphere label; aMCC vs. pACC naming; stated threshold) and Tiemann (p-values). They are flagged in the entries and quoted as printed.
7. **Bentley et al. (2004)** is described in the Kulkarni entry only as Kulkarni et al. report it. It was not independently reviewed (see below).
