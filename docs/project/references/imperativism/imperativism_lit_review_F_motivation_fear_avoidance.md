# Imperativism Field History — Part F: Pain as a Need State, a Defensive System, an Interrupt and a Driver of Avoidance

**Topic folder:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/`
**Part of:** a multi-file review of how ideas about pain's non-sensory side developed. Part A is [[imperativism_lit_review_A_imperative_theories]], the philosophy debate.
**This part:** 6 papers reviewed from PDF, 1 at full depth and 5 as short summaries. 7 more works on the same theme are named but not reviewed because no PDF was held. Reviewed one at a time, 2026-09-17.
**Reviewer:** literature-reviewer

## What this part is about (plain-language entry point)

**The theme.** Philosophers ask whether pain *commands* ("stop that!") or *evaluates* ("this is bad"), which is the debate in Part A. Clinicians and neuroscientists took up the same fact from another angle: pain makes animals and people *do* things. It interrupts what they are doing. It frightens them, and they learn to avoid whatever hurt them. This part follows two lines of work on that theme:

- **Clinical psychology.** The **fear-avoidance model** tries to explain why a few people with ordinary back pain become long-term disabled while most recover. Its answer is that the person who reads pain as a sign of serious damage becomes afraid, stops moving, and gets stuck in a loop. Four of the papers here trace that model over 16 years. In 2000 it was a loop driven by fear. By 2012–2016 it had become a picture of people choosing between avoiding pain and pursuing other goals they value.
- **Neuroanatomy.** Craig (2003) argues from nerve pathways in cats, monkeys and humans that pain is a **homeostatic emotion**. On this view pain is a feeling-plus-drive that keeps the body in balance, like hunger, thirst or feeling too cold. It is not a kind of touch.

**The one experiment.** Claes et al. (2015) gave 57 healthy volunteers mild electric shocks tied to one joystick movement. When a lottery-ticket reward was attached to that same movement, people chose it far more often. Their reported fear of the movement did not change, and neither did how intense or how unpleasant they rated the shock. The behaviour changed while the self-reports stayed put. That kind of split between measures is what a history of "unpleasantness vs. avoidance" needs to record.

**Why it matters for the history.** These papers show a field moving from *fear as the engine of avoidance* to *avoidance as one choice among competing goals*. That change bears directly on whether pain's pull on behaviour is best described as a feeling, a command or a weighed decision. This part records the positions and does not take sides.

## Reading conventions

- **Tiers.** `[tier: short]` entries give a structured summary only: Claim · Method/type · Key result or argument · Place in the field's history · Relevance. `[tier: full]` entries have Phase 1, Phase 2 and a section-by-section backbone appendix. The user authorised this split for the field-history expansion.
- **Page citations** follow the PDF held in `sources/`:
  - *Vlaeyen & Linton 2000*, *Craig 2003*, *Leeuw et al. 2006*: typeset published versions, cited by journal page ("p. N").
  - *Crombez et al. 2012*, *Claes et al. 2015*, *Vlaeyen et al. 2016*: unformatted author manuscripts, cited by manuscript page ("ms. p. N"). These do **not** match the journal pagination. The wording may differ from the published article.
- **No invented mathematics.** None of these papers has equations. The one experiment reports ANOVAs, and those are quoted as reported.
- **Vocabulary.** "Pain" is used as these papers use it, for humans and animals. A few more terms:
  - *Fear*: the response to a present, identifiable threat.
  - *Anxiety*: the response to a vague, future threat.
  - *Avoidance*: behaviour that prevents an aversive event. *Escape*: behaviour that ends one already under way.
  - *Catastrophizing*: interpreting pain as a sign of something extremely threatening.
  - *CS+ / CS−*: a cue (here, a movement) that is / is not followed by a shock.

  Where this project's artificial agents are mentioned, their damage signal is **nociception**, never "pain".

## 1. Vlaeyen & Linton (2000) — Fear-avoidance and its consequences in chronic musculoskeletal pain: a state of the art [tier: short]

**Citation:** Johan W.S. Vlaeyen & Steven J. Linton, *Pain* 85: 317–332, 2000. DOI 10.1016/s0304-3959(99)00242-0.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Vlaeyen and Linton 2000 - Fear-avoidance and its consequences in chronic musculoskeletal pain.pdf` (published version).

**Claim.** In a subgroup of patients, fear of pain and (re)injury, together with the avoidance it produces, explains how acute musculoskeletal pain becomes chronic disability. It explains this better than pain intensity or biomedical findings do. The authors endorse the view that "fear of pain and (re)injury may be more disabling than pain itself" (p. 329).

**Method / type.** Narrative "state of the art" review. It covers history, two models, evidence per prediction, assessment instruments and treatment.

**Key result or argument.**
- **History.** "Fear-avoidance" was first applied to pain by Lethem et al. (1983), with two opposite responses, *confrontation* and *avoidance* (p. 317–318). Other roots named: Fordyce's (1976) avoidance learning and, later, Philips's (1987) cognitive account, in which avoidance follows an *expectancy* that exposure will bring pain.
- **Model 1, the "activity avoidance" model** (Linton et al., 1984; Fig. 1, p. 318–319). A classical plus operant conditioning account. A situation paired with pain comes to trigger fear. Avoiding it is rewarded by relief. Avoidance then resists extinction "because successful avoidance prevents the person from coming into contact with the actual (non-harmful) consequences" (p. 319).
- **Model 2, the "fear" avoidance model** (Vlaeyen et al., 1995; Fig. 2, p. 319/329). Catastrophic interpretation of pain leads to pain-related fear. Fear leads to avoidance and hypervigilance, then to disability, disuse and depression, which feed back into pain. A person who does not catastrophize confronts the pain and recovers. The paper lists six predictions (p. 319).
- **Evidence.** Mostly cross-sectional.
  - Fear questionnaires correlate with poorer lifting and trunk-strength performance (Table 1: significant coefficients r = −.27 to −.49; two non-significant) and with disability (Table 2).
  - In one study, performance related to fear but not to pain intensity (Crombez et al., 1998; p. 322).
  - Fearful patients over-predict pain, and the over-prediction corrects with repeated exposure (p. 321).
  - The authors warn that the correlations "should not be confused with causal effects" (p. 323). They report one prospective study that did not support the concept (Burton et al., 1995; p. 323).
- **Treatment.** Screening, then education, then *graded exposure in vivo*. In a four-patient crossover, improvement came only in the exposure phase (p. 329).

**Place in the field's history.** This is the review that consolidated the cognitive-behavioural fear-avoidance model and made it the standard reference. It treats avoidance as *learned* and *fear-driven*, with pain intensity given a minor role. It cites Wall (1979) for the point that escape and avoidance "may be an effective and appropriate coping response in the short term" (p. 323).

**Relevance.** This entry sets the baseline the later entries revise: fear drives avoidance, and pain intensity is secondary. It also shows how the model *measured* avoidance at the time. The measures were questionnaires about avoidance beliefs and reduced task performance, not observed choices between options.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2000 · clinical-psychology
- Question it asked: What is the evidence that pain-related fear and avoidance cause the move from acute pain to chronic disability, and how should they be assessed and treated?
- Position / finding in one line: Pain-related fear, set off by catastrophic interpretation, drives avoidance, hypervigilance and disuse, and predicts disability better than pain intensity does, though the evidence is mainly correlational.
- Responds to / builds on: Lethem et al. 1983; Fordyce 1976; Fordyce et al. 1982; Turk et al. 1983; Philips 1987; Linton et al. 1984; Waddell et al. 1993; Vlaeyen et al. 1995a,b; Kori et al. 1990; Wall 1979; Cannon 1915; Beecher 1959
- Measures (empirical only): n.a. — review, no new data
- Dissociation reported (empirical only): n.a. — review. (Reviewed studies: performance tracked fear but not pain intensity, Crombez et al. 1998, p. 322.)
- Survey claim check: partly — "the fear-avoidance model across four iterations" → The paper sets out *two* models of its own, the Linton et al. 1984 "activity avoidance" model and the Vlaeyen et al. 1995 "fear" avoidance model, and credits Lethem et al. 1983 as the origin: "the term 'fear-avoidance' applied to the field of pain first appeared in an article by Lethem et al. in 1983" (p. 318). It reviews these versions rather than proposing a new one, and it does not count "iterations".
- PDF version: published

## 2. Craig (2003) — A new view of pain as a homeostatic emotion [tier: short]

**Citation:** A.D. (Bud) Craig, *Trends in Neurosciences* 26(6): 303–307, 2003 (Opinion). DOI 10.1016/s0166-2236(03)00123-1.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Craig 2003 - A new view of pain as a homeostatic emotion.pdf` (published version).

**Claim.** Pain is not a sub-type of touch. It is "both a distinct sensation and a motivation – that is, a specific emotion that reflects homeostatic behavioral drive, similar to temperature, itch, hunger and thirst" (p. 303).

**Method / type.** Opinion piece. It pulls together the author's own anatomical and physiological work in cats and monkeys and human imaging and lesion findings. No new data.

**Key result or argument.**
- **Against the "conventional view."** After gate-control theory (1965), pain was treated as convergent somatosensory activity. Craig says this is contradicted because "neither damage nor stimulation of somatosensory cortices affects pain" (p. 303).
- **The thermoregulation analogy.** Thermal affect is motivational and depends on body state: "a cool glass feels wonderful if your body is hot, but it feels gnawingly unpleasant if you are chilled" (p. 304). Pain likewise "comprises a sensation and a behavioral drive with reflexive autonomic adjustments". It "can be either unpleasant (as usual) or pleasant (as when it relieves an intense itch)" (p. 304).
- **Anatomy (Fig. 1, p. 305).** Small-diameter afferents run to lamina I of the spinal cord. From there they go to spinal autonomic and brainstem homeostatic sites (parabrachial nucleus, periaqueductal gray). In primates there are two further thalamic routes:
  - VMpo to dorsal posterior insula (interoceptive cortex), re-represented in right anterior insula;
  - MDvc to anterior cingulate cortex.

  "These generate the feeling and the motivation, respectively" (Fig. 1 legend, p. 305).
- **Physiology.** "Burning" pain cells that respond to heat, pinch and cold, the thermal grill illusion, and cold discomfort below about 24 °C imply that "homeostasis, rather than the heuristic simplification 'nociception', is the fundamental role" of the system (p. 306).
- **Species claim.** "Only primates seem to have the neuroanatomical capacity to feel pain in the same way that humans do" (p. 306). In non-primates the drive is integrated in the brainstem.
- **Conclusion.** Pain has "a distinct sensation, engendered in interoceptive and anterior insular cortex (the feeling self), and an affective motivation, engendered in the ACC (the behavioral agent)" (p. 306).

**Place in the field's history.** A neuroanatomical counterpart to the motivational readings of pain. It gives the sensation and the drive *separate cortical substrates* (insula vs. anterior cingulate) inside a single homeostatic system. It explicitly rejects the pattern theorists, Melzack and Wall included, as having "confounded [pain] with touch" (p. 304). It says the view "arises directly from functional anatomical findings in cat and monkey, rather than from philosophical considerations" (p. 304). Its claim that feeling pain as humans do is primate-specific is a strong position that later animal-circuit work debates.

**Relevance.** This is the main "need-state" framing in the corpus. It puts pain alongside hunger and thermal discomfort, which makes it the natural neuroscience comparison for philosophers who treat hunger and pain alike as imperatives (Klein 2007, Part A). The two views should not be merged: Craig calls pain an *emotion* with a sensory image and a drive, and never uses imperative language. His note that thermal affect flips with body state is a clear example of valence depending on internal state.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2003 · animal-circuits
- Question it asked: Is pain a sub-type of cutaneous (touch-like) sensation, or a distinct homeostatic feeling-plus-drive like temperature, hunger and thirst?
- Position / finding in one line: Pain is a homeostatic emotion carried by a dedicated lamina I pathway, with the sensation represented in insular cortex and the motivational drive in anterior cingulate cortex.
- Responds to / builds on: Melzack & Wall 1965 (and pattern theorists Goldscheider, Weddell, Noordenboos); Cannon 1939; James 1890; Darwin 1872; Rolls 1999; Damasio; Cabanac 1972; Craig 2002 (Nat Rev Neurosci); Craig 2003 (Annu Rev Neurosci)
- Measures (empirical only): n.a. — opinion/synthesis, no new data
- Dissociation reported (empirical only): n.a. — no new data. (Argued anatomical separation: sensation → insula, motivation → ACC, p. 305–306.)
- Survey claim check: yes — "recasts pain as a homeostatic emotion" → "Recent converging evidence compels a new, specific view of pain as a homeostatic emotion, akin to temperature, itch, hunger and thirst" (p. 303).
- PDF version: published

## 3. Leeuw et al. (2006) — The fear-avoidance model of musculoskeletal pain: current state of scientific evidence [tier: short]

**Citation:** Maaike Leeuw, Mariëlle E.J.B. Goossens, Steven J. Linton, Geert Crombez, Katja Boersma & Johan W.S. Vlaeyen, *Journal of Behavioral Medicine* 30(1): 77–94. Published online 20 Dec 2006, in the Feb 2007 issue. DOI 10.1007/s10865-006-9085-0.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Leeuw et al. 2006 - The fear-avoidance model of musculoskeletal pain - Current state of scientific evidence.pdf` (published version).

**Claim.** Support for the fear-avoidance model is growing, but not evenly across its parts. Fear links well to catastrophizing, hypervigilance and avoidance. The evidence for *disuse* is weak. Pain intensity matters more than the 2000 version allowed.

**Method / type.** Narrative review, component by component, of studies published after Vlaeyen & Linton (2000). Covers the stages of low back pain and treatment.

**Key result or argument.**
- **Updated diagram (Fig. 1, p. 79).** It merges the Vlaeyen & Linton (2000) model with Asmundson et al.'s (2004) *fear-anxiety-avoidance* model. That model removes the direct fear → avoidance link "because by definition one cannot avoid a threat that is already present". In its place is an *anxiety* pathway for anticipated pain alongside the *fear* pathway for present pain (p. 78). The authors keep the single term "pain-related fear" because the added value of the update "is not yet evident" (p. 78).
- **Revision on intensity.** "It has become clear that high pain intensity is in itself a threatening experience that drives escape and avoidance (Eccleston and Crombez, 1999)" (p. 79).
- **Attention.** Pain that is intense or threatening "demands attention and may interrupt ongoing activities" (p. 80). The disruption comes mainly from *difficulty disengaging* attention from pain, not from a faster initial shift towards it (p. 80).
- **Avoidance** is shown mostly as *submaximal performance*: slower walking, lower strength (p. 81).
- **Disuse.** "Neither lower physical activity levels nor the physical consequences of long-term avoidance behavior … were unambiguously confirmed" (p. 82).
- **Causality.** Only two studies tested the model's paths together, and those were structural equation models, "not allowing for causal inferences, which actually constitute the basic premises of the model" (p. 82). "Causality can only be established when each construct is experimentally manipulated" (p. 87).
- **Adaptiveness.** Acute fear is adaptive (citing Wall 1979). "Fear … is never dysfunctional, but it is the prolonged engagement in these safety behaviors that are dysfunctional" (p. 88).
- **Open issues.** The *object* of fear may be wider than pain, e.g. threats to identity (Morley & Eccleston 2004), using the approach- vs. avoidance-goal theory of Carver & Scheier. Fear sometimes produces *task persistence* rather than avoidance (p. 88–89).

**Place in the field's history.** The mid-point audit of the model. It keeps the core loop but concedes three weak points: intensity, disuse and causality. It also names the two ideas that the 2012 "next generation" paper builds on, goals and persistence.

**Relevance.** It records a turn within the community itself, away from "fear, not pain, drives avoidance" and towards "intense pain is itself a threat that drives escape". It also states explicitly that the model's causal claims had not yet been tested by manipulating its components. That is useful for dating when experiments like Claes et al. (2015, entry 5) became the requested standard.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2006 · clinical-psychology
- Question it asked: Since 2000, what is the evidence for each component of the fear-avoidance model and for pain-related fear at each stage of low back pain?
- Position / finding in one line: The fear → catastrophizing / hypervigilance / avoidance links are supported, disuse is not clearly confirmed, pain intensity matters more than assumed, and causal claims still await experimental manipulation.
- Responds to / builds on: Vlaeyen & Linton 2000; Vlaeyen et al. 1995; Lethem et al. 1983; Philips 1987; Waddell et al. 1993; Asmundson et al. 2004; Eccleston & Crombez 1999; Wall 1979; Morley & Eccleston 2004; Carver & Scheier 1998; Bouton 2002; Rachman 2004
- Measures (empirical only): n.a. — review, no new data
- Dissociation reported (empirical only): n.a. — review. (Reviewed: exposure in vivo improved accelerometer-measured behaviour, but education alone did not, although both reduced self-reported fear, de Jong et al. 2005b, p. 86. In complex regional pain syndrome, resumed activity went with lower fear but not lower pain intensity, which fell only later, de Jong et al. 2005a, p. 86.)
- Survey claim check: partly — "the fear-avoidance model across four iterations" → This is an evidence review, not a new formulation. It does print a revised diagram combining two earlier models: "The fear-avoidance model of chronic pain. Based on the fear-avoidance model of Vlaeyen and Linton (2000). and the fear-anxiety-avoidance model of Asmundson et al. (2004)" (Fig. 1 legend, p. 79). It does not number iterations.
- PDF version: published

## 4. Crombez et al. (2012) — Fear-avoidance model of chronic pain: the next generation [tier: short]

**Citation:** Geert Crombez, Christopher Eccleston, Stefaan Van Damme, Johan W.S. Vlaeyen & Paul Karoly, *Clinical Journal of Pain* 28(6): 475–483, 2012. DOI 10.1097/ajp.0b013e3182385392. The journal details are as cited by Claes et al. 2015, ref. 7.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Crombez et al. 2012 - Fear-avoidance model of chronic pain - The next generation.pdf`. **Author manuscript** (unformatted Word file, 35 pages, dated August 2011, no figures). Cited by manuscript page. The wording may differ from the published article.

**Claim.** The original model is well supported, but it needs a "next generation" built on a **motivational, goal-and-self-regulation perspective**. In that version, the fear-avoidant pattern "is recast as capturing the persistent but futile attempts to solve pain-related problems in order to protect and restore life goals" (abstract, ms. p. 2).

**Method / type.** Narrative review plus theoretical proposal. No new data. No diagram in the manuscript.

**Key result or argument.** The paper names three challenges (ms. p. 8–13):
1. **Beyond psychopathology.** The model came out of phobia and panic research, but its "erroneous" beliefs are "normative and culturally endorsed, rather than 'irrational'" (ms. p. 9). Catastrophizing scales measure something closer to worry and rumination. And "the FA model has underplayed the role of pain intensity. Pain is a biologically hardwired signal of bodily threat that is designed to capture attention and disrupt ongoing behavior" (ms. p. 9).
2. **Disability and recovery.** The model is an illness-beliefs model that "remains silent" on how people function despite pain. Disability, more than pain, drives people to seek care, and "recovery" is left undefined (ms. p. 10–11).
3. **Competing goals.** "The goal of avoiding pain often emerges to crowd out other competing goals" (ms. p. 11). Example: someone avoids standing for long but perseveres when cooking for friends. Goals can help each other (facilitation) or get in each other's way (interference). "Avoiders" and "confronters" may not be stable types (ms. p. 12–13).

The proposal (ms. p. 13–20) treats pain as "a fundamentally disruptive experience occurring within a context of daily goal pursuit" (ms. p. 14). It describes three responses:
- (a) **goal persistence**, ignoring pain, which can itself become harmful;
- (b) **misdirected problem-solving**, where pain relief becomes the dominant goal. The belief behind it may be that pain must be solved before life resumes, not only that pain means harm. Avoiding goal *failure* can become an "attractor";
- (c) **acceptance**: dropping unattainable goals, including the goal of pain relief, and taking up other goals.

**Place in the field's history.** This is the point where the model's own authors move the explanatory weight from *fear* to *motivation and goal conflict*. It lists the earlier extensions: Turk (2002) on post-traumatic stress, Asmundson et al. (2004) on anticipation vs. pain itself, and the trait-anxiety origins of catastrophizing (ms. p. 8). It also adopts the "pain demands attention / interrupts" view from Eccleston & Crombez (1999), a work named but not held here.

**Relevance.** For a history of "valence vs. avoidance", this is where avoidance stops being read off fear and starts being treated as the result of weighing goals against each other. This framing sets up the experimental paradigm of entry 5 and the 2016 diagram in entry 6.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2012 · clinical-psychology
- Question it asked: What must a next-generation fear-avoidance model add to handle normative beliefs, functioning and recovery, and multiple competing goals?
- Position / finding in one line: Fear-avoidance should be recast motivationally, as persistent but futile attempts to solve the pain problem in order to protect life goals, where pain-avoidance competes with other goals and may give way to persistence or acceptance.
- Responds to / builds on: Vlaeyen et al. 1995; Vlaeyen & Linton 2000; Leeuw et al. 2007 [this corpus: leeuw2006]; Lethem et al. 1983; Fordyce 1976; Philips 1987; Waddell et al. 1993; Kori et al. 1990; Turk 2002; Asmundson et al. 2004; Eccleston & Crombez 1999; Eccleston & Crombez 2007; Van Damme et al. 2008; Hasenbring & Verbunt 2010; Vlaeyen, Crombez & Linton 2009; Carver & Scheier 1981; Karoly 1993/1999; Legrain et al. 2009
- Measures (empirical only): n.a. — review/proposal, no new data
- Dissociation reported (empirical only): n.a. — no new data
- Survey claim check: partly — "the fear-avoidance model across four iterations" → The paper calls for a next generation rather than drawing a new version. It describes the lineage as a model "originally formulated by Vlaeyen et al., which has been adapted and updated" (ms. p. 4), citing Vlaeyen & Linton 2000 and Leeuw et al. 2007, plus separate extensions by other authors (ms. p. 8). No count of iterations appears.
- PDF version: author copy (unformatted Word manuscript dated 5 Aug 2011; acceptance status not stated in the PDF)

## 5. Claes, Crombez & Vlaeyen (2015) — Pain-avoidance versus reward-seeking: an experimental investigation [tier: full]

**Citation:** Nathalie Claes, Geert Crombez & Johan W.S. Vlaeyen, *Pain*, 2015. DOI 10.1097/j.pain.0000000000000116.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Claes et al. 2015 - Pain-avoidance versus reward-seeking.pdf`. **Author manuscript** (Word file, 31 pages, dated 28 Jan 2015). The file's metadata carries a different working title, "Choosing is losing: pain-avoidance goals versus reward-seeking goals". Figures sit at the end with no data labels, so the values below read off Figs. 3–4 are approximate. Cited by manuscript page. The wording and statistics may differ from the published article.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

The fear-avoidance model says frightened patients avoid movements they expect to hurt. The "next generation" proposal (entry 4) adds that avoiding pain is only one goal, and it competes with others. This study tests that idea in the lab with healthy students. Could a reward for making a painful movement reduce avoidance of it? Would it do so by reducing fear, or while leaving fear as it was? And does it matter which goal a person says is more important?

#### Key Findings

1. **People learned which movement hurt.** Participants expected shocks after the CS+ movement and not after the CS− movement. They reported more fear for the CS+, and they started it slightly more slowly.
2. **Reward did not change fear, pain intensity or unpleasantness.** Attaching lottery tickets to the painful movement left all three ratings statistically unchanged.
3. **Reward strongly changed choice.** When allowed to choose, people picked the painful movement far more often when it also earned tickets. This was the largest effect in the study.
4. **Stated goal priority moderated choice.** Those who said "avoiding pain" was most important picked the rewarded painful movement least (about 4 of 12). Those who said "earning tickets" picked it most (about 11 of 12). "Equally important" fell in between (about 9 of 12).
5. **Persistence depended on the match between goal and context.** Pain-avoiders consistently avoided when there was no reward but switched more when there was one. Reward-seekers consistently chose the painful movement when it paid, but switched more when it did not.

#### Initial Takeaway

In this experiment, *what people did* about a painful movement moved a great deal in response to a competing reward. *What they said they felt* did not move: fear of the movement, how intense the shock was, how unpleasant it was. The authors read this as evidence that avoidance is a context-dependent choice among goals, and not simply a read-out of fear.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Design

| Element | As reported |
|---|---|
| Participants | 65 healthy volunteers recruited (28 male). 8 excluded: 3 did not follow instructions; 5 said both goals were unimportant, "as we reasoned that the experimental manipulation failed". Final **N = 57** (21 male, mean age 22.26) (ms. p. 4). Paid €12. |
| Species / population | Humans; mostly undergraduate students (ms. p. 16) |
| Grouping (not manipulated) | Self-reported most important goal, chosen before acquisition in the experimental condition: **pain-avoidance N = 19**, **reward-seeking N = 21**, **equally important N = 17** (ms. p. 4–5) |
| Design | Crossover, within-subject: control vs. experimental condition. Condition order, movement plane (horizontal / vertical) and which movement is CS+ counterbalanced (ms. p. 5) |
| Paradigm | Voluntary Joystick Movement (VJM) human fear-conditioning paradigm (Meulders et al., 2011). Joystick movements are the conditioned stimuli (ms. p. 5) |
| Pain-US | Electrocutaneous: 1000 ms trains of 10 ms, 50 Hz pulses at the wrist. Intensity self-calibrated to "painful and required some effort to tolerate"; mean 12.2 mA (SD 4.6) (ms. p. 5–6, 8, 11). Participants were told intensity would vary up to that maximum; in fact it was fixed (ms. p. 9) |
| Reward-US | 2 lottery tickets per reinforced CS+, towards a self-chosen prize worth about €100. "Unknown to the participants", every participant actually had an equal chance of winning (ms. p. 6, 10) |
| Phases per condition | Acquisition: 3 blocks × (4 CS+, 4 CS−), CS+ reinforced 50%. Test: 12 CS+, 12 CS−, 50%. **Choice**: 4 blocks × 3 free-choice trials = 12, CS+ reinforced **100%** (ms. p. 9–10; Table 1). In the experimental condition the pain-US and reward-US were delivered **together** |

#### Measures

- **Self-report, 0–10 scales:** fear of performing the previous movement ("pain-related fear"), pain intensity (plus a verbal scale), pain unpleasantness, pain tolerance, pain-US expectancy, and ticket expectancy in the experimental condition (ms. p. 6–7). Ratings were collected in acquisition (1 CS+ and 1 CS− per block) and test (3 CS+ and 3 CS−) (ms. p. 9). The manuscript describes **no ratings during the choice phase.**
- **Response latency:** time from the start signal to leaving the start region (ms. p. 7).
- **Behavioural decisions:** number of CS+ choices out of 12 choice trials. Higher means less avoidance (ms. p. 7).
- **Choice switches:** number of CS+/CS− switches out of 12 choice trials. Lower means more persistence (ms. p. 7–8).
- **Questionnaires, descriptive only:** Fear of Pain Questionnaire (FPQ-III-NL), Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS), and the PANAS mood scale (Positive and Negative Affect Schedule). Groups did not differ on them (ms. p. 7, 11).

#### Analysis (as reported)

- 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA (Condition × CS type), with Group as a between-subjects factor, for ratings and latencies. Condition × Group ANOVA for choices and switches. Planned contrasts. Greenhouse–Geisser corrections. Generalized η² (η²_G) (ms. p. 11).
- Latencies below 250 ms or above 3000 ms, or more than 3 SD from the participant's cell mean, were removed (ms. p. 11).

**Reporting irregularities in the manuscript copy** (noted, not corrected; the published version may differ):
- (i) The contrast "t(54) = −1.47, p = .031" does not match a standard two-tailed test (|t| = 1.47 on 54 df gives p ≈ .15). The multiplicity correction applied is not stated. "p = 1" for a neighbouring contrast suggests some correction was applied.
- (ii) The effect-size benchmarks are given as ".2, .13, and .26" for small/medium/large (ms. p. 11). ".2" is presumably ".02".
- (iii) The Discussion says reward-preferring participants "reported less pain-related fear" (ms. p. 14). Only one of their two pairwise contrasts reached p < .05.

#### Inferential step: from result to claim

1. **Result.** Adding a reward to the painful movement left self-reported fear unchanged (no Condition effect). It greatly increased choices of that movement (η²_G = .557).
2. **Inference drawn** (ms. p. 15): "although self-reported pain-related fear remains unaltered, pursuing a competing goal may result in a diminution of observable avoidance behavior". Goal preference "seems to moderate this effect".
3. **Theoretical conclusion** (ms. p. 15): avoidance "is a dynamic response that is not only influenced by pain and associated responding, but also by contextual factors and competing goals". "Current fear-avoidance models however have difficulties explaining such findings". They would "benefit from including theories on behavioral decision making as well as goal pursuit."
4. **Limits the authors state** (ms. p. 16–17):
   - healthy student sample;
   - limited ecological validity of a monetary reward;
   - goal groups were self-selected, not manipulated, so "one should therefore be careful with making causal inferences";
   - no psychophysiological measures;
   - a latency result from their earlier study (Claes et al., 2014) did not replicate;
   - no extinction phase.
5. **Structural points for readers** (reviewer's observation, from the design as reported):
   - The fear, intensity and unpleasantness ratings come from acquisition and test, where the CS+ was reinforced 50% of the time. The choices come from the choice phase, where it was reinforced 100%. So the "fear unchanged / behaviour changed" split is between measures taken in *different phases*, not within the same trials.
   - During the choice phase the CS+ always delivered shock *and* tickets together. A painful choice therefore traded a certain shock for a certain reward. It was not a probabilistic gamble.

#### Place in the field's history

This paper takes the goal-competition idea from Crombez et al. (2012) and Van Damme et al. (2008, not held) into a controlled conditioning paradigm. It replicates and extends Claes et al. (2014), which found "a concurrent reward reduced avoidance behavior while pain-related fear remained unaltered" (ms. p. 3). It adds individual goal priority as a moderator. It cites neuroeconomic work on weighing pain against reward (Talmi et al. 2009; Vlaev et al. 2009; Roy 2010; ms. p. 3, 15) as related literature, so the clinical-psychology line is here touching decision-science.

#### Relevance

For a history of how pain's *felt badness* relates to its *push on behaviour*, the study provides a laboratory dissociation. Two things moved with a competing incentive: avoidance, measured as choice, and persistence, measured as switching. Two things did not: rated unpleasantness and intensity of the pain, and rated fear. The authors frame this as goal competition. They do not discuss what it implies for theories of pain affect, so any reading in terms of imperativism or evaluativism (Part A) belongs to the synthesis and is not the paper's claim.

## 6. Vlaeyen, Crombez & Linton (2016) — The fear-avoidance model of pain [tier: short]

**Citation:** Johan W.S. Vlaeyen, Geert Crombez & Steven J. Linton, *Pain*, 2016 ("Pain Pictured" feature). DOI 10.1097/j.pain.0000000000000574.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Vlaeyen et al. 2016 - The fear-avoidance model of pain.pdf`. **Author submission** (Word file, dated 25 Aug 2016). It has one page of text ("word count text incl. refs = 503"), an acknowledgements page, and a draft figure page carrying the note "Note for the designer: this is meant as a spiraling process". Page 4 is blank. The published graphic may be redrawn.

**Claim.** The fear-avoidance model is "a theoretical model to guide pain research and management, describing the cascade of events following pain that is perceived as threatening". It cites Vlaeyen & Linton 2012 for this (ms. p. 1). It is now presented as a *learning* model (acquisition, generalization, extinction) set inside a *goal-priority* context.

**Method / type.** Illustrated one-page summary, a visual teaching piece. No new data.

**Key result or argument.** The text has three short sections (ms. p. 1):
1. **Acquisition and generalization.** Fear is "the anticipatory emotional response to imminent threat". It is learned by direct experience, observation or verbal instruction. Cues, often interoceptive or proprioceptive, come to trigger protective responses such as avoidance. Chronic pain may develop when fear and avoidance persist after healing or spread to similar situations. Avoidance can itself signal danger ("I am avoiding, therefore there must be danger"), and relief reinforces it.
2. **Pain in context.** "Pain-related fear is not always associated with avoidance behavior … The goal to avoid pain is only one to be pursued in an environment with concomitant, often competing goals". "Fear-related protective behaviors are inhibited when the value of another life goal outweighs the value of pain, and is given priority." Negative affect and harm representations push towards pain control. Positive affect and optimism push towards valued goals.
3. **Extinction.** Exposure builds new non-threat associations, but "the extinction of pain-related fear is fragile, context-dependent, and it does not easily generalize".

The draft figure (ms. p. 3), top panel, is a spiral:
- NOCICEPTION → PAIN → *Threat: high vs. low*, which forks two ways.
- **Priority to pain control** → FEAR → AVOIDANCE → INTERFERENCE → NEGATIVE AFFECT → back to PAIN (red loop).
- **Priority to valued life goals** → APPROACH → RECOVERY (green path).

Four bottom panels: *Acquisition 1* (a neutral cue paired with a painful unconditioned stimulus, whose unconditioned responses are "interrupt, selective attention, escape"), *Acquisition 2* (the cue alone produces fear, avoidance and safety-seeking, leading to interference), *Generalization* (similar cues produce the same responses), *Extinction* (safety-seeking crossed out).

**Place in the field's history.** Compare the 2000 diagram. There the fork was catastrophizing vs. not catastrophizing (Vlaeyen & Linton 2000, Fig. 2 legend, p. 329). In 2016 the fork is *threat appraisal plus priority to pain control vs. valued life goals*. This is the goal-competition idea of Crombez et al. (2012) and the approach/avoidance language of Claes et al. (2015) built into the model's official picture. The loop now also begins at "nociception" rather than at "injury". Associative-learning terms (unconditioned stimulus/response, conditioned response, generalization, extinction) return explicitly, echoing the 1984 activity-avoidance model.

**Relevance.** Of the diagrams in this batch, this is the latest version of the model drawn by its originators. Two statements in it matter for the synthesis. Fear does not always lead to avoidance. And protective behaviour is "inhibited when the value of another life goal outweighs the value of pain". Both put *value comparison* inside a clinical model of pain behaviour.

## How the entries connect (for the synthesis)

This section is factual cross-referencing only; interpretation is left to the synthesis.

- **On the survey's "four iterations".** The survey's phrase is applied to entries 1, 3, 4 and 6. None of the four papers counts iterations.
  - Entry 1 reviews two earlier models: Linton et al. 1984 and Vlaeyen et al. 1995.
  - Entry 3 prints a merged diagram that adds Asmundson et al. 2004.
  - Entry 4 proposes a reformulation in words only.
  - Entry 6 draws a new diagram.

  A defensible reading from the papers themselves is one research programme with changing diagrams: Lethem 1983 → Vlaeyen 1995 / Vlaeyen & Linton 2000 → Leeuw 2006 → [Vlaeyen & Linton 2012, not held] → Vlaeyen, Crombez & Linton 2016. Crombez et al. 2012 is the theoretical turn between the last two diagrams.
- **Role of pain intensity.** It is minor in entry 1: performance tracked fear, not intensity (p. 322). Entry 3 revises this: intensity is "in itself a threatening experience that drives escape and avoidance" (p. 79). Entry 4 goes further: the model "underplayed" intensity, and pain is "designed to capture attention and disrupt ongoing behavior" (ms. p. 9). Entries 3 and 4 both trace this to Eccleston & Crombez (1999), not held.
- **What "avoidance" was measured as.** Entry 1: questionnaire beliefs and reduced task performance. Entry 3: submaximal performance and walking speed. Entry 5: free choice between a painful and a safe movement under an explicit trade-off.
- **What drives avoidance.** Entries 1 and 3: fear, set off by catastrophizing. Entries 4, 5 and 6: priority among competing goals, where fear can persist without avoidance.
- **Craig (entry 2)** comes from a separate community. He cites none of the fear-avoidance literature, and none of entries 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6 cites him. His separation of *feeling* (insula) from *motivation* (anterior cingulate) is the neuroanatomical proposal in this batch that most closely parallels the rating-vs-choice split in entry 5. The papers themselves draw no such link.
- **Adaptive short-term avoidance** is credited to Wall (1979), not held, by both entry 1 (p. 323) and entry 3 (p. 88).
