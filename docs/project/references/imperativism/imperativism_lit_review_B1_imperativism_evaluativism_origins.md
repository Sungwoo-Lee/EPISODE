**Topic folder:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/`

**Reviewer:** literature-reviewer

## What this batch is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question these papers share.** Pain has a sensory side (where it is, how sharp) and an *unpleasant* side — the part that makes you want it to stop. In the late 2000s philosophers who think experiences are fully explained by what they represent ("representationalists") had no good account of that unpleasant side. What could "hurting" represent?

**Two answers appeared within a few years.**
- **Imperativism — pain is a command.** Martínez (2011) proposed that the unpleasantness of pain is like an order, "Don't have this bodily disturbance!", which can be obeyed or not but is neither true nor false. He did this independently of Colin Klein (2007, reviewed in Part A), whose command was about *actions* ("don't put weight on that ankle").
- **Evaluativism — pain is a perception of badness.** Cutter & Tye (2011) argued that pain represents the damage *as bad for you, to some degree*, and that this badness is something the brain can track. Bain (2013) gave the view its name and argued that commands, unlike evaluations, cannot explain why pain gives us *good reasons* to act.

**The first counter-attack on evaluativism.** Jacobson (2013) pointed out that taking a painkiller — which removes pain but not the injury — is rational. If pain were only a report that the body is in a bad state, that would be like killing the messenger who brought bad news.

**Why it matters for the field's history.** These four papers set the terms of every later round: intensity, pleasure vs. pain, reasons, painkillers and pain asymbolia (pain without being bothered). None settles the debate; this review records each position without endorsing one. For this project's artificial agents the corresponding internal signal is called **nociception**, not pain.

## Reading conventions

- **All four PDFs are published versions**; citations use printed journal pages (Bain 2013 uses the supplement pagination S69–S89).
- **No invented mathematics.** These are philosophy papers without equations. Display blocks are used only to restate a content schema the paper itself gives in words or symbols.
- **Vocabulary.**
  - *Representationalism*: an experience's phenomenal character (what it is like) is fixed by, or supervenes on, its representational content.
  - *Indicative* content describes and can be true or false; *imperative* content commands and can be satisfied or not.
  - *Tracking / teleosemantic* theories of content: a state represents what it co-varies with under the conditions it was selected for.
  - *Evaluativism*: pain's unpleasantness is the experience representing a bodily state as bad for the subject. (The label is Bain's, 2013; Cutter & Tye 2011 do not use it.)
  - *Pain asymbolia*: a condition in which patients report pain but are not bothered by it.

## 1. Martínez (2011) — Imperative content and the painfulness of pain [tier: full]

**Citation:** Manolo Martínez, "Imperative content and the painfulness of pain," *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* 10(1): 67–90, 2011 (online 6 August 2010). doi:10.1007/s11097-010-9172-0.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Martinez 2011 - Imperative content and the painfulness of pain.pdf` — published version (Springer typeset, 24 pp.). Page citations are journal pages (journal page = PDF page + 66).

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

A popular theory of consciousness, *representationalism*, says that what an experience feels like is fixed by what it represents: feeling sand under your feet is representing sand. Pain is an awkward case for two reasons. First, the *awfulness* of pain (Martínez calls it its **painfulness**) does not seem to represent anything. Second, pain does not behave like perception: we accept that we can hallucinate a red patch, but nobody accepts a "pain hallucination" — if it seems to hurt, it hurts. Martínez's proposal is that representationalists went wrong by assuming every representation *describes* how things are. Some contents are **imperative** — like "Open the door!", they are neither true nor false but have conditions under which they are *satisfied*. The painfulness of pain, he argues, is such an imperative content, roughly "Don't have this bodily disturbance!".

#### Key Findings

1. **Rejecting the evaluative reading of Tye.** Michael Tye had proposed that pain's unpleasantness is the experience representing tissue damage *as bad* (harmful). Martínez argues that, on Tye's own causal/teleological theory of content, representing "tissue damage" and "harmful tissue damage" cannot come apart — yet pain science shows the sensory and unpleasant sides of pain can be changed independently (low-dose morphine, hypnosis).
2. **Painfulness is imperative content.** Pain's unpleasant side has *satisfaction conditions* ("I don't have this bodily disturbance"), not truth conditions. He names the view **PANIIC**: poised, abstract, non-conceptual, *imperative*, intentional content.
3. **Disagreement with Klein (2007).** Klein's imperatives command or forbid specific *actions* ("don't put weight on the ankle"). Martínez's imperative is about a *state of affairs* (the disturbance being gone), which leaves open how to achieve it. So headaches — which forbid no particular action — are unproblematic, and letting a surgeon manipulate a broken ankle is not in conflict with the pain.
4. **The puzzles dissolve.** No pain hallucinations: commands cannot be false, so the unpleasant part of a phantom-limb pain is not in error. Why we care about pain: its content makes rational the desire for the disturbance to stop — "it has the caring built in" (p. 83).
5. **Extension.** Pleasures get positive imperatives ("Have more of that!"); emotions are sketched programmatically; liked-but-unpleasant experiences (spicy food, masochism) are handled by a *second-order* imperative ("More of that experience!") about a first-order unpleasant state.

#### Initial Takeaway

This is one of the two founding statements of imperativism about pain (developed, Martínez notes, independently of Klein 2007). It sets out three lasting features of the debate: the representationalist framing, the rejection of "pain represents damage as bad" (what later becomes *evaluativism*), and an in-house disagreement among imperativists about whether pain's command targets **actions** (Klein) or **states of the body** (Martínez).

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Technical Analysis

**Target framework.** Strong PANIC representationalism (Tye): a state's phenomenal character *is* its poised, abstract, non-conceptual intentional content. Martínez's conclusion is explicitly conditional: *if* one finds PANIC attractive, the imperative strategy is the way to extend it to pain (p. 69).

**Two objection clusters against PANIC for pain.**
- *Opacity cluster* (Aydede 2006; Block 2006): perception is transparent and committal about the world; pain is not — "we do not acknowledge pain hallucinations" (Block, quoted p. 70). Tye's two-concepts reply (pain-as-experience vs. pain-as-quality) leaves unexplained *why* we care about the experience rather than the quality.
- *Painfulness*: Armstrong-style **cognitivism** (unpleasantness = a desire for the perception to cease) fails because a deviant wiring could attach such a desire to an affectively neutral perception (pp. 71–72). Tye's alternative — "To experience tissue damage as bad is to undergo an experience which represents that damage as bad" (Tye 2006a, quoted p. 72) — meets Aydede's "not representational at all" objection.

**The naturalistic argument against Tye (pp. 73–75).** Martínez spells out a chain of content theories, each written as a biconditional in the paper:

- (CAC) M has content p iff M causally correlates with p — cannot handle misrepresentation (Fodor's disjunction problem).
- (OC-CAC) … under *optimal conditions* — normative, so not yet naturalistic.
- (TSAC) M has content p if M has the biological function of detecting p, with function fixed by selection history (Ex»F).
- (OC-CAC-Unpacked) optimal conditions = those under which M's tracking p historically furthered its possessors' goals in a way that explains M's presence.

Step: damage detectors were selected *because* they tracked damage apt to harm; so optimal conditions for detecting damage are conditions where damage is apt to harm; so a state that represents damage thereby represents harmful damage. The sensory content and the "bad" content therefore cannot vary independently. But pain affect and sensation *do* dissociate (Hunt & Mantyh 2001; lower morphine doses reduce affective before sensory nociceptive responses in rats, Van der Kam et al. 2008; hypnotic modulation, Rainville et al. 1999; p. 75). Hence Tye's evaluative account is inconsistent with the dissociation evidence *given his own theory of content*.

**Imperative content schema.**

$$
\text{Painfulness}(p) \;=\; \text{!}\,[\,\text{I don't have this bodily disturbance}\,] \quad\text{(SC—Pain, p. 76)}
$$

Satisfaction condition, not truth condition. Imperative content is individuated "by its function to bring about the state of affairs represented by its satisfaction conditions" (p. 76), a function shared with desires. A **common core** for all pains (fn. 14, p. 84): "Stop that_x bodily disturbance!", with the variable's domain narrowed for pain families (thermal, mechanical, chemical).

**Naturalising imperatives (pp. 81–82).** Following Millikan (1984; content fixed by consumers), a counterpart to OC-CAC with the direction of fit reversed:

- (OC-CAC-Imperative) M has imperative content F iff M helps to make it the case that F under optimal conditions.

He flags the indeterminacy problem as unsolved here (fn. 12).

**Content and force (pp. 79–81).** Against the "force treatment" (mood is only force, sense is a moodless proposition), he cites (i) "knows that" vs. "knows whether" and "told Smith that…" vs. "told Smith to…" differing in truth conditions (Hanks 2007; Pendlebury 1986), and (ii) Russell's unity-of-the-proposition problem, where the attitude/mood binds the constituents.

#### Argument Reconstruction

**Argument 1 — Against evaluative representationalism (Tye).**
- P1. On Tye's theory, content = what a state tracks under optimal conditions, unpacked teleologically.
- P2. Damage detection was selected because it tracked harmful damage.
- P3. So representing damage and representing damage-as-harmful are not independently variable.
- P4. The sensory and affective dimensions of pain can be modulated independently (morphine, hypnosis).
- C. Painfulness is not represented harmfulness (on PANIC + OC-CAC).

**Argument 2 — For imperative content (inference to best explanation).**
- P1. Imperative contents exist (linguistic arguments; naturalisable via Millikan).
- P2. If painfulness is imperative, (a) there is no hallucination of it, since imperatives are not veridical/non-veridical; (b) pain seems non-representational because it lacks *indicative* content, while its compelling character is exactly how an imperative would seem; (c) caring is built in because the content rationalises avoidance desires; (d) the variety of unpleasant feels maps onto the variety of satisfaction conditions.
- C. The painfulness of pain is PANIIC.

**Argument 3 — Against Klein/Hall action-imperatives.**
- P1. On Klein, pain's content forbids specific uses of a body part.
- P2. Headaches, cramps and visceral pains forbid no action (Klein's own problem cases).
- P3. Broken-ankle pain makes it rational to let a traumatologist put weight on the ankle, which a content "Don't put weight on that ankle!" cannot make appropriate (p. 78); likewise oiling rather than scratching an itch.
- C. Pain's imperative targets a bodily state, not an action type.

**Replies to objections (pp. 86–88).**
- *Craved unpleasantness*: second-order imperative "More of that experience!" about a first-order unpleasant state; predicts such pleasures need higher-order capacity (he states that evidence of these in creatures without higher-order states would refute this hypothesis).
- *Imperatives rationalising desires*: a transition from imperative content p to desire q is rational if, reliably, q-satisfied ⇒ p-satisfied in context, and that reliability explains the disposition (p. 87).
- *Cognitivism strikes back*: the views differ in prediction — a pain that (abnormally) causes no avoidance desire is painful for the imperativist, painless for the cognitivist — and in what constitutes painfulness (p. 88).
- *Sensory side*: pain's sensory phenomenology is plausibly *indicative* ("There is tissue damage there") and can be hallucinated (phantom limb); opacity attaches only to painfulness.

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- The anti-Tye argument depends on a strong reading of teleosemantics (content fixed by what the tracking was selected *for*); an evaluativist can reject P3, for instance by arguing that aptness to harm is tracked by a separate, avoidance-producing state whose degree can vary independently of the sensory content. Cutter & Tye (2011, entry 2) develop a reply of this kind.
- The dissociation evidence cited is secondary and partly from rat nociception (Van der Kam et al. 2008); the paper runs no study.
- The pleasure and emotion extensions are explicitly programmatic ("should not be taken very seriously", p. 85). Barlassina & Hayward (2019, Part A) later reworked the second-order idea into a *reflexive* imperative about the experience itself.
- Unlike Klein 2007, this imperative is about a state of affairs, so it is less directly tied to action. Its account of how imperatives *rationalise* desires is only sketched ("I have no particular theory to offer", p. 87) — the gap Bain (2013, entry 3) targets.

**Emotions, pleasures and affective phenomenology in general (pp. 83–85).** Pains are not uniformly "pure negative value" (Seager 2002); variety of unpleasant feels matches variety of imperative contents. Fn. 14 Common Core schema. Programmatic emotion contents ("May that dog stay away from me!"); fn. 15 fear vs. pain of cancer (future consequences vs. occurrent disturbance). Pleasures: "Have more of that!"; fn. 16 teleosemantic orgasm content.

**Objections (pp. 86–88).** More of that pain! (second-order PANIICs; higher-order sophistication; masochistic monk and n-th order states). Imperative contents rationalising desires (reliability criterion). Cognitivism strikes back (different predictions; different constitutive basis). ## 2. Cutter & Tye (2011) — Tracking representationalism and the painfulness of pain [tier: full]

**Citation:** Brian Cutter and Michael Tye, "Tracking Representationalism and the Painfulness of Pain," *Philosophical Issues* 21 (*The Epistemology of Perception*): 90–109, 2011. doi:10.1111/j.1533-6077.2011.00199.x.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Cutter and Tye 2011 - Tracking representationalism and the painfulness of pain.pdf` — published version (Wiley download, 20 pp.). Page citations are journal pages (journal page = PDF page + 89).

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

Cutter and Tye defend the same family of theory that Martínez (entry 1) was trying to repair: *representationalism*, where what an experience feels like is fixed by what it represents, combined with a *tracking* theory of representation, where a brain state represents whatever it reliably co-varies with under the conditions it was designed for. The problem: pain states track tissue damage, so the theory seems to say pain represents only "damage of this kind at this place". That leaves out the fact that pain *hurts*. Worse, badness does not look like something a brain state could causally track. The authors' answer is that pain represents the damage **as bad for the subject, to some degree** — and that "bad for you" is a natural, trackable property (being apt to harm you).

#### Key Findings

1. **The problem, stated as two arguments.** (a) If content is exhausted by damage type and location, painfulness is left unexplained. (b) Adam Pautz's *Mild and Severe* case: two evolved communities for whom the same damage is mildly vs. severely dangerous. Their states track the same damage, so the tracking theory seems to give them the same content — yet one is in agony and the other barely bothered.
2. **The content of pain.** "There is a bodily disturbance of (physiological) type d in location l, and d is bad for A to degree x" (p. 99). Badness comes in degrees, like brightness or loudness, and it is **subject-indexed** (bad *for A*), which lets Mild's and Severe's experiences both be accurate.
3. **Badness is trackable.** Bad for you = apt to harm you; harm = hindering a teleological system's functions. The state doing the representing is a *functional* state defined by its forward-looking role of producing mild or severe **avoidance behaviour**; natural selection built creatures to token that state *because* the damage was apt to harm them.
4. **Hard cases.** A vaccine injection feels bad but is good for you now: the content concerns design conditions, not present circumstances. Two cuts of very different danger that feel the same: the experience represents them under a coarse type ("slice of the skin"), not under the dangerous fine type.
5. **Against imperativism** (Klein 2007; Martínez 2011). Imperatives cannot capture *degrees* of painfulness without adding an "urgency" dimension outside content, which breaks representationalism; and imperatives do not explain why some experiences are *positive* and others *negative*, while "represented as good/bad for me" does so directly.

#### Initial Takeaway

This paper is the standard early statement of what became called **evaluativism** about pain affect: the unpleasantness of pain is the experience representing bodily damage as bad for oneself. It also sets two challenges that imperativists answered for the next decade — *intensity* (the Mild/Severe case) and *valence symmetry* (pleasure vs. pain) — and it ties the represented badness to a state defined by avoidance behaviour, which later critics (Bain 2013; Jacobson 2013) exploit.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Technical Analysis

**Framework.** *Representationalism* here is the supervenience thesis: phenomenal character supervenes on representational content (weak representationalism, fn. 1). *Tracking Theory of Intentionality (TTI)*: "Tokens of a state S in an individual x represent that p in virtue of the fact that: under optimal conditions, x tokens S iff p, and because p" (p. 91). Optimal conditions = those the system was designed for by natural selection or ontogenetic analogues. *Tracking representationalism* = representationalism ∧ TTI.

**The content proposal.** For subject A, disturbance type d, location l, degree x:

$$
\mathrm{Content}(e_A) \;=\; \big\langle\, \exists\, d \text{ at } l \;\wedge\; d \text{ is bad for } A \text{ to degree } x \,\big\rangle
$$

- *Gradable determinable*: specific degrees of badness are determinates of badness, as specific brightnesses are of brightness (p. 98).
- *Subject-indexed*: unindexed determinates x ≠ y are contraries, so Mild's or Severe's experience would have to be falsidical; indexing to the subject allows both to be veridical, like "five feet from me" and "ten feet from you" (pp. 98–99). Fn. 8 adds a conjoined-twins variant for token-event readings.

**Naturalising "bad for A".** (6) "Necessarily, x is bad for you iff x is apt to harm you" (p. 99), with harm understood as hindering a teleological system from performing its functions (pp. 99–100).

**Satisfying TTI.** For Mild: (8) under optimal conditions Mild tokens S iff there is a disturbance d that is bad for Mild to degree x, and because of this. The value of S is "a functional state type F defined (very roughly) by its forward-looking causal role of bringing about 'mild' avoidance behavior" (p. 100); for Severe, F′ with "severe" avoidance behaviour. Selection explains why each tokens its state *because* d is (slightly/very) apt to harm. Fn. 9: S is functional rather than neural because different neural states may realise it; the unified picture is "a forward-looking functional state with a backward-looking aspect that gives the state a certain representational content" (p. 107).

**Causal relevance of non-local properties (pp. 101–102).** Objection: aptness to harm does not supervene on local physical properties, so cannot be causally relevant. Reply: semantic properties of an insult, externalist mental properties, and being-a-lion are causally relevant without local supervenience; theories of causation denying this are reduced to absurdity. In any case TTI needs only the "because", not causation specifically (Dretske's indication theory as a cousin, fn. 12).

#### Argument Reconstruction

**The anti-tracking argument (pp. 94–95).**
- (1) If TTI, pain content is exhausted by location and physiological type of a disturbance.
- (2) If so, painfulness is not explained by content.
- (3) So either content is not so exhausted, or phenomenal features cannot in general be explained by content.
- With (1): first disjunct ⇒ ¬TTI; second disjunct ⇒ ¬representationalism. The authors reject an "explanatory gap" escape: the only interesting gap concerns why there is phenomenal character at all, not why it is *this* character; conceding otherwise undermines supervenience.

**The Pautz argument (pp. 96–97).**
- (4) If TTI, possibly two experiences differ in phenomenal character but not content (Mild vs. Severe).
- (5) Representationalism: necessarily, phenomenal difference ⇒ content difference.
- (4) is equivalent to ¬(TTI ∧ representationalism). A desire-strength reply is rejected: Severe desires relief more *because* the pain feels worse (p. 96). Fn. 7 cites Pautz's appeal to a linear relation between S1 firing and reported intensity.

**The response.** Deny (1): TTI delivers valuational content once "bad for A to degree x" is identified with aptness to harm and the tracking state with an avoidance-role functional state.

**Against imperative content (§4, pp. 103–105).**
- *Degrees.* Mild and Severe share indicative content and share "Stop that bodily disturbance!"; imperativists cannot generate a content difference. Adding "urgency" ("(Please) stop…" vs. "Stop…!!!") makes character supervene on content *plus* urgency, which is inconsistent with representationalism and inelegant compared with the brightness/loudness/sweetness model.
- *Valence symmetry.* Positive experiences represent their objects as good for A (apt to benefit A). The imperativist's "continue eating that strawberry!" and "stop that damage!" are both imperatives, and "it is not at all transparent why any of these differences should be relevant to accounting for the difference in valence" (p. 105).
- They also claim §3 removes the motivation for imperativism, which they read as assuming indicative content must be exhausted by location and type.

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- The functional state that carries the value content is individuated by **avoidance behaviour**. That gives evaluativism a built-in link between felt badness and motivation, but it also invites the objection that the account's explanatory work is done by the motivational role, not by the represented value (the line Jacobson 2013 and Bain 2013 press from different sides).
- The degrees objection assumes imperatives cannot differ in content by strength. Klein & Martinez (2018, "Imperativism and pain intensity") is held in the corpus and, by its title, addresses intensity; it is outside this batch and not checked here.
- The account needs pain to represent damage under coarse types (hard case 2), a choice not independently argued.
- The paper does **not** use the word "evaluativism"; that label is applied to this view later (e.g., Bain 2013, entry 3).
- No empirical data; Pautz's S1 firing claim is cited secondhand (fn. 7).

### Appendix: Section-by-Section Backbone

**§1 Introduction (pp. 90–92).** Representationalism and naturalisation; TTI stated; tracking representationalism = the conjunction. Problem: TTI seems to restrict pain content to damage information, missing negative affect. Response: pain represents tissue damage as bad for us to some degree, predicted by TTI. Preview of §4 against imperative content.

**§2 Tissue Damage and the Painfulness of Pain (pp. 92–97).** Pain tracks disturbance type d at location l (shape, volume, intensity, mechanical/thermal/chemical). Negative affect left out. Representationalists want "represents damage as bad", but Aydede (2006) argues valuational properties cannot be tracked: molecule-identical damage need not be bad (quoted p. 93); analogy with Smith's favourite colour. Formal argument (1)–(3); rejection of explanatory-gap escape. Pautz (2010) Mild and Severe (quoted p. 96); desire-strength reply rejected; (4)–(5) show tracking representationalism inconsistent.

**§3 Tracking Representationalism Defended (pp. 97–103).** Plan: content, naturalisation of bad-for-A, causal efficacy. Degrees of badness as determinates; subject-indexing (fn. 8 conjoined case). Content formula (p. 99). (6) bad-for iff apt to harm; harm via teleological systems. (7)–(9) TTI applied; S = forward-looking functional states for mild/severe avoidance (fn. 9–10). Non-local causal relevance defended (insult, externalist desire, lion); TTI needs only "because" (fn. 12 Dretske indication). Hard case 1: needle/amputation — design conditions. Hard case 2: saphenous vein vs. adjacent cut — coarse representational type.

**§4 Concluding Remarks: Against Imperative Content (pp. 103–105).** Imperativists (Martínez 2011; Klein 2007; Hall 2008 for itch/hunger, fn. 13) assume indicative content is exhausted by location/type. Objection 1: degrees of painfulness; urgency dimension breaks representationalism and is inelegant. Objection 2: generalisation to positive affect (strawberries, orgasm) — evaluativism explains valence polarity directly, imperativism does not.

## 3. Bain (2013) — What makes pains unpleasant? [tier: full]

**Citation:** David Bain, "What makes pains unpleasant?," *Philosophical Studies* 166 (Suppl. 1): S69–S89, 2013 (online 4 December 2012). doi:10.1007/s11098-012-0049-7.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Bain 2013 - What makes pains unpleasant.pdf` — published version (Springer typeset, 21 pp.). Page citations are journal pages (S69 = PDF p. 1).

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

Bain starts from two facts about an ordinary unpleasant pain, such as stepping into a bath that is too hot. The pain is **bad for you**, and it **motivates** you to lift your foot — and it does so as a *reason*, not a mere reflex: citing the pain makes your action intelligible. Many philosophers assume that a state that merely *informs* (like a perception) cannot motivate on its own; only desires or commands can. Bain calls this assumption **broad inertness** and argues it has quietly shaped the pain debate, pushing theorists towards desire accounts and command (imperative) accounts. He argues both fail to explain why pain gives us *good* reasons, and defends an alternative he names **evaluativism**: an unpleasant pain is an experience that represents a bodily disturbance *and* represents that disturbance as bad for you.

#### Key Findings

1. **Constraints on any theory ("hedomotive claims").** Unpleasant pain is bad for its subject; it is a good (justifying) reason for avoidance behaviour; causing it is prima facie wrong; it motivates avoidance independently of further desires; it rationalises (is a motivating reason for) that behaviour. All "defeasibly".
2. **The desire view fails.** If "wanting the pain to stop" is a *mere inclination* (a disposition to act), it cannot rationalise or justify anything. Saying the inclination is itself unpleasant assumes what was to be explained.
3. **Imperativism fails** (Klein 2007, Hall 2008, Martínez 2011). Receiving a command is not in itself a reason to obey. Saying the command is "shouted" (unpleasant) is circular. Saying the pain module is a reliable guide makes the reason contingent on reliability — yet in an **evil-genius allodynia case**, where harmless caresses are agonising, the agony is still plainly a reason to stop them. And imperativism cannot explain why taking a painkiller (silencing rather than obeying the command) is rational.
4. **The dislike approach half-works.** Treating the desire as an *evaluation* ("it would be bad if this pain continued") can rationalise action, but makes the evaluation self-verifying (the pain is bad because it seems bad) and implies we find pain unpleasant *because* we dislike it, rather than the reverse.
5. **Evaluativism.** Direct the evaluation at the **body**, not the experience: the pain represents the foot's disturbance as bad for you "in the bodily sense". This evaluative layer makes the pain unpleasant, motivating and reason-giving; a pain-asymbolia patient has the disturbance representation without the evaluative layer.
6. **Defences.** Broad inertness is rejected (Smith's argument for inert beliefs does not transfer to perceptions); painkillers *silence* a reason about the foot rather than achieve the pain's aim; and the intrinsic badness of being told your body is in a bad state is compared with grief and fear, where something seeming bad to you is itself a kind of suffering.

#### Initial Takeaway

Bain gives the view its name and its most influential motivation: the question is not only *what pain represents* but *how pain can be a reason*. His objection to imperativism — commands do not by themselves give reasons, and cannot explain why silencing them with painkillers is rational — becomes a standard challenge in the literature. The "messenger-shooting" worry he raises against his own view (why is it bad to *receive* accurate bad news about one's body?) is picked up as the main objection to evaluativism by Jacobson (2013, entry 4).

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Technical Analysis

**Scope and asymbolia.** Pains are "neither essentially nor always unpleasant": asymbolics report pain without being bothered (Grahek 2007; p. S69). Bain assumes, for simplicity, that asymbolics are in pain (fn. 1). This fixes the explanandum as what distinguishes normal from asymbolic pain.

**Justifying vs. motivating reasons (p. S71).** A justifying reason is a good reason (the train is going where you need, whether or not you believe it); a motivating reason is a state capable of rationalising action (a false belief about the train). A2 requires pain's unpleasantness to be both.

**The Humean challenge (pp. S71–S73).** *Pure perceptualism*: unpleasant pain = a somatosensory perception representing a bodily disturbance (truth-apt). Almost no one accepts it, Bain suggests, because of an argument modelled on metaethics:

- Narrow Inertness Argument: moral evaluations motivate; judgements do not; so moral evaluations are not purely judgements.
- **Broad Inertness Argument**: (1) unpleasant pains are motivational states; (2) truth-apt states are not; (3) so unpleasant pains are not purely truth-apt.

Bain says he knows of no explicit statement of the broad argument before this paper (p. S73). The metaethical parallel — Hume (desires) and Hare (commands) — maps onto the desire view and imperativism.

**Four accounts compared.**

| Account | Component (ii) added to a neutral disturbance-representing experience | Truth-apt? | Directed at |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inclination approach (desire view) | disposition to act to make the pain cease | no | the experience |
| Imperativism | command from the "pain module" to stop what you are doing (Klein) / not have the disturbance (Martínez) | no | action or body |
| Dislike approach (desire as evaluation) | impression that it would be bad if the pain continued | yes | the experience |
| **Evaluativism** | the *same* experience also represents the disturbance as bad for the subject in the bodily sense | yes | the body |

(Bain notes Klein's 2007 imperativism is not a composite view, fn. 20.)

**What "bad in the bodily sense" means** is left open: apt to harm the body/impede its proper functioning (Cutter & Tye 2011, p. 99) or additionally requiring that the subject *care* about proper functioning (Helm 2002) (p. S82, fn. 31–33). No psychosemantics is given. Fn. 33 mentions Bain's forthcoming suggestion that asymbolics may lack the relevant care; fn. 35 claims evaluativism handles intensity better than imperativism (referring to Bain 2011).

**Two senses of "bad" (pp. S82–S83).** The *disturbance* is represented as bad in the bodily sense; the *pain* is thereby made bad in the experiential sense. Different objects and different senses, so no self-verification.

**Anzio soldiers (pp. S80–S81, S83).** Beecher (1959): soldiers reported less (and less unpleasant) pain than civilians with similar wounds. Hall (1989) explains this as a positive judgement about the injury reducing dislike. Bain's evaluativist re-description: the positive judgement *cognitively penetrates* the evaluative experience (the disturbance is experienced as less bad), which reduces unpleasantness, which reduces dislike. Fn. 28 notes Wall (1979) and Melzack et al. (1982) are sceptical of Beecher's explanation; Bain's claim is conditional on it.

#### Argument Reconstruction

**Argument 1 — Against the inclination approach (pp. S75–S77).**
- P1. Pain's unpleasantness rationalises and justifies avoidance.
- P2. Mere inclinations do not rationalise (Quinn's radio case: a bare disposition to turn on radios gives no perspective from which doing so seems reasonable) and are not intrinsically bad.
- C. Unpleasantness is not a mere inclination. The Lockean "uneasy desire" repair reverses the order of explanation.

**Argument 2 — Against imperativism (pp. S77–S79).**
- P1. Unpleasant pain is a justifying and motivating reason for avoidance.
- P2. Receiving a command is not, as such, a reason to obey.
- P3a. *Shouted-command reply* explains the command's force by its unpleasantness — circular.
- P3b. *Authority/reliability reply* makes the reason contingent on the module's link to damage; the evil-genius allodynia case (caresses cause agony, damage causes none) keeps the reason while removing the link.
- P4. Pain also gives reason to take painkillers, which *silence* rather than obey the command; imperativism cannot say what is bad about being told to stop.
- C. Imperativism must be rejected.

**Argument 3 — Against the dislike approach (pp. S79–S81).** Euthyphro questions:
- (A) Is pain represented as bad because it is bad, or bad because represented as bad? The dislike view is committed to the latter, making a supposedly truth-apt impression self-verifying, hence not genuinely truth-apt.
- (B) Do we dislike pain because it is unpleasant, or is it unpleasant because disliked? The view is committed to the latter, blocking the natural explanation of dislike.
- Dilemma: e-desires as evaluations are self-verifying; e-desires as non-evaluations cannot be reasons.

**Argument 4 — For evaluativism (pp. S81–S83).** Evaluative content can rationalise (radio case, revised); body-direction avoids self-verification; asymbolia = missing evaluative layer; better Anzio explanation that preserves "we dislike pain because it is unpleasant".

**Defence 1 — Broad inertness (pp. S83–S85).** Smith's direction-of-fit argument: a belief that p tends to be eliminated by a perception that not-p, so it cannot aim to make the world fit it. Substituting "perception" makes premise 1 false: a stick that looks bent is not eliminated by its feeling straight. So the argument does not establish that perceptions are inert. Ad hominem: broad inertness would also sink the dislike view. Against pure perceptualism: evaluativism handles asymbolia and supplies evaluative content.

**Defence 2 — Wrong reasons (pp. S85–S86).** Objection (a) *No bodily ends*: pain aims only at its own cessation (painkillers fully satisfy it). Reply: what terminates a motivational state need not be its aim (Wittgenstein: a punch ends a desire for an apple). Bain accepts (b) *Hedonic ends* — unpleasant pain is a good reason to end the pain — but denies (b) supports (a): the painkiller silences a reason about the foot.

**Defence 3 — Messenger-shooting (pp. S86–S87).** Why should being informed that one's foot is in a bad state be intrinsically bad, especially in a known phantom-limb case? Effects (distraction, exhaustion) are not enough (a year-long orgasm is also exhausting). Reply: grief and fear are states in which something *strikes you* as bad for you, and being in such a state is itself bad; "seems" is non-doxastic in both pain and emotion (the witting phobic parallels the witting phantom-limb patient).

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- The paper's constraints are normative (reasons, badness). Whether a theory of pain's *nature* must deliver them is itself contested; later imperativist work held in the corpus (e.g., Martínez 2015, "Pains as reasons") takes up reasons by its title, but is outside this batch and not checked here.
- The anti-imperativist allodynia case targets the *reliability* defence of commands' authority; it does not test imperativist views that ground reasons differently.
- The messenger-shooting reply rests on an analogy with grief and fear; Jacobson (2013, entry 4) argues evaluativism still cannot explain intrinsic badness.
- No empirical data. The asymbolia and Anzio evidence are secondary and, for Anzio, disputed (fn. 28).

### Appendix: Section-by-Section Backbone

Philosophers doubt indicative content can explain unpleasantness because of the belief-like reading of perceptual content and the Humean idea that informational states cannot motivate. Plan: argue against two noncognitivist views; defend an indicative *evaluative* content view.

Conclusion: evaluativism is "our most promising account of unpleasant pains" (p. S88).

## 4. Jacobson (2013) — Killing the messenger: Representationalism and the painfulness of pain [tier: full]

**Citation:** Hilla Jacobson, "Killing the Messenger: Representationalism and the Painfulness of Pain," *The Philosophical Quarterly* 63(252): 509–519, July 2013. doi:10.1111/1467-9213.12029.
**PDF:** `docs/project/references/imperativism/sources/Jacobson 2013 - Killing the messenger - Representationalism and the painfulness of pain.pdf` — published version (typeset journal article, 11 pp., downloaded from academic.oup.com). Page citations are journal pages (journal page = PDF page + 508).

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

#### Introduction

By 2011 representationalists such as Cutter and Tye (entry 2) had a ready answer to "why does pain hurt?": pain represents bodily damage *as bad for you*, and the badness it represents is why it feels bad. Jacobson's short paper argues that no answer of this kind can work. Her test case is ordinary: people take painkillers. A painkiller does nothing to the damaged tissue; it only removes the pain. Yet taking one is perfectly rational — and so is a phantom-limb patient's attempt to relieve pain in a limb she knows she does not have. If a pain were only a *report* that one's body is in a bad state, getting rid of the report without fixing the body would be like a ruler killing the messenger who brought bad news. Since taking painkillers is not irrational, painfulness cannot be just a representation of badness.

#### Key Findings

1. **A datum about reasons.** Being in pain gives a person a reason to get rid of *the pain itself*, even when the represented bodily condition is absent (phantom pain; clinical conditions where a light touch causes excruciating pain) or will not be changed (painkillers). That reason shows up as a negative attitude directed at the pain.
2. **How evaluative representations give reasons.** A state that *represents* something as bad (an evaluative belief, or a weaker "seeming") only passes on the reason carried by its object. It is "a conduit" (Stampe): its own existence adds no further reason.
3. **Conclusion.** So a representation of the body as bad can explain why one has reason to change the *body*, but not why one has reason to end *the pain* independently of the body. Representationalism "does leave out the painfulness of pain" (p. 519).
4. **Hinted positive view.** A footnote says the reasoning can be extended to show that the negative attitude constitutive of pain is **conative** (desire-like) rather than cognitive, deferred to future work (fn. 16, p. 518).

#### Initial Takeaway

This paper states the "messenger-shooting" objection to evaluativism as a stand-alone argument. The same worry appears, in the same year, as an objection that David Bain (entry 3) raises against himself and tries to answer by analogy with grief and fear; Jacobson does not cite Bain 2013 and does not discuss that reply. The objection later becomes one of the main pressure points that imperativist and hedonic accounts use against evaluativism. Jacobson herself does not argue for imperativism.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Technical Analysis

**Target.** *Strong representationalism*: an experience has its phenomenal character in virtue of its representational content, typically paired with Russellian contents built from response-independent physical properties and a naturalistic (e.g., tracking) psychosemantics (pp. 509–510). The paper also rules out the weak supervenience fallback (quoting Cutter & Tye's own argument against it, pp. 511–512) and the desire-based route ("cart before the horse", p. 512), leaving the *rich-content* route: pain represents the disturbance as noxious or bad for the subject (p. 512; Cutter & Tye 2011).

**Con-attitudes (§II, p. 513).** Mental states with an evaluative dimension: *conative* con-attitudes (desires, preferences — evaluative in virtue of mode) and *cognitive* con-attitudes (evaluative beliefs, and the valuational content representationalists attribute to pain — evaluative in virtue of content). The question: what is the *object* of the negative evaluation in pain — (1) the represented bodily disturbance, or (2) the pain / its painfulness? The representationalist says both: a first-order cognitive con-attitude towards the disturbance, plus a second-order conative con-attitude (the desire to be rid of the pain) explained by the first. Transparency (Harman; Tye; Crane) supports aversion being felt as directed at the represented condition of the hand (p. 514).

**Asymmetry between perceptual illusion and non-veridical pain (fn. 15, p. 518).** A person who knows she is hallucinating a pink elephant gets no good reason to believe an elephant is present; a person viewing the Müller-Lyer drawing is not justified in believing the lines differ. But a person who knows her pain is non-veridical still has good reason to act on it. Jacobson separates this from the familiar problem of "empty" Russellian contents: her objection applies equally to veridical pains.

#### Argument Reconstruction

**Premise-establishing argument (§III, pp. 515–516).**
- D1. Non-veridical pains (phantom pain; allodynia-type conditions) falsely represent a noxious bodily condition, and the subject may know this.
- D2. Such subjects act rationally when they try to relieve the pain.
- D3. Their reason cannot be to change the represented condition (it does not obtain).
- D4. Veridical case: painkillers act "symptomatic only", yet a person aware of this has reason to take them.
- **P (datum).** Being in pain gives a subject a reason to get rid of her pain *even without* getting rid of its intentional object, typically expressed as a con-attitude towards the pain (p. 516).

**Main argument (§IV, pp. 516–519).**
- P1. = the datum P.
- P2. The reason a cognitive evaluative attitude (belief or seeming, whatever its assertoric strength) provides is "rooted solely in the evaluable or normative features of the object which it represents" (p. 517); the attitude is transparent, a conduit, and not constitutive of the reason-providing fact.
- P3. So the existence of such an attitude provides no reason beyond those grounded in its object; "one has no reason to object to being in a state which merely represents that another state is bad" (p. 518).
- P4. Representationalism about painfulness holds that painfulness consists in a first-order cognitive con-attitude towards the bodily condition.
- C1. Then the reason to end the pain independently of the bodily condition, and the second-order con-attitude towards the pain, are "wholly inexplicable" (p. 518).
- C2. Equivalently, each painkiller would be an irrational killing of the messenger (p. 519) — contradicting P1.
- **C.** Representationalism cannot explain the painfulness of pain.

#### Critical assessment (reviewer)

- The argument targets any view on which painfulness is *exhausted* by a cognitive evaluation of the body. Bain's (2013) reply — that a state in which something *seems* bad for you can itself be bad, as in grief and fear — contests P3; Jacobson's paper does not engage it, so the exchange is unresolved within this batch.
- P2 (evaluative attitudes are pure conduits) is argued briefly, with a pointer to the author's earlier work (Jacobson-Horowitz 2006, fn. 13).
- The paper criticises representationalism generally, including rich-content versions, but says nothing about imperative-content representationalism (Klein 2007; Martínez 2011). Whether imperativism escapes the argument is not addressed.
- The positive "conative" conclusion is announced but not argued (fn. 16).
- No empirical data; clinical conditions (phantom pain, touch-evoked pain) are used as illustrations only.

### Appendix: Section-by-Section Backbone

**Abstract (p. 509).** Representationalism lacks the resources to explain painfulness; via pain's rationalising role, it would make a subject who knowingly relieves pain without changing the represented condition irrational — shooting the messenger.

**§I (pp. 509–513).** Strong representationalism and its naturalising appeal; Russellian contents of response-independent properties; "kicking the phenomenal character downstairs" (Shoemaker). Simplest pain version (bodily disturbance at a location) omits painfulness (Cutter & Tye quoted, p. 511). Supervenience fallback rejected (Cutter & Tye quoted, pp. 511–512). Desire route rejected as cart before horse (fn. 7 notes Clark's functional-drive view; fn. 8 Pautz's Mild and Severe). The live route: pains represent disturbances as noxious/bad for the subject (fn. 9 Cutter & Tye). Fn. 5: macro- vs. micro-injury versions (Bain 2003); the argument targets both.

**§II (pp. 513–514).** Conative vs. cognitive con-attitudes. Is the evaluation directed at the represented disturbance or at the pain? Representationalist picture: first-order cognitive con-attitude towards the disturbance plus second-order conative desire to be rid of the pain; on this view the painfulness and the represented noxiousness are one feature. Transparency suggests the aversion is (also) directed at the represented condition.

**§III (pp. 515–516).** Reason-giving force of non-veridical pains (touch-evoked pain conditions, phantom pain) and of painkillers. Datum: pain gives a reason to get rid of the pain even without getting rid of its intentional object, expressed as a con-attitude against the pain.

**§IV (pp. 516–519).** Cognitive evaluations — belief-like or weaker seemings; their mode does not matter — provide only object-grounded reasons (Stampe's "conduit"; fn. 13 Jacobson-Horowitz 2006). So they cannot explain the reason to end the pain itself. Fn. 15 distinguishes the argument from the empty-content problem and contrasts known illusions (no reason to believe) with known non-veridical pains (reason to act). Fn. 16: the con-attitudes are conative (deferred). Messenger-killing ruler image; conclusion.
