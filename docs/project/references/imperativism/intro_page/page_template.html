The Imperativism Debate

Why does pain feel bad? One answer says a feeling is a command. The other says it is a perception of value. This page introduces the debate through five papers.

PURPOSE

A stubbed toe does two things at once. It tells you something about your body, and it hurts. Philosophers call that second, good-or-bad quality of an experience its valence. Pain has it, and so do pleasure, fear, pride, desire and gloom. What valence actually is remains an open question.

This page introduces one live dispute about it. Imperativism says an unpleasant experience is, at bottom, a command: “don’t put weight on that ankle”, or, in its newer form, “less of this very experience!”. Evaluativism says it is a perception of value: the thing seems bad to you, the way a tomato seems red.

The page is built around Barlassina’s 2020 paper, which argues that the command view wins {{CITE:barlassina2020}}. It sets that paper beside the founding statement of imperativism about pain, the evaluativist view it attacks, the fuller theory behind it, and the evaluativist reply. The debate is not settled in these papers. Each side has a strong argument the other has not fully answered, and §6 names them.

The project cares because it studies pain-like behaviour in learning agents, and each theory says something different about what an agent’s internal damage signal would have to do to count as valence (§7).

01 The question: what makes a feeling good or bad?

A pain has two sides. One is sensory: where it is, how intense, whether it throbs or stabs. The other is affective: it is unpleasant, and you want it gone. The two can come apart. Patients on morphine, or after certain frontal-lobe surgery, sometimes say the pain is still there but they no longer mind it {{CITE:klein2007}}. In pain asymbolia, people recognise a stimulus as painful but are not distressed by it and make no move to withdraw from it {{CITE:bh2019}}. The opposite may also happen. A stroke patient whose somatosensory cortex (the region that maps bodily sensation) was damaged reported a “clearly unpleasant” feeling he wanted to avoid, while rejecting every word offered for it, from “warm” to “intense pain” {{CITE:bh2019}}. Whether this is unpleasantness with no sensation at all is contested: he still placed the feeling vaguely in his arm {{CITE:carruthers2023}}.

So what is the unpleasant part? Both sides in this debate agree on a lot. Valence is one kind of thing across pain, pleasure, emotion and desire. It runs on a shared brain network, and acetaminophen (paracetamol) dulls both physical and social pain. It also acts as a common currency that lets you compare a film with a meal {{CITE:carruthers2018}}. Their disagreement is about what kind of content a feeling carries when it feels bad.

The one distinction everything turns on

A mental state can describe (“there is damage here”, which can be true or false) or command (“stop doing that”, which can be obeyed or not). It can also be about the world or body, or about the experience itself. The three theories take different positions on these two choices.

02 Three answers

All three answers say the unpleasantness is a matter of what the experience says, not an extra raw feeling. They differ on what it says, and about what.

ImperativismKlein 2007{{CITE:klein2007}}

“Pain is there to stop us.”

A pain does not report tissue damage; it forbids a use of the body. Klein points to the telling phrase “it hurts when I do this”. How intense a pain is equals how strong the command is. Morphine does not refute the view. The drug removes the worry and the urge to act, and a prohibition with nothing to prohibit is “a stop sign in a ghost town”. Scope: pain only.

EvaluativismCarruthers 2018{{CITE:carruthers2018}} and 2023{{CITE:carruthers2023}}

Feeling bad is something seeming bad.

Valence is a perception-like representation of value that needs no concept of “bad”, the way seeing roughly thirty dots needs no concept of thirty. When you fear a bear, the bear looks bad. In pain, it is the felt bodily state that is represented as bad. By 2023 the content is spelled out as adaptive value: how good or bad something is for survival and reproduction, or has been learned to predict. Scope: all feelings.

Reflexive imperativismBarlassina & Hayward 2019{{CITE:bh2019}}

“Get more of me! Get less of me!”

Commands are the right idea, but Klein-style commands aim at the wrong target. A command about the body cannot explain why you take a painkiller, or why a gloom with no object feels bad. So an unpleasant experience commands less of itself, and a pleasant one more of itself. How to comply is left open: fix the tooth, take the painkiller, or change how you walk. Scope: all feelings.

03 How the exchange unfolded

This is not a neat back-and-forth. The early papers argue with other opponents, and the two sides meet head-on only in the last two papers.

2007
Klein founds imperativism about pain
Against the view that pain reports damage. A report would invite flexible, context-dependent responses, but for pain there is usually only one right response: stop {{CITE:klein2007}}.
2013
Bain’s evaluativist critique (not read here)
Going by its abstract only, Bain argues that command-based accounts cannot explain why an unpleasant pain gives you a reason to act, and that pain’s content must be evaluative instead {{CITE:bain2013}}.
2018
Carruthers states evaluativism for all feelings
Aimed at a different rival, the view that valence is a raw good or bad feel. It never mentions imperativism. It argues that choices are about the world, not about feelings: “evolution couldn’t care less about how one feels” {{CITE:carruthers2018}}.
2019
Barlassina & Hayward turn the command inward
Aimed at other imperativists, including Klein’s 2015 book {{CITE:klein2015}}, with one short passage against evaluativism. It also sets out the evolutionary story: feelings are a reward-and-punishment system for creatures that must learn by trial and error {{CITE:bh2019}}.
2020
Barlassina takes on Carruthers directly
The anchor paper. It judges both theories by Carruthers’s own two tests, what feelings feel like and how we decide by imagining options, and argues that imperativism passes both better (§4) {{CITE:barlassina2020}}.
2023
Carruthers replies
Valence signals track value, so they represent value. The science of learning, choice and attention has no need for commands, and in two places, where valence sits in the brain and why people stare at horrors, it contradicts them. The paper also answers most of the test cases (§5) {{CITE:carruthers2023}}.

04 The anchor paper: four cases and a pizza

Barlassina accepts Carruthers’s own standard: a theory of valence should explain what valence feels like and what it does when we decide by imagining the options {{CITE:barlassina2020}}. His first strategy is to find cases where feeling bad and something seeming bad come apart. Evaluativism treats the two as the same thing, so every such case is trouble for it.

The cake

Olivia is hungry and has left her wallet at home. The cake in the window looks wonderful, but her desire for it feels unpleasant and she would like to be rid of it. A guitarist on her way to a concert has an unfulfilled desire too, and hers feels wonderful.

Evaluativism
If desires represent their objects as good, every desire should feel pleasant. If an unfulfilled desire represents not having the thing as bad, every one should feel unpleasant. Neither fits both women.
Imperativism
What the desire is about does not fix its valence. Olivia’s desire says “less of me!”, and the guitarist’s says “more of me!”.
2023 reply
The cake still seems good. What seems bad is the situation, that she will not get it, which is felt as frustration {{CITE:carruthers2023}}.
The general and the saint

Both saved the village and both are proud, so their pride presents their deeds as good. The general’s pride feels pleasant. The saint’s feels unpleasant, because she believes she should stay humble.

Evaluativism
Even if a second state judges her pride as bad, her deeds still seem good. The theory should then predict mixed feelings, but “she is not feeling any pleasure at all”.
Imperativism
Same evaluation, opposite commands: the saint’s pride says “less of me!”.
2023 reply
She also appraises her pride as a betrayal of her values, and that appraisal is strongly negative. The reply does not take up the point about mixed feelings {{CITE:carruthers2023}}.
The dentist

Heavily anaesthetised, Barlassina sees his wrecked mouth in the mirror. It looks bad, yet the experience feels neither pleasant nor unpleasant.

Evaluativism
The experience represents the mouth as bad, so evaluativism predicts it should feel bad.
Imperativism
The drug stops the experience from acquiring any “more / less of me” command.
2023 reply
Seeing damage is recognising it, a matter of concepts, or at most seeing something that implies damage. Neither is the concept-free sense of value that evaluativism identifies with valence {{CITE:carruthers2023}}.
Bonjour tristesse

Jean-Paul wakes up miserable. Nothing in particular seems bad to him, yet the misery is unpleasant and he wants it gone.

Evaluativism
There is no object for the misery to represent as bad.
Imperativism
“Less of me!” with nothing else attached is pure misery.
2023 reply
Moods lack an identifiable cause, not an object: the world looks flat, and “the fact that nothing seems good seems bad”. The reply is made about pure moods in general. Jean-Paul is not named {{CITE:carruthers2023}}.

Deciding by imagining: tasting pizza, or pizza?

Both theories agree on the outline. To choose between pizza and pasta you imagine each, each image gives off some pleasantness, and you pick the more pleasant one. They disagree about what you imagine. Barlassina says you imagine tasting pizza, an experience. Carruthers says you imagine the pizza, a worldly outcome.

Barlassina offers three lines of evidence for the experience reading {{CITE:barlassina2020}}. Forecasting how things will feel uses the brain’s default network, which also supports memory and imagining experiences. The main value-comparison region is recruited when people imagine things from their own point of view. And projection bias: current hunger changes what people choose to eat later, which is what you would expect if imagining a taste re-uses the real tasting system.

Carruthers replies that everyone agrees imagining uses sensory imagery. What gets appraised is what the image shows, not the imagining as an experience {{CITE:carruthers2023}}. He does not address the projection-bias evidence, or the two turned-around objections below.

He then turns Carruthers’s two objections around. Why would anyone die for a cause if choices aim at experiences? Because people who make that sacrifice typically feel fused with their group, so they can imagine going on through it. Why would evolution care about feelings? Because evolution tinkers with what exists, and human future-thinking already works by simulating experiences. Neither of these two replies is answered in the 2023 paper.

05 The reply: value is enough

Carruthers’s 2023 paper does more than defend. Its main move shifts the burden of proof onto imperativism {{CITE:carruthers2023}}.

Valence carries information about value, so it represents value.
Pleasure and pain are triggered by things that were good or bad for survival, such as food when hungry or actual or threatened tissue damage, and by things learned to predict them. After food poisoning, chicken becomes disgusting. Valence does its jobs because it carries that information. On standard theories of mental content, that makes it a representation of value. At most, imperativists can add a command on top, a pushmi-pullyu state that both describes and directs. They owe an argument that the command part is needed: “Otherwise evaluativism gets to win by default.”
The science does not use commands.
Learning from surprise can be told entirely as updating stored values. Economic models of choice weigh value, probability and effort. Imperativists can re-describe all of this in terms of command strength, but nothing requires them to.
Commands would sit in the wrong part of the brain.
A command to act should live near motor planning. Valence signals are found in value areas, including the striatum, amygdala, insula and orbitofrontal cortex, separate from the networks that plan and hold goals.
We stare at horrible things.
“I couldn’t help looking, it was so horrible.” If seeing a gruesome accident commanded “less of this experience!”, people would look away. Evaluativism predicts that bad things grab attention because they matter.
The view slides into hedonism.
If valence drives every choice and valence is about one’s own experiences, then every choice aims at getting experiences for oneself. That is motivational hedonism, which the 2018 paper argued against.

06 Where it stands

Within these five papers the debate is open. The table sets the strongest objection to each view, as the review of the corpus judges it, beside what remains unanswered.

EvaluativismFeeling and evaluation come apart: the saint, objectless misery. Each case needs its own extra appraisal, which costs the unity the view claims. Is the disagreement partly about which theory of content to use?
Both sides fix content by what a signal is for. Carruthers says valence is for carrying information about value. Barlassina and Hayward say it is for regulating one’s own experience. Neither paper settles which role explains why the signal exists.
Two sources the corpus leans on were not read.
Several objections Carruthers answers come from a Barlassina & Hayward paper outside this set {{CITE:loopy2019}}, so his versions of them could not be checked. The two sides also read Klein’s 2015 book differently: as a theory of pain’s badness, or only of its sensory side {{CITE:klein2015}}.
What to read next.
Bain’s evaluativist critique of pain imperativism {{CITE:bain2013}}, and Martínez & Klein’s argument that pain signals are mostly imperative, framed in terms of how biological signals work {{CITE:mk2016}}.

07 Why it matters for this project

Interpretive, not a finding

These papers are about conscious experience. Nothing here shows that an artificial agent has valence. For the project’s agents, the observation channel that reports injury is called nociception. “Pain” is what the project is trying to explain, and it is never used as a name for the agent’s signal.

The project trains reinforcement-learning agents (agents that learn by trial and error from reward and penalty) in a grid world where they can be injured and receive a nociception signal. It studies behaviour that looks pain-like, such as staying hidden after an injury. Read functionally, each theory gives a different answer to what that signal would have to do to count as valence:

Klein
A signal that forbids particular actions. It grows while the action continues, fades when the agent backs off, and shows no effect when the agent is not trying to act. A number that merely stands for how much damage there is would not be enough. The project’s nociception channel is such a number, so on Klein’s view it is a report, not a pain-like command. Anything command-like would have to show up in how the agent’s actions are restricted, not in the channel itself.
Reflexive
A second component, distinct from the damage read-out, whose job is to reduce the whole state it belongs to, leaving the method to learning. The nociception channel on its own would be only the read-out half. The signature would be an agent drawn to lower that read-out even when doing so does not repair the damage, the analogue of taking a painkiller. There is a caution. In an engineered agent, what the signal is “for” is whatever the designer chose to reward, so meeting the letter of the theory proves little.
Evaluativism
A graded estimate of how good or bad a state of body or world is for survival, part built in and part learned, used to compare options and updated by surprise. The nearest analogue is a fixed cost for damage plus a learned value estimate over states that predict it, with the caveat that an agent’s value estimate tracks whatever reward its designer chose, not survival as such. On this reading, staying hidden after injury would be a learned re-valuation of dangerous states.

One warning from the corpus: the same learning machinery can be described either as updating values or as updating commands {{CITE:carruthers2023}}. A standard learning agent therefore favours neither theory by itself. The theories come apart only on dissociations, and each one separates a different pair. Signal without valence, as in asymbolia, and lowering the signal rather than the damage, as with painkillers, tell against Klein’s body-directed command but fit both of the other two. Valence with no object, as in objectless misery, is contested between them. Only the last, whether strongly bad states pull attention in or push it away, sets reflexive imperativism and evaluativism directly against each other.

The project has touched this question once already. An earlier analysis of bush hiding asked whether the nociception signal behaves like a description or a Klein-style command. It found evidence pointing both ways and did not treat either strand as decisive. That discussion is in docs/experiments/active/trajectory_factors/a01_hiding_drivers.html. Whether any of these dissociations can be tested here, and whether a positive result would license the word “pain-like”, is a question of construct validity (does the measurement capture the concept it is named for) for the project’s pain-science reviewer, and of priority for its research triage. This page does not settle it.

08 Glossary

valence
The pleasant-or-unpleasant quality of an experience. Barlassina & Hayward call it affective phenomenal character.
sensory / affective
The two sides of a pain: what and where it is, and how bad it feels.
content
What a mental state says or is about.
describe / command
Content that can be true or false (indicative) versus content that can be obeyed or not (imperative).
imperativism
Valence is imperative content. Klein’s version: commands about using the body.
reflexive imperativism
The command is about the experience itself: “more / less of me!”. In the 2020 paper “me” means that type of experience, so future experiences of the same kind can satisfy it.
evaluativism
Valence is a concept-free representation of good or bad. For Carruthers in 2023, of adaptive value.
adaptive value
How beneficial or harmful something is for survival and reproduction, or has been learned to predict.
pain asymbolia
A condition in which people recognise a stimulus as painful but are not distressed by it and make no move to withdraw.
pushmi-pullyu
A state that describes and directs at once, a term from Millikan.
projection bias
Letting how you feel now, for example hungry, colour predictions of how you will feel later.
identity fusion
A felt oneness with a group strong enough that its survival feels like one’s own.
motivational hedonism
The view that every choice ultimately aims at one’s own pleasant experiences.
nociception
In this project, the observation channel that reports the agent’s injury to it. In pain science the term means the nervous system’s encoding of actually or potentially damaging stimuli; the project’s use is looser. Never called “pain”.
