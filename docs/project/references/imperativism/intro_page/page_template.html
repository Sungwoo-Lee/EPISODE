Pain Beyond Sensation

How six research communities have tried to explain why pain feels bad and pushes us to act: how the ideas developed, what was argued over, and where each question stands now.

PURPOSE

Pain tells you where and how strongly something hurts. It also feels bad, and it pushes you to do something about it: pull away, protect the injury, take a painkiller. This page is a history of research on that second side of pain. It follows philosophers, brain-imaging researchers, animal-circuit neuroscientists, clinical psychologists, neurologists and computational modellers from the 1960s to 2026.

It is a review of a field, not an argument for a theory. It is built from {{N_REFS}} works, {{N_READ}} of them read from the PDF, and it sets each debate's positions side by side at their strongest. The headline is that most of the questions are still open. Philosophers still disagree whether pain's badness is a command ("less of this!") or a perception of value ("this is bad for me"). In small human experiments, most rated at high or unclear risk of bias by a 2019 systematic review, how unpleasant pain feels has shifted more easily than how strong it feels, but the two ratings stay closely linked. And none of the {{N_READ}} studies read here measured how unpleasant a person's own pain felt and how much they avoided it on the same trials.

The page began with one paper on imperativism, the view that feelings are commands {{CITE:barlassina2020}}, and grew from the reference list of a survey written for a specific study design. That origin tilts the collection towards imperativism, and the page says where.

How to read this page

§1–§2 give the whole field at a glance and its five eras. §3 shows how pain has been divided into parts. §4 maps the eight debates. §5–§6 say what the evidence can and cannot show and how the communities connect. §7 is the current status on one screen. The last sections check the survey the collection started from, list what is missing, and give the reference list. Click any figure to open it full size.

Colour. On the figures, colour and marker shape mean research community, and a filled mark means the work was read, an open mark that it is only named. Figure 5 alone uses blue for commands and orange for evaluations. Because hues carry data, nothing else on the page is coloured.

Scope. Every count, status and "no study" statement describes this collection of {{N_REFS}} works, not the whole literature.

01 The field at a glance

Before the details, the shape. The earliest work in the collection is a 1962 neurosurgery report and the latest a 2026 philosophy paper. Philosophy enters the collection late, in 2007, and then becomes one of the densest lanes. Animal-circuit and computational work are thin here, which reflects how the collection was built as much as the size of those literatures.

02 Five eras

The synthesis divides the history where the questions being asked changed. The boundaries are approximate, and each era is labelled by its most distinctive new question, which makes the 2007 and 2020 labels lean philosophical even though most papers in those eras come from other communities.

1962–
1989
Clinical dissociations and the gate
Pain stops being a fixed line from receptor to brain. The gate-control theory proposes that signals are modulated in the spinal cord and from the brain, and speaks of pain's "sensory and affective components" {{CITE:melzackwall1965}}. Surgery reports describe patients who still feel pain but are no longer bothered by it {{CITE:melzackwall1965}}. Cingulotomy and asymbolia reports, known here only through later papers, become the template for evidence that pain's parts come apart {{CITE:foltz1962}}{{CITE:berthier1988}}. The roots go further back, to Sherrington's "imperative protective reflex" and Beecher's wounded soldiers of the 1950s, which sit outside the collection.
1990–
2006
Mapping the dimensions
Brain imaging looks for separate homes for how strong pain is and how bad it feels. Hypnotic suggestion changes unpleasantness and anterior cingulate activity while the body-map cortex does not follow {{CITE:rainville1997}}; the reverse experiment is messier {{CITE:hofbauer2001}}. A review turns this into a serial model {{CITE:price2000}}. In rats, lesions of the front of the cingulate abolish learned avoidance of a place where pain happened {{CITE:johansen2001}}. The clinical fear-avoidance model says fear, not pain intensity, predicts disability {{CITE:vlaeyen2000}}.
2007–
2013
Pain's badness and pull on behaviour become explicit problems
Philosophy founds two camps. Imperativism says pain commands {{CITE:klein2007}}{{CITE:martinez2011}}; evaluativism, named in 2013, says pain represents something as bad {{CITE:cuttertye2011}}{{CITE:bain2013}}. Each side argues the other cannot explain why we take painkillers {{CITE:bain2013}}{{CITE:jacobson2013}}. Neuroscience puts pain beside pleasure {{CITE:leknestracey2008}} and finds midcingulate pain responses tied to control and action {{CITE:shackman2011}}{{CITE:perini2013}}. Clinical psychology recasts avoidance as a conflict of goals {{CITE:crombez2012}}.
2014–
2019
Elaboration, formal models and the turn to valence
The densest era. Imperativism gets books and formal models {{CITE:klein2015}}{{CITE:mk2016}}; the philosophical question widens from pain to all good and bad feelings {{CITE:carruthers2018}}{{CITE:bh2019}}. Pain is framed as a reinforcement signal {{CITE:seymour2019}}. One experiment moves avoidance choices without significantly changing ratings {{CITE:claes2015}}, and silencing an amygdala cell group in mice reduced protective and escape behaviour but not reflexes, which its authors read as pain's unpleasantness {{CITE:corder2019}}. A systematic review audits the human evidence for separable dimensions and leans towards pain as one experience {{CITE:talbot2019}}.
2020–
2026
Revised definition, valence across all feelings, graded dissociations
The revised definition of pain appears in 2020 (known here only by name). Philosophers argue over how to read learning and decision science {{CITE:carruthers2023}}{{CITE:mb2026}}, and over whether asymbolia is pain at all {{CITE:griffithkind2023}}{{CITE:duvalklein2025}}. One human imaging study reports a graded, not clean, difference between intensity and unpleasantness {{CITE:stankewitz2023}}. Two learning studies disagree on whether people learn more from pain received or pain avoided {{CITE:jepma2022}}{{CITE:le2024}}.

03 How pain has been divided

Almost every account splits pain into parts, but the parts keep changing. The 1968 model, as later papers describe it, named three: a sensory, a motivational-affective and a cognitive-evaluative part. Later imaging work mostly measured two, intensity and unpleasantness. Philosophy re-cut the non-sensory part as a command or an evaluation, and a 2024 study recast it as signed valence shared with pleasure {{CITE:lee2024}}. A 2019 review questioned whether the parts can be separated at all {{CITE:talbot2019}}.

04 Eight debates

Eight questions cut across the eras and communities. Each status uses one scale and describes this collection. Open: positions still in dispute and new work still arriving. Leaning: papers converge in a named direction, with dissent or missing causal tests. Stalled: no new evidence arriving; the debate moves only by reinterpreting old evidence. Settled: reviewed papers on all sides accept the claim. Not yet tested: the question is posed, but no reviewed study measures what would decide it. No debate here has either of those two.

What pain's badness is

Three debates are mostly philosophical, though they lean on clinical cases. The map below shows each debate's positions as rows and who critiqued or replied to whom as arrows.

What is the non-sensory component: evaluation, command, or something else?
open

When pain feels bad, is that feeling a command ("don't use that", "less of me"), a perception that something is bad, a desire, or something else? Imperativists split into body-directed {{CITE:klein2007}}, bodily-state {{CITE:martinez2011}}, reflexive {{CITE:bh2019}} and relational {{CITE:kauppinen2021}} versions. Evaluativists hold that pain represents bodily badness {{CITE:cuttertye2011}}{{CITE:bain2017}}, or, for every feeling, adaptive value {{CITE:carruthers2023}}. The latest exchange in the collection is a 2026 reply to Carruthers {{CITE:mb2026}}, which leaves his attention and hedonism arguments unanswered. All of this evidence is second-hand: none of these papers collects data.

Corpus limit: imperativist authors wrote twice as many reviewed papers as evaluativists, and evaluativist replies such as Cutter & Tye 2014, Bain 2014 and Jacobson 2019 are only known by name.

Why take painkillers? The direction of pain's motivation
open

A painkiller removes the feeling but not the injury. Commands seem unable to explain that {{CITE:bain2013}}; evaluations seem to make it "killing the messenger" {{CITE:jacobson2013}}. Answers include relief-seeking as avoiding "spammy" requests {{CITE:martinez2015}}, separate desires for relief {{CITE:bain2019}}, relief-seeking as built into the feeling {{CITE:loopy2019}}, and a separate displeasure at feeling bad {{CITE:kauppinen2021}}. Carruthers charges that experience-directed views make every choice about one's own experiences {{CITE:carruthers2023}}.

Corpus limit: the replies written directly on this question, Cutter & Tye 2014 and Jacobson 2019, are not in the collection.

Is asymbolic pain pain, and what does it show?
stalled

Some brain-lesion patients recognise a pinprick as painful yet show no distress and make no move to withdraw. The standard reading is pain without painfulness. Klein argued instead that they lost the capacity to care about their body {{CITE:klein2015asym}}; Griffith and Kind that the record does not show it is pain {{CITE:griffithkind2023}}; Duval and Klein replied that it is probably still pain and the real question is how to classify it {{CITE:duvalklein2025}}. All three agree the clinical record is small, old and confounded by language problems.

Why "stalled": the philosophical exchange was active in 2023–2025, but no clinical data newer than 1999 enter the collection. Grahek 2007 and Bain 2014, central to the exchange, are only known by name.

Whether pain's parts can be separated

Three debates are mostly empirical and cross human imaging, animal circuits and clinical work.

How many components does pain have, and can they be moved separately?
open

Are "how strong" and "how bad" two parts of pain or two descriptions of one experience? Hypnosis moved unpleasantness while intensity did not change significantly {{CITE:rainville1997}}; a review read that as a serial chain, intensity then unpleasantness {{CITE:price2000}}. The reverse experiment moved both {{CITE:hofbauer2001}}. A 2019 systematic review read the same asymmetry as intensity not being movable on its own, and leaned toward one experience {{CITE:talbot2019}}. A 2023 imaging study, without any manipulation, found a graded difference and wrote "we can not assume distinct processes" {{CITE:stankewitz2023}}.

Corpus limit: the 2019 review covered psychological manipulations only; drug and lesion evidence enters through a few small separate studies.

Is felt unpleasantness separable from avoidance and motivation?
open

Is how bad pain feels the same thing as the urge to get away from it? Animal studies infer unpleasantness from avoidance and never measure it separately {{CITE:johansen2001}}{{CITE:corder2019}}. Two human experiments raised avoidance with a reward while the pain ratings they collected did not track the behaviour {{CITE:claes2015}}{{CITE:flury2025}}, but ratings came from other phases or occasional probe trials on mild heat, and the 2025 authors decline to conclude the two were separately modulated. A pathway study moved reflex and avoidance together {{CITE:lee2022}}.

In the 2025 study reward did shift unpleasantness slightly across both phases (p = .027), which its authors do not read as separate modulation. Corpus limit: placebo and desire-for-relief research, which did rate desire beside pain, is largely outside the collection.

Where does pain meet action in cortex?
open

When pain activates the cingulate cortex, is that the feeling of badness, general control, or getting ready to move? One reading makes it the seat of unpleasantness {{CITE:rainville1997}}; another an integration hub for pain, negative feeling and control {{CITE:shackman2011}}; a third finds midcingulate pain responses only when an action is made {{CITE:perini2013}}, extended to people with fewer pain fibres {{CITE:perini2020}}. A preregistered test of action effects came out null, with support only from exploratory contrasts {{CITE:koppel2022}}.

Corpus limit: the action-dependence line is mostly one lab's studies, and counter-evidence such as Kragel et al. 2018 is known here only by name. "ACC" also names different subregions across papers.

Pain, learning and avoidance

That painful events teach animals and people what to avoid is textbook conditioning and is not disputed here. The two debates are about what exactly does the teaching, and what drives avoidance in chronic pain.

Is pain's felt badness the learning signal, and how is learning from pain structured?
open

Stimulating the rat anterior cingulate taught avoidance, and its authors called it an "aversive teaching signal", while calling the evidence "not conclusive" {{CITE:johansen2004}}. A theoretical review argues pain is a reinforcement and control signal and that behaviour should be its ultimate measure {{CITE:seymour2019}}. One study found people learn faster from pain received {{CITE:jepma2022}}; another faster from pain avoided {{CITE:le2024}}; neither discusses the other result. Philosophers read the same learning models as value updating {{CITE:carruthers2023}} or as commands {{CITE:mb2026}}.

Corpus limit: the reviewed computational papers were selected for this framing, and none of their experiments rated pain during avoidance.

What drives pain-related avoidance and disability: fear, pain itself, or competing goals?
leaning · toward fear plus goal competition

The fear-avoidance model held that fear, not pain intensity, predicts disability {{CITE:vlaeyen2000}}. An evidence audit found intense pain is itself threatening and the causal claims untested {{CITE:leeuw2006}}. The model was then recast as a conflict between avoiding pain and pursuing other goals {{CITE:crombez2012}}, tested in one lab experiment with self-selected goal groups {{CITE:claes2015}}, and redrawn with a goal-priority fork that keeps the fear loop {{CITE:vlaeyen2016}}.

Corpus limit: only the originators' research programme is reviewed; rival clinical models such as endurance responses are only named.

Gaps that recur

  • Own unpleasantness and avoidance on the same trials: not measured in any of the {{N_READ}} papers read.
  • Desire for relief beside unpleasantness: not measured in the papers read, but this is a gap in the collection, not in the field. Placebo research did rate desire for relief {{CITE:vase2003}}, and that strand is only named here.
  • Rating order: intensity was always rated before unpleasantness where the order is stated, and no study discusses order effects.
  • Animal unpleasantness is never measured apart from avoidance or protective behaviour.
  • Small samples: 8 and 10 people in the founding hypnosis studies; 15 in a pain-avoidance learning study {{CITE:wang2018}}.

06 How the communities connect

The communities ask versions of the same questions, but in this collection they rarely engage each other. Most recorded links stay inside one community, and among the 31 non-philosophy papers read that appeared after philosophy entered the collection in 2007, none is recorded engaging a philosophy paper. A few bridges exist: animal work motivated by the clinical fear-avoidance model {{CITE:lee2022}}, and the 1997 hypnosis study as a shared anchor for human and animal research {{CITE:rainville1997}}.

10 Why it matters for this project

Interpretive, not a finding

Nothing in these papers shows that an artificial agent has pain or valence. For the project's agents, the observation channel that reports injury is called nociception, and pain is what the project is trying to explain. Turning any point below into an experiment is a question for the project's pain-science reviewer and its research triage.

The project trains reinforcement-learning agents (agents that learn by trial and error from reward and penalty) that receive a nociception signal, and studies behaviour that looks pain-like, such as what the project calls hypervigilance after injury. That label names a behaviour pattern, not the clinical attention construct. The history bears on how such behaviour can be described in four ways.

  • The agent is in the animal's position. In animals, "unpleasantness" is often avoidance under another name, and behaviour alone cannot tell a command reading from a value reading or plain learned avoidance. The one affect-specific animal readout, avoidance with a spared reflex, has no agent analogue unless the agent has something like a reflex.
  • Theories come apart on dissociations: signal without concern (asymbolia), lowering the signal instead of repairing the damage (painkillers), avoidance changing while ratings do not, and reflex against learned avoidance.
  • There is no single human benchmark for learning from damage received versus avoided: the two human studies disagree {{CITE:jepma2022}}{{CITE:le2024}}.
  • "Behaviour should measure pain" {{CITE:seymour2019}} is a position in an open debate, not a settled method.

11 Glossary

sensory-discriminative
Where pain is, what it is like and how strong; usually an intensity rating.
affective
How bad or unpleasant pain feels; usually an unpleasantness rating.
motivational
The drive to act: escape, avoid, protect. Often fused with "affective" in the label "affective-motivational".
valence
The good-or-bad quality shared by pains, pleasures, emotions and moods.
nociception
The nervous system's encoding of damaging or potentially damaging stimuli, which is not the same as pain. In this project, the agent's injury-reporting channel.
imperativism
The view that pain's badness is a command. Versions differ on the command's target: the body, a bodily state, the experience itself, or the experience in relation to what one cares about.
evaluativism
The view that pain's unpleasantness represents something as bad for the subject.
pain asymbolia
A rare lesion condition: a stimulus is recognised as painful but causes no distress and no withdrawal.
reflex / nocifensive
A reflex is a fast spinal withdrawal; nocifensive behaviour, such as licking or guarding, is organised in the brain.
conditioned place avoidance
An animal learns to avoid a place where it was hurt; the standard rodent readout of pain's affective side.
ACC / aMCC
Anterior cingulate cortex; anterior midcingulate cortex. The same labels cover different subregions across papers.
S1
Primary somatosensory cortex, the brain's body map.
PAG
Periaqueductal gray, a midbrain hub for defence and pain modulation.
fear-avoidance model
Clinical model in which catastrophic thoughts about pain lead to fear, avoidance and disability.
equivalence test
A test that can show an effect is too small to matter, which a non-significant result alone cannot.
named only
On this page: a work whose PDF could not be obtained, so it was not reviewed.
