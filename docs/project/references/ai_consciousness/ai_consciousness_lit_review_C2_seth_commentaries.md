# AI consciousness — Part C2: open peer commentaries on Seth (2025), e328–e340

## What this part covers (plain-language entry point)

Anil Seth's target article in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* (BBS) argues for **biological naturalism**: the view that consciousness (having experiences at all, "something it is like" to be you) may depend on being a living system, so today's AI, which runs on ordinary digital computers, is unlikely to be conscious. Its main opponent is **computational functionalism**: the view that running the right computation is enough for consciousness, whatever the hardware.

BBS prints short replies ("open peer commentaries") from other researchers. This part reviews thirteen of them, numbered e328 to e340. They split roughly three ways:

- **Agree with Seth's conclusion, but want a different or narrower basis** (Feinberg & Mallatt; Jablonka et al.; Friston; Hohwy; Godfrey-Smith; Evers & Farisco on ethics).
- **Reject or weaken his case** that biology is necessary (Dołęga & Cleeremans; De Brigard; Dung; Fleming & Shea).
- **Reframe the question itself** (Clark: drop the "what it is like" target; Fields & Glazebrook: ask what a system *could be aware of*; Gomez-Marin: question naturalism as a whole).

Seth's own reply to these commentaries is reviewed in Part D. The Seth preprint itself is reviewed in the sibling topic `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/` (Part C §5); this part does not repeat that review.

**Source and method.** All entries come from `sources/Seth 2025 - Conscious artificial intelligence and biological naturalism (BBS with commentaries and response).pdf`, PDF pages 39–56 (page 57 holds only the next batch's commentary, Kleiner e341). The Read tool could not render this PDF in this environment, so the text of exactly pages 39–57 was extracted with `pypdf`; quotes are copied from that extraction. Page numbers (p. N) are PDF page positions, which here equal the journal's printed page numbers. Section tags (§) follow the section list in the shared brief; where a commentator's wording does not let me pin a section, I say so.

## e328. Clark (2026) — Why Seth (and everyone else) ought to be an illusionist [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `clark2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 39–40 · **Field:** philosophy of mind / cognitive science (University of Sussex)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §1 (Seth's split between intelligence, "what a system can do", and consciousness, "what it is like"); §4.4 (the "real problem" of consciousness); indirectly §3.1 and §3.6.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — Clark accepts that Seth's conclusion about living organisation "might still obtain", but rejects the way Seth frames the target of explanation.
- **The argument in plain words:** Seth defines consciousness through Nagel's "what it is like to be" a system, and sets it apart from intelligence. Clark follows the **illusionists** (Dennett, Frankish): people who hold that experience has no special "intrinsic" properties (qualia) beyond everything a creature can discriminate, report and react to. On that view consciousness is a large bundle of reactive dispositions, so there is no hard problem. This rules out a total failure of functionalism in general, but it still allows computational functionalism to fail: the needed response profile might only arise in nested, living-like systems. Clark sees a tension in Seth: the "what it is like" definition points to something beyond any response profile, while Seth's "real problem" programme (explain, predict and control properties of experience) is fully about testable profiles. Clark says the "what it is like" notion breeds zombie scenarios that resist science. The remaining task is the **meta-problem** (Chalmers 2018): explaining why experience *seems* so puzzling to us.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** It argues that Seth's own "real problem" method is already illusionist in spirit. It replaces a yes/no question about consciousness with a high-dimensional space of capacities in which animals (and by extension machines) occupy different positions, often with no simple "fact of the matter".
- **Key quotes:**
  - "If the illusionist is right, then consciousness is not special. It is just a label for a large and complex set of reactive dispositions." (p. 39)
  - "This is an illusionist-friendly picture of the scientific study of consciousness." (p. 39)
  - "Seth should join the (badly named) illusionists!" (p. 40)
- **Builds on:** Dennett 1991, 1998, 2016; Frankish 2012, 2019; Chalmers 1996, 2018 (meta-problem); Clark 2019; Clark, Friston & Wilkinson 2019.

## e329. Dołęga & Cleeremans (2026) — Meatless minds vs. beast machines: Are simulated subjects impossible? [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `dolega2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 40–41 · **Field:** philosophy of mind (Ruhr-Universität Bochum) and cognitive psychology / consciousness science (Université libre de Bruxelles)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.7 (simulation, implementation and realisation; Seth's claim that simulations "lack the causal powers and intrinsic properties" of what they simulate); §3.3 (footnote 22, on begging the question); §5.5 (which they read as treating intrinsic properties as reducible).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** rejects (the specific claim that simulations cannot in principle realise consciousness) — they say Seth has not shown impossibility, though they do not claim to show possibility.
- **The argument in plain words:** Seth separates *simulating* a process from *realising* it: a simulated storm does not get anything wet. The authors agree a simulated storm cannot sink a real ship, but note it can sink simulated ships. If consciousness has causal effects, they are effects on behaviour and on cognitive states. Most cognitive scientists accept that behaviour and cognitive functions can be simulated, and large language models already change real people's beliefs. So why could a simulated agent not have conscious states with the right causal powers over its own simulated cognition? Seth cannot simply assume that these powers need biophysical causes without begging the question against computational functionalism. On **intrinsic properties** (qualia), Seth faces a dilemma. If they reduce to familiar processes, simulating those processes should be enough. If they are irreducible (as Searle held), Seth inherits Searle's known problems and undercuts a naturalistic science of consciousness.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** It turns the simulation/realisation distinction into a dilemma about the reducibility of qualia. It points out that simulations already have real-world causal effects (Costello et al. 2024). It notes that the extra "secret sauce" argument only works if consciousness is fundamentally different from cognition.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "simulation, no matter how fine-grained, is simply not the kind of thing that could ever realize conscious states." (p. 40) — the authors' reading of Seth, which they challenge.
  - "is this not a clear example of a simulation exerting causal powers in reality?" (p. 41)
  - "we do think that Seth fails to conclusively show that it is impossible." (p. 41)
- **Builds on / argues against:** Chalmers 1995; Searle's biological naturalism and its critics (Kim 1995; Warfield 1999); Dennett 1991; Frankish 2016; Dretske, Lycan, Rosenthal (representational / higher-order reductions); Costello, Pennycook & Rand 2024.
- **Reviewer's note:** The extracted text of the second half of p. 41 has broken letter spacing (e.g. "t h a td e t e r m i n e"); no quote was taken from that passage.

## e330. De Brigard (2026) — Vitalism in the age of AI [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `debrigard2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 41–43 (title block at the foot of p. 41; body on p. 42; references on p. 43) · **Field:** philosophy / cognitive neuroscience (Duke University)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.3 (substrate independence; generative entrenchment; "what brains do" vs "what they are"); §3.4 (non-Turing computation); the overall biological-naturalist conclusion (§7).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies / rejects in part — agrees current digital AI is probably not conscious, rejects the inference that consciousness is therefore not computational or that AI consciousness is impossible in principle.
- **The argument in plain words:** De Brigard revives Andy Clark's 1989 **microfunctionalism**: computations are multiply realisable in principle, but in practice only a few materials (neurons, glia) can carry the very complex computations that make up minds. Engineering history shows the same pattern: vacuum tubes gave way to transistors because they could not run more complex computations. That some materials fail does not make computation substrate-dependent. Seth's definition of substrate independence says mental states "can occur" in different substrates; this does not mean *any* substrate will do. So the fact that today's digital AI is not conscious says nothing against a future AI built on the right substrate. Seth's point about generative entrenchment fits microfunctionalism well. But if consciousness is explained by what brains *are* rather than what they *do*, the view risks a new vitalism: brains are conscious because of an unexplained "élan consciente".
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** A distinction between *possible* and *actual* substrate independence (the "modal scope" of multiple realisability). The historical charge of **vitalism** (the old idea that life needs a special non-physical force) against substrate-based explanations. A Dennett (2005) quote: future theories will reach "down into the subcellular" yet "still be functionalist in the broad sense".
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Computation remains substrate independent, yet computational complexity rules out most materials." (p. 42)
  - "the more complex the computation, the fewer actual physical substrates can likely instantiate it." (p. 42)
  - "Current digital architectures fall short, but future systems might instantiate conscious states if built on appropriate computational substrates." (p. 42)
- **Builds on:** Clark 1989 (*Microcognition*); Block 1978 (China brain / Bolivian economy); Putnam 1967; Dennett 2005; Bullock 1959 (against the simple neuron doctrine).
- **Reviewer's note:** The brief lists p. 42 as this commentary's first page; the title and author block actually begin at the bottom of p. 41.

## e331. Dung (2026) — Why I am not a biological naturalist [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `dung2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 43–44 · **Field:** philosophy of mind / AI ethics (Ruhr-Universität Bochum)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.1 and §3.3 (the relation between computational functionalism and substrate flexibility); §3.4 (mortal, neural, biological, analogue and neuromorphic computation); §4.1–§4.2 (predictive processing and the free energy principle); §7 (the conclusion that there are "good reasons" for biological naturalism).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** rejects — the evidence leaves the two views tied at best, and independent arguments tip the balance towards AI consciousness being possible.
- **The argument in plain words:** Dung makes three claims. First, rejecting biological naturalism does not require accepting computational functionalism; silicon AI could be conscious even if computation alone is not enough. Biological naturalism is also stronger than "AI cannot be conscious": it rules out non-living aliens too. Second, Seth's arguments share one form: some feature F (say, mortal computation) exists only in living systems (P1), and F is necessary for consciousness (P2). Seth supports P1 but gives no reason for P2. A functionalist can mirror the argument with a feature G that AI has and that is *sufficient* for consciousness. Without a reason for either P2, the two views are in a **stalemate**. Predictive processing does not break the tie, because it can be read either algorithmically or biologically. Third, there are several weak but real reasons for the functionalist P2: more unified explanations of behaviour, fit with consciousness's evolutionary function, folk intuitions, conceptual engineering, and the "implausible coincidence" charge against biological naturalism (Schwitzgebel & Pober 2024).
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** A formal reconstruction of Seth's case as a schema with an unsupported necessity premise. A mirror-image functionalist argument. A list of five positive considerations for the possibility of AI consciousness.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "denying biological naturalism does not logically require computational functionalism." (p. 43)
  - "Until someone gives us a reason to accept P2 of one of these arguments, we are at a stalemate between both views." (p. 43)
  - "we should conclude that it is more likely that AI consciousness is possible than that it is not possible." (p. 44)
- **Builds on / argues against:** Schwitzgebel & Pober 2024; Goldstein & Kirk-Giannini (n.d., language agents and global workspace); Shevlin (n.d.); Cappelen 2018 (conceptual engineering); Schlicht & Dołęga 2021 (PP may not be a theory of consciousness); Dung & Kersten 2024; Shiller 2024; Mogensen 2024.

## e332. Evers & Farisco (2026) — Is conscious AI desirable? [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `evers2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 44–45 · **Field:** neuroethics / bioethics (Uppsala University; Biogem, Italy)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §6.1 ("What should we (not) do"; Seth's line that real artificial consciousness "should not be an explicit goal"); §6.2 (conscious-seeming AI and human attributions).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** extends — agrees with Seth's caution and adds risk and epistemic arguments against building conscious AI.
- **The argument in plain words:** The authors grant the hoped-for benefits of conscious AI: better understanding of biological consciousness, faster progress, perhaps better alignment with human values. But intelligence plus consciousness does not make a being kind. In humans it has often come with hostility to "out-groups", and AI is trained on human data full of such behaviour. So expecting a conscious machine to care about humans is naive. The reverse risk is **machine welfare**: given how humans treat animals and other humans, conscious machines could suffer unnoticed. There are also problems of knowing. Consciousness is known only in living things; no empirical evidence yet shows it elsewhere. AI trained on human data can mimic signs of feeling (the **gaming problem**, Birch & Andrews 2024), so behaviour is no evidence. Looking for differences instead runs into **commensurability**: if a machine's consciousness were very different, we might be unable to detect, understand or communicate with it.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** A risk argument about the *desirability* of conscious AI, separate from its feasibility. The out-group / xenophobia argument. The detect / comprehend / communicate triad as a source of extra risk in both directions (for humans and for the machine).
- **Key quotes:**
  - "The combination of intelligence and consciousness does not entail benevolence." (p. 45)
  - "Empirical validation is essential to turn logical possibility into actuality." (p. 45)
  - "engineering conscious AI is not desirable because the potential risks far outweigh the possible benefits." (p. 45)
- **Builds on:** Birch & Andrews 2024 (gaming problem); Evers 2015, 2024; Evers et al. 2025 (multidimensional heuristic for artificial consciousness); Farisco, Evers & Changeux 2024; Khamassi, Nahon & Chatila 2024 (alignment); LeCun 2022.

## e333. Feinberg & Mallatt (2026) — Sentience, emergence in neurobiological systems, and the prospects for artificial consciousness [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `feinberg2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 46–47 · **Field:** neurology / psychiatry (Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai) and evolutionary biology (University of Idaho)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §1 ("Section 1.1": Nagel's definition; Seth's preference for "consciousness" over "sentience"); §3 broadly and §3.3 (differences between silicon circuits and brains; generative entrenchment); §3.5 (hierarchical emergence); §4.4 (the real problem); §5.5 (the strong substrate-dependent hypothesis). They also cite a "Section 4.5", which is not in the brief's section list; I record it as they wrote it.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** supports — agrees that the processes behind consciousness are tied to a living nervous system, and adds an emergence-based argument.
- **The argument in plain words:** The authors prefer the word **sentience** (from the Latin "to feel"), because the feeling aspect is what matters for AI. They endorse Seth's list of brain–computer differences: embodiment, recurrent processing, high integration, spike synchronisation, multisensory sensing for survival. Their main addition is **emergence**: a system-level feature that is not present in the parts alone and arises from interactions across levels of a hierarchy, like respiration or digestion. Sentience emerged in vertebrates as the forebrain added new levels. Complexity drives emergence, and the most complex systems in nature are biological. In their evolutionary survey, sentience requires higher-order mapped sensory representations, interoceptive-affective feelings with positive or negative valence, and hierarchical integration. Only vertebrates, arthropods and cephalopods meet these conditions. Sentience is therefore "emergently substrate-dependent". Even life-like future AI would probably not experience the way animals do.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** Neurobiological emergentism as the basis for substrate dependence. A concrete list of neural criteria for sentience and its distribution across animal groups. A terminological case for "sentience".
- **Key quotes:**
  - "like life, sentience is best understood as an emergent feature of a complex system" (p. 46)
  - "Therefore, it is highly emergently substrate-dependent" (p. 47)
- **Builds on:** Feinberg & Mallatt 2016, 2020; Mallatt & Feinberg 2021 ("constrained multiple realizability"); Feinberg 2024, 2025; Searle 1992; Kim 1998, 2006; Chalmers 2006 (strong vs weak emergence); Thompson 2007; Birch 2024.
- **Reviewer's note:** "Strong/weak emergence" is a contested distinction (Chalmers 2006, which they cite). The commentary does not say which kind it means; its biological examples (digestion, respiration) are usually treated as weak emergence.

## e334. Fields & Glazebrook (2026) — The question is not "are they conscious?" but "what could they be conscious of?" [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `fields2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 47–48 · **Field:** theoretical biology / physics of information (Allen Discovery Center, Tufts) and mathematics (Eastern Illinois University)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.1 and §3.4 (Seth's "Turing computation" and non-von-Neumann architectures); §4.2 (the free energy principle as a divider between living and non-living); §4.4 (the real problem); §6.2 (people attributing human-like awareness to AI).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** rejects the framing — both computational functionalism and biological naturalism ask an unanswerable ontological question (what a thing *is*).
- **The argument in plain words:** Seth's critique closely echoes Searle's 1980 BBS paper, written in an "AI winter", and both share one assumption: that whether something computes is a fact about what it is. The authors say instead that computation is a matter of **measurement and interpretation**: one process can be read as computing many functions. Any dynamical system can therefore be regarded as a computer. The free energy principle (FEP) applies to *any* system with a Markov blanket (a statistical boundary between inside and outside), so it cannot separate living from non-living. Whether a system has such a boundary is itself a matter of measurement. We can only observe what systems *do*. What science can discover is what a system is capable of being aware of, meaning what it can respond to differentially. Laptops can register key presses, LLMs text strings, bacteria osmotic stress; none of this is human-like.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** An interpretation-relative account of computation. A reading of the FEP as scale-free and neutral about life. Reservoir computing as a neglected non-von-Neumann architecture. A proposal to replace "is it conscious?" with "what could it be aware of?" as the practical antidote to over-attribution.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Whether a process is computation, however, is not a matter of fact, but a matter of measurement and interpretation" (p. 48)
  - "Laptops are capable of being aware of key presses, and large language models (LLMs) are capable of being aware of text strings." (p. 48)
  - "The key to the “real problem” of consciousness, in our view, is careful attention to what systems could be aware of." (p. 48)
- **Builds on / argues against:** Searle 1980; Horsman et al. 2014 (when a physical system computes); Bongard & Levin 2023; Fields, Glazebrook & Levin 2021, 2024; Tanaka et al. 2019 (reservoir computing); Turing 1937.
- **Reviewer's note:** The authors use "aware of" for any differential response, so laptops and rocks count. Whether this sense of "aware" is the same as phenomenal consciousness is exactly what Seth's article is about; the commentary sets that question aside as unanswerable rather than answering it.

## e335. Fleming & Shea (2026) — Where is the asymmetry between intelligence and consciousness? [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `fleming2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 49–50 · **Field:** cognitive neuroscience / metacognition (UCL) and philosophy of mind (Institute of Philosophy, University of London)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.8 (What about intelligence? — Seth restricts "intelligence" to input–output mappings); §4 (candidate consciousness-related properties: PP, autopoiesis, affect, informational measures); §5 (theoretical options).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — the claimed asymmetry (intelligence is substrate-flexible, consciousness may not be) is not established.
- **The argument in plain words:** Seth says intelligence could arise in silicon but consciousness may be tied to biology. In §3.8 he keeps this contrast by limiting "intelligence" to input–output mappings. Fleming and Shea say this excludes planning, reasoning and language, the very abilities that motivated talk of AGI. Planning needs represented goals, perhaps real needs, perhaps metabolism. Reasoning works on contents, which may not reduce to input–output. Language may need understanding. Several "intelligent" capacities are also tied empirically to consciousness: metacognition is more efficient on conscious representations, some instrumental learning seems to require awareness. If a capacity depends on consciousness, it shares its substrate dependence. Seth's §4 properties also lack a test for **exclusivity**: predictive processing and autopoiesis may be present everywhere, not just with consciousness. They propose the **natural kind method**: look for properties that reliably cluster with conscious states across systems, and use computational models to do so.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** The symmetry argument (the same open questions apply to intelligence). Empirical links between consciousness and metacognition / learning. An explicit exclusivity test. The natural-kind research method, which works whether or not computational functionalism is true.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "If a capacity depends on consciousness, it inherits whatever substrate dependencies consciousness has." (p. 49)
  - "What is missing is an explicit test for exclusivity" (p. 49)
  - "The upshot is that an alleged asymmetry between intelligence and consciousness has not been established." (p. 50)
- **Builds on:** Shea & Bayne 2010; Shea 2012; Bayne & Shea 2020 (natural kind method); Michel 2023; Skora et al. 2021; Godfrey-Smith 2019; Keller & Mrsic-Flogel 2018; Di Paolo 2005.

## e336. Friston (2026) — To be or not to be? A question of mortality [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `friston2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 50–52 (references end at the top of p. 52) · **Field:** computational neuroscience / free energy principle (UCL Queen Square Institute of Neurology; VERSES)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §4.2 (free energy principle; Seth's claim that thermodynamic and variational free energy minimisation are "in some physical sense" the same thing); §3.4 (mortal vs immortal computation; whether Turing computation could simulate mortal computation); §4.3 or §3.3 (the quote "no way to separate what they are from what they do" for autopoietic systems — the commentary does not give the section); §1 ("We humans know we're conscious").
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** supports and extends — gives a thermodynamic account of why immortal computation can simulate but not realise mortal computation, while leaving room for neuromorphic conscious artefacts.
- **The argument in plain words:** Friston endorses Seth's link between metabolism and prediction. Inside a Markov blanket, internal states carry two "information geometries": one about the physical (thermodynamic) state, one about beliefs over the outside world. Moving in one means moving in the other, so belief updating has a thermodynamic cost, with Landauer's principle as a lower bound. This holds only for **mortal computation** (computation tied to its own physical substrate). On von Neumann machines, most energy goes to reading and writing memory (the "von Neumann bottleneck"). That split puts a boundary between processor and memory, so memory cannot be self-producing. Such machines can simulate but not realise mortal computation. Mortal computation needs **in-memory processing** (neuromorphic, memristor, photonic hardware). But then memories cannot be uploaded or shared, so consciousness, read as self-evidencing, could not be copied. Finally, because only a blanket's surface is observable, we can never know whether another thing is conscious, only infer it.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** A thermodynamic link (a limiting case of the Jarzynski relation) between belief updating and heat. The von Neumann bottleneck as the concrete reason digital machines are "immortal". Non-shareability of memories as a possible line between intelligence and consciousness. A closing question: does knowing one is conscious depend on first inferring consciousness in others ("knowing together")?
- **Key quotes:**
  - "there is a thermodynamic cost to belief updating or, equivalently, metabolism entails belief updating." (p. 51)
  - "In short, it could simulate but not realise mortal computation." (p. 51)
  - "This suggests that consciousness – read as self-evidencing – cannot be uploaded, downloaded, or shared." (p. 51)
- **Builds on:** Friston 2019; Friston, Wiese & Hobson 2020 (Markovian monism); Jarzynski 1997; Crooks 2007; Landauer 1961; Conant & Ashby 1970; Fields et al. 2021; Hutter 2006; Zou et al. 2021 (processing-in-memory).
- **Reviewer's note:** The single displayed equation on p. 51 is garbled in the text extraction ("Uðπ τÞ/C0 Uðπ0Þ¼ ΔU ¼ kBT /C1 ΔF"). It appears to state that the change in internal energy equals `k_B T` times the change in variational free energy, but this reading is a reconstruction and should be checked against the rendered page.

## e337. Godfrey-Smith (2026) — Biological naturalism without the primacy of prediction [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `godfreysmith2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 52–53 · **Field:** philosophy of biology / philosophy of mind (University of Sydney)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §4.1 (predictive processing); §4.2 (free energy principle, in its ambitious form).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — welcomes biological naturalism but argues that building it on ambitious predictive processing is "probably a wrong turn".
- **The argument in plain words:** Predictive processing can be held in a modest form (a theory of perception in complex animals) or an ambitious form (all behaviour serves to minimise sensory surprise). Seth seems tempted by the ambitious form. In it, action serves perception by making input predictable. Godfrey-Smith says action is for getting things done, whether or not it reduces surprise. He gives a Darwinian version of the **dark room** objection. A male funnel-web spider that stays in its den avoids surprise but never mates; surprise-minimising males lose to risk-takers. Seth's reply (organisms must let some things change so others stay the same) does not fit reproduction, which is not about keeping things the same. Corals that release gametes into the sea never learn whether the timing was good, so better action brings no smoother sensory flow. Moving the "prediction" to the population level makes it metaphorical, and low-variance strategies are not always favoured by selection.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** Biologically realistic counterexamples (spider, coral) to surprise minimisation as the basic imperative. The claim that the functional dependence runs from perception to action, not the reverse. A suggestion that biological naturalism should be detached from the FEP.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Action, though, is for getting things done, whether the results are surprising to the organism or not" (p. 52)
  - "Action does not serve prediction and perception; the functional relationship runs in the other direction." (p. 52)
- **Builds on / argues against:** Parr, Pezzulo & Friston 2022 (argues against); Seth 2021 *Being You* (argues against its dark-room reply); Godfrey-Smith 2024; Gillespie 1977 and Seger & Brockman 1987 (bet-hedging); discussion with Paul Griffiths.

## e338. Gomez-Marin (2026) — A stroll beyond the confines of naturalism [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `gomezmarin2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 53–54 · **Field:** neuroscience / philosophy of science (Instituto de Neurociencias, CSIC-UMH, Alicante)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** the framing of the whole debate (§1, §3.1, §3.6); Seth's suggestion that machines may need to be alive before they can be conscious (§5 scenarios, §7). No section numbers are named in the commentary.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** rejects the shared framing — both functionalism and biological naturalism sit inside one Western ontology, naturalism.
- **The argument in plain words:** Functionalism conflates "doing" with "being"; structuralism conflates "being" with "being made of". Seth places biology in the middle. Gomez-Marin reads Seth as saying machines lack not interiority but the right kind of "exteriority" (a living body). He then steps back to what all positions share: **naturalism**, defined via anthropologist Philippe Descola as one shared physical world with differing inner lives. Descola lists four "ontologies": naturalism, animism (shared inner life, different bodies), totemism and analogism. Under animism, an artefact may have intentionality not tied to its material. Descola also argues that denying a speaking computer a mind because it lacks "vitality" brings back the old subject/object dualism. So biological naturalism returns us to Cartesian dualism. Conscious AI is then also an anthropological question: how we relate to non-human others.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** An anthropological, cross-cultural frame (Descola's four ontologies). The claim that the life requirement re-imports Cartesian dualism. A recasting of conscious AI as a "real-world laboratory" for relating to alien minds.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "do not ask what naturalism can do for AI but what AI can do for naturalism." (p. 53)
  - "We are back to Cartesian square one." (p. 53)
  - "The key may lie in anthropology rather than biology." (p. 54)
- **Builds on:** Descola 2005 (*Beyond Nature and Culture*), its only reference.
- **Reviewer's note:** The commentary proposes a change of perspective rather than an argument about which systems are conscious; it states no test or criterion that would decide the AI question.

## e339. Hohwy (2026) — Self-evidencing and consciousness-specific mortal computation [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `hohwy2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 54–55 · **Field:** philosophy of mind / predictive processing (Monash Centre for Consciousness and Contemplative Studies)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §4.3 (membranes, mitochondria and xenobots: electromagnetic fields, anaesthetics and mitochondria, self-organising biological material); §5 (the weak and strong substrate-dependent forms of biological naturalism, cf. §5.5, versus a "mortal computational" form); §3.4 (mortal vs immortal computation and the energy argument); §4.2 (self-evidencing and the FEP).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — supports a computational, mortal-computation form of biological naturalism, rejects the move to weak/strong substrate dependence as premature, and keeps the door open to future conscious AI.
- **The argument in plain words:** Electromagnetic field theories and self-organising cell findings are interesting, but not yet specific to consciousness: they may serve life or agency in general. So the best option is the mortal-computational version of biological naturalism. Seth's energy argument (brains are efficient, so they are not doing immortal computation) assumes the mortal/immortal difference matters for consciousness. That needs support: our being conscious and our brains being mortal computers "could be a coincidence". The answer must come from *function*. Self-evidencing for survival is too broad; it would count simple organisms or groups. What is distinctive of mortal computation is flexibility under uncertainty and volatility, not energy use. Our consciousness shows such functions: detaching from input (dreaming, mind-wandering), re-attaching (attention), and modelling ourselves as persistent agents. Artificial systems "educated" by distillation rather than parameter copying, with precision control inside a Markov blanket, could have this structure.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** A demand that substrate claims be consciousness-specific. A shift from substrate to the *function* of mortal computation. A concrete list of consciousness-relevant functions. "Existence-dependent" rather than substrate-dependent self-evidencing as a route to conscious AI.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "the fact that we are conscious and that our brains are mortal computers could be a coincidence, after all." (p. 54)
  - "mortal computers can solve tasks in uncertain, changing environments" (p. 55)
  - "Artificial agents could potentially have such inner structure, in ways specific to consciousness." (p. 55)
- **Builds on:** Hinton 2022 (mortal computation, distillation); Ororbia & Friston 2023; Fisher & Hohwy 2024; Hohwy (in press); Whyte et al. (in press, minimal theory of consciousness in active inference).

## e340. Jablonka, Ginsburg & Bronfman (2026) — Consciousness is not "universal" but depends on specifically organized biological affordances [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `jablonka2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 55–56 · **Field:** philosophy and history of biology (Tel Aviv University), evolutionary biology (Open University of Israel), industry data science (Pecan.ai)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.1 and §3.6 (computational functionalism neither necessary nor sufficient); §4.3 (autopoietic processes); §4.1 (multimodal integration, which they say Seth "hints" at); §1 (Nagel's definition, which they find too vague).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** supports and extends — agrees with Seth's conclusion and adds an account of which living organisms are conscious and why.
- **The argument in plain words:** Seth does not say that all living things are conscious, so what makes only some conscious? First, define consciousness: a mode of being grounded in felt, private bodily experiences that share one perspective and have reportable physiological and behavioural aspects. On this definition, no current AI algorithm is conscious. Dreaming or deep thought are partly disconnected from the senses, but they depend on connected bodily states in evolution and development. So LLM-style "disconnected" operations are not like human thought. Embodied robots with sensors might seem to qualify, which leads to **biological affordances**: material properties (elasticity, excitability, inner turbulence), growth, layered memory and nested value systems. These are necessary but not sufficient. Minimal consciousness, they argue, came with **Unlimited Associative Learning (UAL)**: open-ended, general learning that needs a central nervous system. UAL in a silicon robot cannot be ruled out but looks implausible now.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** An explicit working definition of biological consciousness. A specific evolutionary marker (UAL) for which organisms are conscious. A list of biological affordances any conscious robot would need analogues of. The suggestion that AI inner states may need new concepts rather than the word "conscious".
- **Key quotes:**
  - "We go along with Seth’s claim that computational functionalism is neither necessary nor sufficient for consciousness as we know it." (p. 55)
  - "Assuming that disconnected, shallow, and solipsistic AI operations (such as those of LLMs) are similar enough to human conscious thinking is therefore unwarranted." (p. 56)
  - "Consciousness-as-we-know-it is probably not a universal like energy or mass" (p. 56)
- **Builds on:** Ginsburg & Jablonka 2019 (*The Evolution of the Sensitive Soul*, UAL); Jablonka & Ginsburg 2022; Bronfman, Ginsburg & Jablonka 2021 ("When will robots be sentient?").

## Cross-commentary notes

**Who agrees with whom.**

- *The pro-biology group* — Feinberg & Mallatt (e333), Jablonka et al. (e340), Friston (e336) — all accept Seth's conclusion and each offers a *different* basis: emergence across neural levels, evolutionary affordances plus Unlimited Associative Learning, and thermodynamics of mortal computation. Feinberg & Mallatt and Jablonka et al. agree closely: both tie consciousness to evolved nervous systems with valenced, integrated, multimodal representation, and both place it in a limited set of animal groups (e333 names vertebrates, arthropods and cephalopods; e340 dates the onset to the Cambrian).
- *The "not yet shown" group* — Dołęga & Cleeremans (e329), Dung (e331), Fleming & Shea (e335), Hohwy (e339) — share one structural objection: Seth shows that brains and computers *differ*, but not that the difference *matters for consciousness*. Dung calls it the missing necessity premise (P2); Hohwy calls it the coincidence worry and asks for consciousness-specific functions; Fleming & Shea call it the missing exclusivity test; Dołęga & Cleeremans say assuming it begs the question.
- *Computation is broader than Seth's target* — De Brigard (e330), Fields & Glazebrook (e334) and Hohwy (e339) all say a biological basis does not mean a non-computational one. De Brigard says complex computations just have few possible substrates; Fields & Glazebrook say everything can be read as computing; Hohwy keeps a "mortal computational" form open to AI.

**Where they clash.**

- *Predictive processing and the FEP.* Friston (e336) and Hohwy (e339) build on the FEP. Godfrey-Smith (e337) argues the ambitious FEP is a wrong turn for biological naturalism; Fields & Glazebrook (e334) say the FEP cannot divide living from non-living at all; Dung (e331) and Fleming & Shea (e335) say PP does not help decide the question because it is algorithmic or ubiquitous.
- *What "consciousness" should mean.* Clark (e328) wants to drop the "what it is like" target in favour of response profiles; Feinberg & Mallatt (e333) want to *strengthen* it as "feeling" (sentience); Jablonka et al. (e340) call Nagel's phrase too vague and give a bodily definition; Fields & Glazebrook (e334) use "aware of" for any differential response. Dołęga & Cleeremans (e329) show that Seth's position depends on which reading of qualia he takes.
- *Can neuromorphic hardware close the gap?* Friston (e336) and Hohwy (e339) say in-memory or distillation-trained artefacts might be conscious; Feinberg & Mallatt (e333) and Jablonka et al. (e340) think even life-like machines would probably not experience as animals do; De Brigard (e330) thinks the right future substrate could suffice.
- *Intelligence vs consciousness.* Clark (e328) and Fleming & Shea (e335) both attack Seth's sharp split between them, from different sides (illusionism; empirical entanglement). Friston (e336) instead proposes a new line between them: intelligence can be shared and copied, consciousness cannot.

**Recurring themes.**

1. The **burden of proof** for substrate dependence (e329, e331, e335, e339).
2. **Mortal computation** and energy (e331, e336, e339), first introduced by Hinton 2022.
3. **Simulation vs realisation** (e329 against; e336 for, on thermodynamic grounds).
4. **Vitalism and dualism worries** about the life requirement (e330, e338).
5. **Epistemic limits on detecting machine consciousness**: the gaming problem (e332), the Markov blanket's observable surface (e336), unanswerable ontology (e334).
6. **Ethics**: only Evers & Farisco (e332) address desirability and machine welfare directly; Fields & Glazebrook (e334) touch on over-attribution.
7. **Evolution and comparative biology** as evidence (e333, e337, e340); Godfrey-Smith uses evolution *against* the FEP, Feinberg & Mallatt and Jablonka et al. use it *for* biological naturalism.
