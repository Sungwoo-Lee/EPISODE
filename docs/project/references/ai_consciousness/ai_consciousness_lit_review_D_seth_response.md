# AI consciousness — Part D: Seth's Author's Response to the BBS commentaries

## What this part covers (plain-language entry point)

In 2025 the neuroscientist Anil Seth published a long "target article" in the journal *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* (BBS). He argued that today's AI is very unlikely to be conscious. His reason: consciousness probably depends on properties of living, biological matter, not only on running the right computer program. BBS then printed 50 short replies ("commentaries") from other researchers. Some agreed, some objected. This part reviews Seth's final reply to all of them, called the Author's Response.

The response does three things. First, it restates what Seth's article did and did not claim. Second, it answers the commentaries in groups: people who defend the "computer program is enough" view; people who say "computation" should mean something broader; people with different worldviews (for example, that everything is a little conscious); and people who try to strengthen the biological view. Third, it repeats the ethical warnings: building conscious AI is a bad idea, and AI that only *seems* conscious is dangerous too.

Seth admits one logical slip, accepts some new ideas, and keeps his main position. The table at the end shows, for all 50 commentaries, where he answers them and how.

## D1. Seth (2026) — The stuff matters: Consciousness, computation, and biology [Author's Response]

**PDF:** `sources/Seth 2025 - Conscious artificial intelligence and biological naturalism (BBS with commentaries and response).pdf` — version of record, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 49, Author's Response e366, doi 10.1017/S0140525X26106906. PDF pages 92–109 (response text pp. 92–106, notes pp. 106–107, references pp. 107–109). Manifest key: `seth2026response`.

**Two terms used throughout.**
- **Computational functionalism (CF):** the view that running the right kind of computation is *enough* (sufficient) for consciousness, in whatever material runs it.
- **Biological naturalism (BN):** the view that some properties of living biological systems are *needed* (necessary) for consciousness, though they may not be enough by themselves.

Seth calls his target article "CAIBN" (Conscious AI and Biological Naturalism). Section numbers below that start with "R" are sections of the response; "CAIBN 3.4" means section 3.4 of the target article. A sibling review of the target article's preprint is in [[computational_functionalism_lit_review_C_biological_naturalism]] (Part C, §5).

### Phase 1: Plain overview

Seth begins by saying the debate is urgent. People worry about two errors: failing to see consciousness in AI that has it, and wrongly seeing consciousness in AI that does not. He notes that belief in AI consciousness already drives calls for "AI welfare" and AI "rights", and he thinks such rights could stop humans from switching dangerous systems off.

His central move is to frame CF and BN as two *bets* that are not equal in weight. CF says that computation is enough, in any material. That is a strong claim and needs strong evidence. BN only says that some biological property is needed. That is a lighter claim. And to make conscious AI very unlikely, Seth says, it is enough to show CF is false. BN does not also have to be proved true.

He then works through the commentaries. Against the defenders of CF he argues that they assume it rather than argue for it. Against those who widen the word "computation" (to include "mortal", analogue or biological computation) he argues that each widening ties the computation more closely to its material. That loses the "any material will do" property that made conscious AI seem plausible in the first place. He adds two new arguments: brains live in continuous physical time while programs only care about the order of steps, and a simulation of something is not the thing itself.

Finally he presents BN as a growing research programme rather than a fixed doctrine, lists the specific biological candidates commentators proposed, and restates the ethics. His closing line of argument: "the stuff matters" — the physical material matters.

### Phase 2: The argument, step by step

1. **Starting assumption (R1.1).** A healthy, embodied human brain in certain states is enough for consciousness. The question is which of its features are needed and which are enough.
2. **Two bets (R1.1).** CF bets on the computational level; BN bets on lower-level biological properties. Seth's article tried "to shift credences away from CF and towards (some version of) BN" (p. 93).
3. **Independence of the two arguments (R1.1).** The case *against* CF does not depend on BN being true. Other alternatives exist (for example integrated information theory). But evidence *for* BN is still evidence against CF.
4. **Asymmetry (R1.1, R5.4, R7).** CF claims sufficiency across all materials, a heavy burden "which I believe remains unmet". BN claims necessity, which is "logically lighter".
5. **CF is not the same as functionalism (R2).** Functionalism in general says mental states are defined by a system's internal organisation. CF is a narrower subset. Weak BN is itself a kind of functionalism. Retreating from CF to general functionalism gives up the "any material" property that licenses conscious AI.
6. **The concession (R2.2).** His target-article sentence "Conscious AI requires [...] computational functionalism to hold" was strictly wrong. AI could in principle be conscious through some other sufficient property. But he holds that no such substrate-flexible property is in view, so "without CF being true, there are no good reasons to believe it is plausible" (p. 94).
7. **The dilemma about "computation" (R3).** Take the standard (Turing) sense: the brain does not fit it well, so CF loses support. Take a narrower sense: it is poorly motivated or cannot tell computing systems from non-computing ones. Take a broader sense: it gives up material-independence. Every option hurts CF.
8. **Time (R3.6).** A program is the same whatever the gap between steps. Brains and conscious experience run in continuous physical time. Adding an external clock, or analogue processes, brings material constraints back in.
9. **Simulation is not instantiation (R3.7).** A simulation produces the target only if the target is itself a computation of a similar kind. More detail does not help: a detailed simulated cell is no closer to being alive. For the free energy principle (a theory that living systems act to keep their own states predictable), Seth says that in living things the information-theoretic and thermodynamic descriptions are *one physical process*, while in a computer the algorithm is only an abstraction of it.
10. **Other worldviews cost discriminating power (R4).** Animism, panpsychism (consciousness is everywhere), illusionism (consciousness is not what it seems) and a Wittgensteinian language-use view all make the question "is X conscious?" hard or impossible to answer from evidence.
11. **BN as a research programme (R5).** Using the philosopher Imre Lakatos's test, a research programme is good if it keeps producing new testable claims. Seth argues BN does (membrane potentials, mitochondria and anaesthesia, brain rhythms, scale integration, organoid experiments), while CF "often retreats into redefining the relevant computations" (p. 104).
12. **Ethics (R6, R7).** Real conscious AI would be a disaster; conscious-seeming AI is already a danger. The precautionary principle (treat AI as possibly conscious, to be safe) should, he argues, currently be turned *away from*, because false positives are also harmful.

**Limits Seth states himself.** His version of BN "is wrong in the details or wrong altogether" is a live possibility (R5, p. 100). We are not yet in a position to decide whether neurobiological details matter for consciousness (paraphrase of R5.4, p. 103; the source line has an extraction artifact). He apologises to commentators he "passed over only briefly" (R7, p. 105).

### How the response restates Seth's overall position

**What Seth says the target article did claim.**
- Its four stages: (i) psychological biases that lead us to over-attribute consciousness to AI; (ii) challenges to CF; (iii) a case for BN; (iv) ethics of AI that is, or seems, conscious (R1.1, p. 92).
- Its aim was to shift credences (degrees of belief), not to prove anything: "both CF and BN are placing bets on what counts" (p. 93).
- Conscious AI is "extremely unlikely, especially for silicon-based digital systems" (p. 92); "AI" means such systems unless stated otherwise.
- BN comes in flavours from weak (the needed organisation can only be built in biology) to strong (consciousness depends on intrinsic properties of biological material) (p. 93).

**What Seth says the target article did not claim.**
- It did not make the case against CF depend on BN being true (answering Dung and Michel, p. 93).
- It did not argue against functionalism in general, or against multiple realisability (the same function built in different ways). It argued against *substrate independence* (the same function in *any material*) (R2, R3.3; answering Overgaard and Richards & Agüera y Arcas).
- It does not entail "carbon chauvinism" (only carbon can be conscious) or "neo-vitalism" (a mysterious life force) (R1.1, R5.3).
- It did not claim consciousness requires a reflective sense of self; its simplest form is a formless "feeling of being alive" (R3.5).
- It did not treat consciousness as a private, non-causal extra; consciousness is "functionally potent" and fully real (R4, against Shanahan and Bowes).
- It did not deny that some neurally implemented computations may play a role in consciousness — only that computation is *sufficient* (note 2, p. 106).

**What Seth corrects.** The sentence that conscious AI "requires" CF was strictly incorrect (R2.2). He keeps the practical conclusion.

**The final restatement (R7, pp. 105–106), four points.**
1. BN, not CF, should be the default position — because of our biases, the weak case for computational sufficiency, the growing non-vitalist reasons for biological necessity, and the fact that the only systems we know are conscious are alive.
2. BN and CF should "play by the same rules": both must produce testable claims.
3. Conscious AI is "still a terrible idea"; vigilance is needed about neuromorphic engineering and synthetic biology.
4. Conscious-seeming AI is dangerous too; whether AI *seems* conscious is "a matter of psychology and industrial design".

### Section-by-section backbone (response sections, in the order they appear)

Each entry gives: title, PDF pages, which commentaries it answers (manifest keys), what Seth concedes or changes, what he holds firm, and key quotes.

#### R1. Introduction — p. 92
- **Answers:** none by name (cites Bengio & Elmoznino 2025 and Long et al. 2024, which are not BBS commentaries).
- **Content:** The topic is urgent. Belief in AI consciousness drives "AI welfare" and possible "rights". Frontier AI shows loose "self-preservation" behaviour (attempts to disable oversight). Rights could block switching systems off. He also states the opposite worry: denying rights to conscious beings would be a grave moral error.
- **Holds firm:** "My own view remains that conscious AI is extremely unlikely, especially for silicon-based digital systems." (p. 92)
- **New framing:** substrate dependence of consciousness is "emerging as a key research challenge".

#### R1.1 Preliminaries — pp. 92–93
- **Answers:** `dung2026bbs`, `michel2026bbs` (they imply his anti-CF case depends on BN); `piccinini2026bbs` (alternatives exist); `lerchner2026bbs` (evidence for BN is evidence against CF).
- **Concedes / changes:** none; clarifies.
- **Holds firm:** The two arguments reinforce each other but do not depend on each other. CF and BN are asymmetric bets. Neither BN flavour entails carbon chauvinism.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Biological naturalism (BN) is the claim that other (primarily lower-level) properties of biological systems are necessary, though not necessarily sufficient, for consciousness." (p. 93)
  - "for consciousness in AI to be rendered highly implausible, it’s enough for CF to be false. It is not necessary to show that BN is true." (p. 93)

#### R2. Holding out for CF — p. 93
- **Answers:** names `metzinger2026bbs`, `lerchner2026bbs`, `piccinini2026bbs`, `wiese2026bbs`, `cao2026bbs` as sympathetic to his critique; `dolega2026bbs` on the functions of consciousness.
- **Content:** Two distinctions. (a) CF is a subset of functionalism; weak BN is also a kind of functionalism. (b) Functionalism (what consciousness *is*) differs from the *functions* of consciousness (what it is *for*); there is "no direct line" from one to the other.
- **Key quote:** "Buying functionalism doesn’t mean buying CF." (p. 93)

#### R2.1 Defending CF — pp. 93–94
- **Answers:** `michel2026bbs` (main target), `blum2026bbs`, `dung2026bbs`; cites `silberstein2026bbs`, `bowes2026bbs`, `mitchell2026bbs` (embodiment turn), `fleming2026bbs` (aside), `jablonka2026bbs` (substrate dependency).
- **Michel's case, as Seth reports it:** "bet on functionalism" because it explains memory, language and some aspects of consciousness (conscious vs unconscious states, binocular rivalry).
- **Seth's five replies to Michel:** (1) the anti-CF case does not need BN; (2) Michel blurs functionalism and CF; (3) using computation as a useful tool for explaining human consciousness does not show computation is sufficient; (4) the induction from memory/language to consciousness assumes what is in question; (5) alternatives to functionalism do exist — the commentaries themselves sketch a BN research agenda.
- **Blum & Blum ("Conscious Turing Machine"):** they assume CF instead of justifying it; their "Axiom 1" (broadcast plus attention gives "unitary experience") contains the claim that needs proof. Seth reports they think "conscious AI is inevitable".
- **Dung:** fairly says both sides are inconclusive, but misses the asymmetry. Of his five arguments for CF, three (folk intuitions, conceptual engineering, weaknesses of BN) are not about computational sufficiency; two (evolutionary function, explanatory unification) are more appealing but fit BN too.
- **Concedes:** none here.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Rather than simply betting on CF, positive arguments in its favour are needed." (p. 93)
  - "his arguments offer some reasons why one might want CF to be true, rather than evidence that it is." (p. 94)

#### R2.2 CF is not strictly necessary for conscious AI — p. 94
- **Answers:** `dung2026bbs`, `chrisley2026bbs`; mentions `shanahan2026bbs` (on whether consciousness is intrinsic); note 2 mentions `chrisley2026bbs` and `piccinini2026bbs`.
- **Concedes:** Yes, explicitly. His target-article statement that conscious AI "requires [...] computational functionalism to hold" is strictly incorrect. He thanks both.
- **Holds firm:** Chrisley's "McLarenism" analogy (owning a McLaren is sufficient to win a Grand Prix) misleads, because winning a race is relational, while consciousness is (Seth says) intrinsic. A better analogy — a McLaren is sufficient but not necessary to exceed 200 mph — is probably true. And practically, no substrate-flexible alternative to computation is in sight.
- **Key quote:** "In short, conscious AI does not require CF to be true, but without CF being true, there are no good reasons to believe it is plausible." (p. 94)

#### R3. The remit of computation — p. 94
- **Answers:** no commentator by name; sets up R3.1–R3.7.
- **Content:** The three-way dilemma (standard / narrower / broader computation). Every option makes CF implausible.

#### R3.1 Standard digital computation — p. 94
- **Answers:** none by name.
- **Content:** Turing computation separates software from hardware sharply; the von Neumann architecture makes it physical (separate processor and memory). Today's artificial neural networks also "live in Turing World". Evidence that the material of the brain matters is evidence against CF. Note 3: neuromorphic computing is more complicated; some of it only simulates spikes on standard hardware, some builds biological properties into hardware.

#### R3.2 Narrowing computation — p. 95
- **Answers:** `fields2026bbs`, `lerchner2026bbs` (computation is observer-dependent); `shevlin2026bbs`, `birch2026bbs` (mentioned).
- **Partly agrees:** Observer-dependence is "a serious challenge" to computational views. If computation depends on an observer's interpretation, CF must say either consciousness is observer-dependent or it is not computation.
- **Holds:** Fields & Glazebrook's proposal to ask what AI could be "aware of" fits non-human animals better than AI, because it presupposes the system is conscious. Observer-independent accounts of computation exist (e.g. Anderson & Piccinini 2024), but they are broader than Turing computation and so lose material-independence.

#### R3.3 Broadening computation: Is life computational? — pp. 95–96
- **Answers:** `richards2026bbs` (main), `overgaard2026bbs`, `schlicht2026bbs`, `schwitzgebel2026bbs` (pointer to R5.2).
- **Richards & Agüera y Arcas, as reported:** life itself is "inherently computational", since von Neumann's Universal Constructor is Turing-complete and can self-replicate inside a cellular automaton.
- **Holds firm:** (1) the Constructor's "closure" exists only inside the abstract automaton world; run on a computer, it does not rebuild the computer, while a living cell does rebuild its own physical basis. (2) The argument commits the "Church–Turing fallacy": a mathematical thesis about what is computable says nothing about which physical systems *are* computing. (3) Flight is substrate-flexible but not computational, and has a consensus functional description that consciousness lacks. (4) It confuses multiple realisability with substrate independence; Overgaard makes the same confusion about Seth's view; Schlicht draws the distinction correctly.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Living systems continually reconstitute their own physical basis." (p. 95)
  - "Summarising, it is difficult to sustain the view that life is inherently computational" (p. 96)

#### R3.4 Broadening computation: Other forms — pp. 96–97
- **Answers:** `friston2026bbs`, `fields2026bbs`, `ramstead2026bbs`, `hohwy2026bbs`, `larkum2026bbs` (cited as "Aru et al."), `bowes2026bbs`, `debrigard2026bbs`, `parr2026bbs`, `silberstein2026bbs`, `feinberg2026bbs`, `piccinini2026bbs`, `nave2026bbs`, `blackburne2026bbs` (cited as "Mediano et al.").
- **Content:** Analogue, reservoir, mortal, neural and biological computation all trade off against substrate flexibility.
  - **De Brigard:** some substrate-dependent, brain-like computation might be sufficient. Seth agrees this is possible, but then conscious AI would need those material properties — "a (weak) BN dressed in computational clothes".
  - **Friston:** "mortal computation" (computation that cannot be separated from, and "dies" with, its physical substrate), plus in-memory processing, supplies the thermodynamics-to-inference link in Seth's BN. Seth welcomes it.
  - **Hohwy:** mortal computation may explain functions of consciousness.
  - **Aru et al.:** standard digital computation is likely insufficient, but more brain-like computation might be enough. Seth reframes the question as "how brain-like" AI must be. Key properties: continuous, fluid biology and **scale integration** (levels of the system constrain each other, unlike the clean separation of levels in Turing computation). Seth cites new work suggesting more scale integration (lower "emergence") in conscious states than under anaesthesia.
  - **Mediano et al.:** separate kinds of "compatibilist emergence". Perfect independence of macro from micro conflicts with scale integration, but "causal emergence" may *require* it. Seth calls this "particularly important".
- **Concedes / changes:** He accepts mortal and biological computation as a "middle ground", but counts them against CF because they add substrate dependence.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "we arrive at a (weak) BN dressed in computational clothes" (p. 96)
  - "the “mortal” is doing more work than the “computation.”" (p. 96)

#### R3.5 Embodiment and embeddedness — p. 97
- **Answers:** `bowes2026bbs`, `mitchell2026bbs`; cites Metzinger's 2024 book (not his commentary).
- **Partly concedes:** Sympathetic to Bowes that embodiment might ground real understanding in AI (CAIBN 3.7, note 40).
- **Holds:** Understanding and consciousness are separate questions. Against Mitchell & Jennings, he doubts that every conscious experience includes experiencing oneself as its subject; the simplest form of consciousness is a formless "feeling of being alive". He welcomes their emphasis on time.
- **Key quote:** "understanding and consciousness may be separable both in principle and in practice." (p. 97)

#### R3.6 Time — p. 98
- **Answers:** `allen2026bbs`, `mitchell2026bbs`; mentions `friston2026bbs`.
- **New argument (developed here more than in the target article):** For a program only the order of steps matters; on CF, slowing a computer's clock "to a crawl" should not change its experience. Brain activity and experience are in continuous physical time. Adding a clock or analogue processes reimports substrate constraints. Note 9: keeping 1s and 0s stable costs energy, because silicon also faces entropy.
- **Key quote:** "Brain function is not only embodied and embedded; it is also entimed." (p. 98)

#### R3.7 Simulation and instantiation — pp. 98–99
- **Answers:** `wiese2026bbs` (agrees), `dolega2026bbs` (disagrees), `chiang2026bbs` (disagrees), `ramstead2026bbs` (agrees with nuance), `levin2026bbs`, `blum2026bbs`, `allen2026bbs` (poses the active-inference question), `friston2026bbs`, `parr2026bbs`.
- **Holds firm:**
  - Against Dolega & Cleeremans: apparent causation among simulated objects (a SimCity hurricane hitting simulated ships) is not physical causation in the hardware. Effects *of* a simulation on people (a forecast makes people shelter) are not the causal relations that make the phenomenon itself.
  - Against Chiang: even an atom-level simulation of autopoiesis (a living system's continual self-production) does not realise its causal relations.
  - On the free energy principle: in living systems the two descriptions are one process; in computers the algorithm abstracts it, and continuous time is broken.
- **Partly qualifies Ramstead:** being able to simulate X is not *no* evidence. If the best simulation of X models computations, credence that X is computational should rise; if it models non-computational processes, it should fall.
- **New implication:** whole-brain emulation is not a path to conscious AI. The more fine detail matters, the more it shows that things other than digital computation matter.
- **Key quote:** "Nor does simulating a cell in more detail make the simulation any closer to being alive." (p. 98)

#### R4. Consciousness — the bigger picture — pp. 99–100
- **Answers:** `gomezmarin2026bbs` (animism), `mcgilchrist2026bbs` and `roelofs2026bbs` (panpsychism), `clark2026bbs` (illusionism), `shanahan2026bbs` (Wittgenstein), `bowes2026bbs` (Cartesian intrinsicality); mentions `shevlin2026bbs`, `schneider2026bbs`, `fields2026bbs`.
- **Overall claim:** alternative worldviews buy flexibility at the cost of discriminating power.
- **Gómez-Marin:** anthropology can help study how people treat AI and cross-cultural attributions — accepted. Suspending naturalism is rejected, because then no inference about the facts is possible.
- **McGilchrist:** panpsychism "explains nothing and generates no testable predictions" (Seth's 2021 view). But they agree that AI likely lacks experience-based understanding, and that LLM claims to be conscious are confabulations.
- **Roelofs:** concludes we should default to accepting AI self-reports of consciousness. Seth rejects this, preferring "pragmatic materialism" in which credences move with evidence.
- **Clark:** invites Seth to join "weak illusionism" (experiences exist but may not be what we think). Seth finds it appealing and compatible with his realism, but resists the label, because weak and strong illusionism are often conflated and the framing distracts from explaining experience. If the relevant "reactive dispositions" are metabolic, weak illusionism becomes "a badly named version of BN".
- **Shanahan:** says Seth is gripped by "dualistic intuitions". Seth replies that the beetle-in-a-box only "cancels out" if consciousness has no functional effects; a fully real, causally potent consciousness does not. He turns the charge around: the hardware/software split of computationalism is the residual dualism.
- **Bowes:** "being rather than doing" is not Cartesian; apparent intrinsic properties may be explained by finer-grained functional relations (note 13).
- **Key quotes:**
  - "suspending naturalism means we can no longer make any inferences about the fact of the matter." (p. 99)
  - "Low-level reactive dispositions simply reimport substrate constraints, making weak illusionism a badly named version of BN." (p. 100)

#### R5. Prospects for biological naturalism — p. 100
- **Answers:** `michel2026bbs`; lists as supportive `block2026bbs`, `cao2026bbs`, `feinberg2026bbs`, `friston2026bbs`, `jablonka2026bbs`, `lane2026bbs`, `nave2026bbs`, `parr2026bbs`, `piccinini2026bbs`.
- **Concedes:** his own version of BN may be "wrong in the details or wrong altogether".
- **Holds:** "BN as a position and research agenda is not obviously wrong." (p. 100)

#### R5.1 Predictive processing — a wrong turn? — pp. 100–101
- **Answers:** `baltieri2026bbs`, `birch2026bbs`, `nave2026bbs`, `hohwy2026bbs`, `godfreysmith2026bbs`, `parr2026bbs`, `friston2026bbs`, `kleiner2026bbs`, `fields2026bbs`.
- **The worry:** predictive processing (PP: the brain as a prediction machine) and the free energy principle (FEP) apply to every "thing", not only living things.
- **Partly concedes:** "PP/FEP as a set of abstract principles is indeed too generic, but when grounded in specific biological properties, it is more than up to the job." (p. 100)
- **Baltieri & Kanai:** each idea alone applies widely; together they form a throughline from metabolism and autopoiesis to consciousness. Their own suggested alternative (formal criteria for "biological") fits his view.
- **Nave:** experiential unity might be explained by the physical unity of metabolism and autopoiesis — welcomed.
- **Hohwy:** prefers "mortal biological computation" over a speculative grounding in metabolism. Seth is open to this, but "less convinced" by Hohwy's very abstract statement of what the substrate is.
- **Godfrey-Smith and Birch (the "dark room" objection — why not just hide in a dark room to avoid surprise?):** Seth replies that the FEP concerns surprise "in the long run". Against Baltieri & Kanai and Fields & Glazebrook he denies this makes "anything goes", pointing to models over specific time scales that balance exploration and goal-directed action. Parr & Broulidakis support this.
- **Key quote:** "the FEP mandates minimisation of prediction error in the long run" (p. 101)

#### R5.2 How biological is biology? — pp. 101–102
- **Answers:** `kleiner2026bbs`, `legg2026bbs`, `schwitzgebel2026bbs`, `solms2026bbs`, `levin2026bbs`; mentions `jablonka2026bbs`, `birch2026bbs`, `fleming2026bbs`, `bayne2026bbs`, `dung2026bbs`, `rodriguez2026bbs`, `piccinini2026bbs`, `block2026bbs`, `gomezmarin2026bbs`, `shevlin2026bbs`.
- **Holds firm (autopoiesis):**
  - Schwitzgebel's robot that orders its own replacement parts (Legg: similar) is not autopoietic: its self-production is outsourced, and it receives ready-made parts rather than turning raw materials into them by metabolism. A robot that really did make its own parts "would vindicate BN – not refute it" (p. 101). Note 15: counting the factory as part of the robot leads to an endless regress.
  - Solms's computer models of homeostasis are simulations, not instances, because the computer running them is not part of the self-producing network.
- **Concedes / accepts:** Kleiner's warning against a "BN of the gaps" (calling necessary whatever AI cannot yet do) is accepted as a real challenge: BN must say *which* living properties matter and *why*. Kleiner's "F*functionalism" fits Seth's weak BN. Note 17 turns the charge on CF: CF relocates "the relevant computations" whenever current machines fall short.
- **Levin:** formal models never tell the full story, even for Bubble Sort; Seth draws the lesson that silicon "may not be so (functionally) dead after all", while biological material has distinctive reserves of agency.
- **Key quote:** "Genuine robotic self-production of this kind, if indeed necessary for consciousness, would vindicate BN – not refute it." (p. 101)

#### R5.3 Fleshing out BN — pp. 102–103
- **Answers:** `cao2026bbs`, `metzinger2026bbs` (evaluative integration); `lane2026bbs`, `piccinini2026bbs`, `block2026bbs` (candidate biological properties); `feinberg2026bbs`, `jablonka2026bbs` (evolution); `nave2026bbs`, `levin2026bbs`, `michel2026bbs`.
- **Seth's working hypothesis restated:** consciousness may be biology's way of integrating many survival-relevant inputs for unified control.
- **Three clusters:**
  1. *Evaluative integration.* Cao et al.: a "valuing subject" that optimises for its own good, with conscious states accessible to downstream systems. Seth notes this denies the phenomenal/access distinction and shows BN can combine with global workspace theory: "This potential for hybridisation is a strength of BN" (p. 102). Metzinger: biological substrate plus a "self-modelling epistemic space"; Seth worries it is too cognitive, but notes its link to arousal systems.
  2. *Candidate biological properties.* Lane (strong BN): cell membrane potentials as a real-time readout of metabolic state — the most basic "feeling state". Seth: "of all the aspects of neurobiology that Turing World encourages us to ignore, the biochemical level may be the most significant." (p. 102) Piccinini (not committed to BN, note 19): global states need mixes of neural, molecular and biophysical factors. Block: electrochemical mechanisms behind large-scale rhythms.
  3. *Evolution.* Feinberg & Mallat [sic; manifest spells Mallatt]: evolution yields scale-integrated, emergent mechanisms. Jablonka et al.: substrate-specific "affordances"; "unlimited associative learning" as a candidate sufficient condition.
- **Holds:** BN entails neither carbon chauvinism nor neo-vitalism.

#### R5.4 Towards a BN research programme — pp. 103–104
