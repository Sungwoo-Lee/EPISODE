# AI consciousness — Part B: Seth's target article (version of record)

## What this part covers (plain-language entry point)

This part reviews one paper: Anil Seth's article *Conscious artificial intelligence and biological naturalism*, in the final published form in the journal *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* (BBS). In BBS, a "target article" is printed together with many short replies ("commentaries") from other researchers and a final reply from the author. Here, 50 commentaries respond to Seth. Those commentaries and Seth's reply are reviewed in other parts of this topic. This part covers only the article they all respond to.

Seth asks whether AI could be conscious, meaning whether it could have experiences, not only behave intelligently. His answer has three steps. First, our belief that AI might be conscious comes partly from human biases. Second, the idea that running the right computer program is enough for consciousness ("computational functionalism") is an assumption, and there are reasons to doubt it. Third, he argues that consciousness may depend on being alive ("biological naturalism"). His conclusion: real conscious AI is unlikely with today's technology. It becomes more plausible only if machines become more brain-like or life-like. He ends with ethical warnings, mainly about AI that only *seems* conscious.

This part also lists what changed from the preprint, and gives a numbered list of Seth's claims so that each commentary can be matched to the claim it targets.

### Phase 1: Plain overview

**The question.** As AI becomes more capable, can it also become conscious? Seth uses Thomas Nagel's definition of consciousness: there is "something it is like" to be the system (p. 2). This is different from intelligence, which is about what a system can *do*. He says no definitive answer is possible today, because scientists do not agree on what is minimally enough (the "sufficient conditions") for consciousness (p. 1). He states the aim of the paper with care: "It is not intended to show that it is impossible to build a conscious artefact, but that such artefacts may have to be closer to biological systems than allowed for by conventional digital computation." (p. 1)

**The negative case (§§2–3).** First, Seth argues that three human biases push us to see consciousness in AI. These are *anthropocentrism* (seeing the world in human terms), *human exceptionalism* (seeing humans as special and superior) and *anthropomorphism* (projecting human qualities onto non-human things). A fourth push comes from excitement about technology. Language models are especially persuasive because we treat language as the mark of the human mind. Second, he examines *computational functionalism*: the view that running the right computations is enough for consciousness. He argues that conscious AI needs two things to be true. Computational functionalism must hold. And the relevant computations must be able to run on silicon, which he calls *substrate flexibility* ("substrate" means the physical material a system is made of). He gives reasons to doubt both, without claiming to disprove either.

**The positive case (§4).** Seth then argues for *biological naturalism*: consciousness is "a property of only, but not necessarily all, living systems" (p. 1). He builds this from two directions. The first is *predictive processing*, a theory in which the brain constantly predicts its sensory input and corrects its errors. The second is the *free energy principle*, a theory about how living systems keep themselves in existence. Both lead to *autopoiesis*: the way living cells continuously rebuild themselves and keep their own boundary. For such systems, he says, there is no clean line between what they are made of and what they do.

**Scenarios, ethics, conclusion (§§5–7).** Seth sets out five scenarios for conscious AI, from "it will just appear as AI gets smarter" to "consciousness needs living material". His own view leans to the last one: "conscious AI would need to be “living” AI." (p. 12) On ethics, he separates AI that *is* conscious from AI that only *seems* conscious. Creating truly conscious AI risks new forms of suffering and "should not be an explicit goal" (p. 14). AI that seems conscious is almost certain to arrive. It raises its own problems, because the impression may persist even when we know better. His closing line: "If we sell our minds too cheaply to our machine creations, we not only overestimate them – we underestimate ourselves." (p. 1, abstract)

**Why it matters for this topic.** This is the article that 50 commentators answer. Its section numbers, its five-scenario table and its distinction between simulation and real instantiation shape how the debate is organised.

### Phase 2: The argument, step by step

The structure below follows Seth's own two-part summary at the start of §5 (pp. 10–11): "If the negative arguments are on track, conscious AI will not be possible for the current substrate-independent trajectories of AI. If the positive arguments are on track, then real artificial consciousness will only be possible if we create machines that are also in some relevant sense alive." (p. 11)

#### Step 0 — Ground rules (§1.1, pp. 1–2)

- **Intelligence** is "what a system can do" (p. 1), for example the "ability to achieve goals in a wide range of environments" (Legg & Hutter). Even an ant finding its way home through a desert is intelligent in this sense.
- **Consciousness** is having experience at all. Seth treats it as the same as "awareness". He avoids "sentience" because some people use that word for mere responsiveness and others for experience that always feels good or bad.
- There is "a fact of the matter" about whether something is conscious. Social or linguistic agreement does not decide it.
- Intelligence and consciousness are different concepts. In practice, though, they may not be independent (note 2, p. 15: plausibly they are logically and nomologically independent; "nomologically" means "given the actual laws of nature").

#### Step 1 — Why we over-attribute consciousness to AI (§2, pp. 2–3)

1. Anthropocentrism plus human exceptionalism lead us to treat human-typical abilities, especially language, as coming bundled with consciousness. Seth traces this back to Descartes.
2. Anthropomorphism makes us project these human qualities onto systems that show the abilities. It can be useful (Dennett's "intentional stance", treating a system as if it has beliefs and goals in order to predict it), but it misleads when it produces poor explanations.
3. Together these give the picture of a "royal road" (p. 2): as machines get smarter, they cross a threshold and become conscious, perhaps at artificial general intelligence. Language models are especially persuasive. Seth's example is the word "hallucinate" for model errors, which implies experience: "It would be more appropriate to say that they “confabulate.”" (p. 2)
4. A fourth push is technophilia: a difficulty in grasping exponential change, the hope of a Singularity, and the wish to upload one's mind.
5. Conclusion of the step: the bias account frees us "from assuming that making machines smarter will inevitably make them conscious, even though we may become more susceptible to judging them as being conscious" (p. 3). The step explains why the belief is common. It does not by itself show the belief is false. Seth then turns to the alternative of building consciousness in on purpose, guided by theories of consciousness (Butlin et al. 2023; Seth & Bayne 2022; Evers et al. 2025). That route, he says, still requires computational functionalism.

#### Step 2 — Fixing what "computation" means (§3.1, pp. 3–4)

- "Computational functionalism is the view that computations of some kind are sufficient to instantiate consciousness." (p. 3) It comes from *functionalism*: mind depends on what a system does (its "functional organisation", which includes its internal causal structure), not on what it is made of.
- Unless he says otherwise, "computation" means **Turing computation**: step-by-step rule-following of the kind any ordinary digital computer performs. Conventional AI runs on this kind of computation.
- Not every function is Turing-computable. Turing showed this in his response to the "Entscheidungsproblem" (Hilbert's decision problem). Other examples include "some" functions with continuous variables, random elements, or extreme sensitivity to starting conditions (p. 3). Computers can approximate some of these, but generally not exactly.
- So brains might use **non-computational functions**: continuous electromagnetic fields, precise timing, chemicals that diffuse freely, or functions that need a particular material, like digestion or metabolism. The view that mind depends on such functions is *non-computational functionalism* (Piccinini). Some parts of the mind could be computational while consciousness is not.
- "Information processing" is too vague to stand in for "computation" (p. 3; note 7, p. 16).
- Note 6 (p. 15) separates a "modest" physical Church–Turing thesis (whatever a physical system *computes*, a Turing machine can compute), which Seth accepts, from a "bold" one (whatever a physical system *does*, a Turing machine can compute), which he rejects.
- Butlin, Long and colleagues' influential report explicitly assumes computational functionalism and says its conclusions depend on it (pp. 3–4).

#### Step 3 — Why computational functionalism appeals (§3.2, p. 4)

- Functionalism was a middle path between behaviourism and identity theory (the view that a mental state simply *is* a brain state). It allowed *multiple realisability*: the same mental state can be produced in different ways. But: "Importantly, multiple realisability does not imply that any mental state can be implemented in any physical system." (p. 4)
- The specific appeal of the computational version is a "mathematical marriage of convenience" between the Turing machine and the artificial neural network, a drastic simplification of the brain (p. 4).
- The key inference Seth rejects: "The fact that mental processes can often be usefully described in terms of computation is not sufficient to conclude that the brain actually computes or that consciousness is a form of computation." (p. 4)

#### Step 4 — Substrate flexibility and the neural-replacement argument (§3.3, pp. 4–5)

- Unlimited substrate independence (any material will do) is unlikely, so Seth introduces **substrate flexibility**: conscious states are not tied to carbon-based biology and might be realised in some, but not all, other materials. "Conscious AI requires substrate flexibility to hold at least for the substrate in question: silicon." (p. 4)
- Logical link: computational functionalism is sufficient for substrate flexibility, "But it is not necessary." (p. 4) Evidence of substrate flexibility would still support computational functionalism, because it explains the flexibility.
- **The neural-replacement thought experiment.** Replace each brain cell, one by one, with a silicon part that copies its input–output behaviour exactly. Behaviour stays the same. If replacing one cell makes no difference to consciousness, "and this seems plausible" (p. 4), why would replacing all of them? So accept silicon flexibility.
- **Replies:**
  - Block: the argument begs the question. If consciousness depends on the material, something *would* change. The person need not notice, because "the change of experience does not always entail the experience of change" (p. 4).
  - Godfrey-Smith: swapping living parts for non-living ones will change how the system works inside and how it behaves, because of metabolism, electromagnetic fields and fine timing. If behaviour changes, there is no pressure to say consciousness is kept.
  - Cao: evolved mental functions are thickly bound to their physical and metabolic basis ("generative entrenchment", Wimsatt's term for features that many later features depend on). Examples: nitric oxide, a signalling chemical that crosses cell membranes freely (a silicon copy would have to detect it at an impossible number of places); spikes that protect neurons from harmful reactive oxygen molecules (Chintaluri & Vogels 2023), so spiking "cannot be decoupled from its metabolic foundations" (p. 5); bacterial movement towards food that may be inseparable from metabolism.
  - Shiller: a liberal functionalism would give a mind to a bucket of water "carved up" the right way. We need limits on what counts as a part, and current neural networks do not meet them.
- Conclusion: "These plausibility arguments do not disprove silicon substrate flexibility, but they do challenge assumptions that it must be the case." (p. 5) And: "Brains seem to be the kind of thing for which it is hard and perhaps impossible to separate what they do from what they are." (p. 5)
- Notes 13–14 (p. 16): these objections are about what is possible under the real laws of nature, not about what is logically imaginable. Seth says "just imagine we can" arguments tell us little about our world: "This is a weakness with conceivability arguments in general." (p. 16) The same applies to the zombie argument and to Chalmers's "dancing qualia" argument.

#### Step 5 — Broader kinds of computation (§3.4, p. 5)

- **Mortal computation** (Hinton; Ororbia & Friston). Ordinary software is "immortal": it outlives any one machine. This needs constant error correction, which costs energy. Brains are very energy-efficient, so if they compute at all, "these computations are likely to be mortal, which means they cannot be separated from the “hardware” (or “wetware”) which implements them." (p. 5) Conventional AI assumes immortal computation, so the needed flexibility is unlikely. Seth finds this argument especially provocative "because it is based on limitations arising from within a computational view of mind." (p. 5)
- Related ideas: Piccinini's *neural computation* (mixing continuous and discrete signals); Kirkpatrick's *biological computation*; analogue computers; neuromorphic chips (hardware built to resemble neurons); living neurons connected to computer inputs and outputs. "It is plausible that the more analogue or neuromorphic hardware becomes, the less substrate flexibility there is for the functions or computations it performs." (p. 5)
- Could a normal computer just simulate these? "the computations themselves would be different, and the simulations could not be guaranteed to be exact." (p. 5) Redefining computational functionalism to include them would weaken its motivation. And "it remains an open question as to whether brains “compute” at all." (p. 5)

#### Step 6 — Separation of scales (§3.5, pp. 5–6)

- Software works on many machines because of a strong "separation of scales": higher levels can be described without knowing lower-level detail (engineers build bridges without quantum mechanics).
- New measures make this precise. A higher-level variable is *informationally closed* if observing it predicts the higher-level outcome as well as anything else can, and *causally closed* if intervening on it determines that outcome. In both cases lower-level detail adds nothing (Barnett & Seth 2023; Rosas et al. 2024).
- Computers have this closure by design. Biology may not: "thanks to the generative entrenchment inherent in evolutionary design, they may be partially or wholly absent in biological systems." (p. 6)
- The payoff is a testable proposal: "In practice, such measures may help empirically determine whether, or to what extent, neural dynamics can be abstracted away from finer-grained levels of description." (p. 6)

#### Step 7 — Beyond computational functionalism (§3.6, pp. 6–7)

- Seth's concession: "The arguments so far do not disprove computational functionalism. But they do render it less plausible and less appealing" (p. 6).
- Other arguments are mentioned only briefly: syntax versus meaning (Searle), with an observer-dependence worry (note 19, p. 16: computational functionalism would make consciousness observer-dependent, "which it isn’t"); intentionality; Penrose's argument from Gödel's theorem.
- The brain has no sharp split between "mindware" and "wetware". A single neuron is busy maintaining itself. "The metaphor of the brain as a computer is an increasingly inconvenient fiction" (p. 6).
- Alternatives: non-computational functionalism; the dynamical-systems approach (modelling the brain with differential equations rather than computation); "4E" cognitive science (embodied, embedded, enactive, extended); neurophenomenology. Dynamical theories of consciousness include attractor dynamics, gamma synchrony, the "dynamic core" and integrated information theory, recurrent processing theory, and, on some readings, predictive processing and the free energy principle.
- Limit Seth states himself: these approaches "are in general non-committal about whether implementing the dynamical principles proposed by a given theory, in some alternative material, would be sufficient to instantiate consciousness." (p. 6) Once Turing-style independence from the material is given up, they face the same substrate-flexibility problem.

#### Step 8 — Simulation, implementation and realisation (§3.7, p. 7)

- To **simulate** a mechanism is to describe it as a Turing computation and run it on a digital computer. Anything can be simulated, perhaps only roughly.
- To **implement** it is to reproduce finer-grained or non-computational features in another way (neuromorphic engineering, synthetic biology). The limit is an exact physical copy. The line between the two can blur.
- To **realise** (instantiate) a property is to actually produce it.
- The key rule: "For a simulation of X to be guaranteed to realise X, one would have to be confident that computation is sufficient for X." (p. 7) That holds when X is itself a computation. It fails for rainstorms and digestion: "Nothing gets wet in a weather forecasting computer." (p. 7) For consciousness, it holds only if computational functionalism is true.
- By contrast, "implementing the neurobiological mechanisms underlying consciousness, at the right level of granularity, would by definition realise consciousness." (p. 7) But that is hard to do.
- Note 22 (p. 16) answers the reply "a simulated storm is wet for a simulated agent": "The objection doesn’t work because it presupposes that computation is sufficient for experience".
- Note 21 (p. 16) raises an open issue: a Turing simulation of a non-Turing process may preserve input–output behaviour while using different computations. Whether that matters depends on how strictly computational functionalism is read; "There is room for different opinions."

#### Step 9 — Intelligence is different (§3.8, p. 7)

- "Because intelligence is broadly about doing, sufficiently accurate simulations may count as realisations, even if underlying mechanisms and intrinsic properties are different." (p. 7) Language models really do mimic aspects of language; computers really do play (a version of) chess.
- Some intelligence may still be distinctively biological and escape simulation (Levin).
- "But for consciousness to be instantiated computationally in any form, one needs to assume computational functionalism." (p. 7)

#### Step 10 — Summary of the negative case (§3.9, p. 7)

Conscious AI needs computational functionalism *and* enough substrate flexibility to run on silicon. "But the functions or computations implemented by (conscious) biological systems may not be separable from their material basis." (p. 7) There are also alternatives to assuming that brains compute.

#### Step 11 — Biological naturalism from predictive processing (§4.0–4.1, pp. 7–9)

- Seth's sense of biological naturalism: "consciousness is a property of only (but not necessarily all) living systems" (p. 8). This differs from Searle's original version (consciousness as a higher-level property of neurobiology) and from *biopsychism* (all life is conscious).
- **Predictive processing:** perceptual content is the brain's "best guess" about the causes of its sensory signals. The brain builds this guess by comparing top-down predictions with incoming signals and reducing the mismatch ("prediction error"). Actions can also reduce prediction error (*active inference*). Conscious contents are the joint content of the top-down predictions, a "controlled hallucination".
- **Interoception** (sensing the body's internal state) is mainly for control, not discovery. "Instrumental inference prioritises control over discovery." (p. 8) Predictions can act as set points, supporting homeostasis and allostasis (allostasis = keeping the body stable by anticipating needs; note 26, p. 16). Hence: "the evolutionary driver for predictive processing as a general mechanism underlying conscious perception may have been a fundamental biological imperative for allostasis – for staying alive." (p. 8)
- **Phenomenology fits:** vision feels spatial and object-like, matching the job of finding out "what's there". Emotions are shaped by valence (good or bad), matching the job of regulation.
- **Epistemic biological naturalism:** "human conscious experiences can only be understood in light of our nature as living, self-sustaining organisms" (p. 8). Perhaps there is a minimal "ground state" of experience, a formless feeling of "being alive".
- **No bottom level:** self-maintenance goes all the way down to single cells. "If there is no clear boundary separating the substrate from the processes and dynamics that arise from it, then it is harder to motivate the intuition – central to computational functionalism – that the substrate does not matter." (p. 8) Seth links this to the "skin in the game" idea of Aru, Larkum & Shine (2023).
- **Autopoiesis** ("self-production", Maturana & Varela): cells rebuild their own parts and keep their own boundary. Computers and factories are *allopoietic* ("other-producing"). "For autopoietic systems, there really is no way to separate what they are from what they do." (p. 9) Note 29 (p. 16): synthetic biology comes closest to human-made autopoiesis but still only modifies cells.

#### Step 12 — Biological naturalism from the free energy principle (§4.2, pp. 9–10)

The free energy principle (Friston) is a mathematical framework for how living systems stay in existence. Seth states its steps in words; there are no equations in the article.

1. Living systems resist the spreading-out (dispersion) of their internal states that the second law of thermodynamics would otherwise bring. So they keep themselves in low-entropy states, out of equilibrium with their surroundings. "It is better to say that living systems take advantage of the second law" (p. 9): metabolism turns low-entropy food into high-entropy waste.
2. A system cannot measure its own entropy. But *variational free energy* is a measurable upper bound on it (note 33, p. 16: strictly a bound on "surprisal", how unexpected an input is under the system's model; its long-run average is entropy).
3. Under simple assumptions (Gaussian distributions, note 34), free energy can be read as sensory prediction error (strictly, prediction errors relate to free-energy *gradients*).
4. Minimising free energy brings the system's beliefs close to the best possible ones and maximises the evidence for its model (note 35: free energy bounds the "evidence lower bound", ELBO). Organisms "self-evidence" (Hohwy).
5. Thermodynamic free energy in metabolism and variational free energy in inference are linked by the Jarzynski equality (average work between states "is lower-bounded by the free energy difference between these states", p. 9), by the Landauer principle (a minimum energy cost to erase one bit), and by Sengupta et al. (accurate predictions avoid costly state changes).
6. Claim: the free energy minimisation behind metabolism, autopoiesis and perception "is not just metaphorically equivalent but is, in some physical sense, the same thing." (p. 9) Seth adds at once that the meaning of this equivalence is debated, and that the free energy principle's larger claims are debated (Aguilera et al.; Biehl et al.; Bruineberg et al.; Di Paolo et al.; Nave).
7. **Self-objection:** is predictive processing not itself a computation? Seth's reply: in living systems it is "a dynamical and (plausibly) substrate-dependent process" grounded in minimising a continuous quantity. "In this sense, predictive processing becomes a dynamical, non-computational theory" (p. 9), and computer versions are "pale shadows of the embodied and embedded processes unfolding in biological brains and bodies." (p. 10)

#### Step 13 — Supporting hints (§4.3, p. 10)

- Nick Lane: electrical potentials across bacterial membranes, maintained by metabolism, give "an integrated, valenced, and action-oriented read-out" of the organism's condition. In complex animals, mitochondria may produce electromagnetic fields that affect neurons; anaesthetics may act by disrupting them.
- Michael Levin: "xenobots" and "anthrobots", clusters of frog or human cells that organise themselves into new behaving forms. Seth's own caveat: "While there is not yet any direct connection to consciousness here" (p. 10). The lesson he draws: "Instead of computational rigidity, there is an excitable fluidity to the matter of life." (p. 10)

#### Step 14 — Answering the "this is just identity theory" worry (§4.4, p. 10)

Seth uses his "real problem" approach: as we become able to explain, predict and control properties of consciousness through mechanisms, the sense of mystery fades, as the mystery of life faded for vitalism (he notes an objection to this analogy, Chalmers 2003). "If features of being alive explain (and predict and control) features of being conscious, then a necessary relation between the two becomes more plausible." (p. 10)

#### Step 15 — Two strengths of the view (§4.5, p. 10)

- **Epistemic:** consciousness can only be *understood* in light of our living nature. "This view is the easiest to defend, though it relies on accepting predictive processing as a useful explanatory framework." (p. 10)
- **Ontological:** consciousness *depends* in a stronger way on its living substrate.
- The claim is about consciousness, not about brain function in general. "we are conscious creatures because we are living, flesh-and-blood “beast machines”" (p. 10).
- Three disavowals: not biopsychism; not carbon chauvinism (non-carbon "living" systems might qualify); no neo-vitalism (no special "life force"). Open questions left aside: what separates conscious from non-conscious living things, and what counts as "living".

#### Step 16 — Five scenarios (§5, pp. 10–13)

Seth first sets a high bar: a theory used to support conscious AI must give *true sufficient conditions*. "Not only are all current theories likely wrong (or at best incomplete), but many are restricted to describing potential mechanisms of consciousness where it is believed to exist" (p. 11). The scenarios are listed in [Table 1 below](#table-1-as-printed-in-the-version-of-record). In brief:

- **§5.1 Naïve along for the ride:** consciousness emerges as AI gets smarter. Seth: "this scenario is mostly grounded in psychological biases and in underexamined assumptions regarding computational functionalism, and so is implausible." (p. 11)
- **§5.2 Theory-based computational:** build in a computational theory of consciousness (global workspace, higher-order thought, attention schema). Still needs computational functionalism and silicon flexibility. Butlin et al. (2023) is called an excellent overview; Dung & Kersten (2024) a recent defence of computational views.
- **§5.3 Substrate-dependent computational:** computation, but only on some materials (for example mortal computation). It gives up the multiple-realisability motivation, and "Nor are there theories of consciousness which, to my knowledge, leverage this scenario." (p. 11) It includes "computational biological naturalism" (only living systems can run the relevant computations; phrase credited to Chalmers, note 37; compare Wiese's "weak computational functionalism"). Challenge: say which computations need life, and why. Note 38 (pp. 16–17) discusses Kleiner's (2024) argument and an apparent contradiction; one resolution: "And if computational functionalism is false, then conscious AI is not possible anyway." (p. 17)
- **§5.4 Substrate-dependent, weak:** consciousness depends on non-computational functions (continuous processes, fields, timing) that only some materials support. A "weak" biological naturalism and dynamical and electromagnetic-field theories sit here. "This possibility seems more appealing than the “computational biological naturalism” mentioned above" (p. 12). "Substrate flexibility, while possible in this scenario, seems challenging since the in-principle substrate independence of computation can no longer be leveraged." (p. 12)
- **§5.5 Substrate-dependent, strong:** consciousness depends on intrinsic properties of living material. This can be read functionally (the function includes the material, as digestion needs real food) or, as in Searle, by skipping functions. "My case for biological naturalism is drawn towards this scenario." (p. 12) Seth is agnostic between the two readings. If intrinsic properties turn out to be finer-grained functions, "Life would still matter, and it would do so thanks to the properties that warrant the term “life.”" (p. 12) Conclusion: "consciousness is not a matter of computation, and conscious AI would need to be “living” AI." (p. 12)
- **§5.6 Other theories:** integrated information theory (IIT) states sufficient conditions: experience is identical to a physical system's cause–effect structure at a maximum of integrated information. "If IIT is right, conventional AI is not on a path to consciousness, but there is also nothing special about life." (p. 12) Quantum theories: if quantum effects matter in biology, "this could be a contingent property of biological consciousness, rather than a necessary property of consciousness in general" (p. 13). "Quantum computational functionalism" would allow quantum, but not conventional, computers.
- **§5.7 Embodiment and embeddedness:** how much bodies matter depends on the scenario. If computation is enough, virtual or robotic bodies are enough. Under substrate dependence, bodies may be needed. "physically embodied robots could have energetic and structural integrity requirements entailing real, though non-biological, forms of homeostasis and allostasis (but not autopoiesis)." (p. 13) Cerebral organoids (lab-grown brain tissue, usually without a body) are a test case. AI "agents" may have only weak, observer-dependent agency, "in contrast to the strong, endogenous forms available to biological systems" (p. 13). Note 40 (p. 17): embodiment may be needed for real understanding in language models, apart from consciousness.
- **§5.8 Summary:** Seth argues for the strong flavour, not because of carbon or vitalism. "just as there can be wind without a storm, there can be life without consciousness." (p. 13) "My case is not watertight and may well be wrong in the details or wrong altogether." (p. 13) "Even if biological naturalism turns out to be false as an account of necessary conditions, a biological perspective may still be needed to understand the nature of consciousness in humans and other animals." (p. 13)

#### Step 17 — Ethics (§6, pp. 13–15)

- **Starting point (§6.0):** we do not know how to make conscious AI or how to make sure it does not happen; we lack reliable tests beyond clear human (and perhaps some animal) cases. Seth separates *real* artificial consciousness from AI that *seems* conscious: "The latter are more likely than the former, and different ethical issues arise in each case." (p. 13)
- **Real artificial consciousness (§6.1):** "Real artificial consciousness may be very unlikely or impossible." (p. 13) But if it arose, by design or accident, "ethical catastrophe awaits". Consciousness gives moral status, so "Creating real artificial consciousness risks a mass inauguration of new forms of suffering." (p. 13) We might not recognise this suffering. Designing out bad feelings cannot be guaranteed, and any consciousness may give moral status. The AI might have its own interests, which complicates "value alignment" (making AI act in line with human values) both practically and morally. "The development of real artificial consciousness for its own sake should not be an explicit goal." (p. 14) We should update our confidence as tests improve, and steer development away. Tension: functions linked to consciousness (fast generalisation, metacognition, learning from little data) may be useful. Seth's line: build them in ways "sufficiently different" from biology; where the line falls depends on one's theory. The same caution applies to scientists' computer models of consciousness. Notes: Garland test (note 41), Godfrey-Smith's narrow and broad sentientism (note 42), frustrating the preferences of even unconscious AI and Bengio's "oracle" idea (note 43), Metzinger's moratorium (note 44).
- **Conscious-seeming AI (§6.2):** "it is almost certain that AI systems will soon give us compelling impressions of being conscious." (p. 14) Like the Müller-Lyer illusion (Figure 1, p. 14), where two equal lines keep looking unequal, "Sufficiently advanced AI systems may give rise to cognitively impenetrable illusions of consciousness." (p. 14) ("Cognitively impenetrable" = not changed by what we know.) Harms: exploiting psychological weaknesses; loss of privacy. The "Westworld" dilemma: if we care for systems we believe are not conscious, we distort the circle of moral concern; "If we decide to not care about these systems, we risk brutalising our own minds." (p. 14) Avatars of dead relatives are a dystopian case. Possible benefits: easier use, accessibility, therapy, loneliness. Treating models as role-playing may help a little. Conscious-seeming AI is not inevitable: "Alternative futures are possible, in which conscious-seeming AIs are restricted to well-justified use cases" (p. 15). Note 45 (p. 17): models may become so good that they stop seeming humanlike.

#### Step 18 — Conclusions (§7, p. 15)

- "Consciousness will not just come along for the ride as AI gets smarter" (p. 15).
- Symmetry claim: "Simply noting that all known examples of conscious systems are biological and alive suggests that biological naturalism is no more speculative than computational functionalism." (p. 15)
- Research agenda: the links between predictive processing, the free energy principle, autopoiesis and the thermodynamics of metabolism; whether neuromorphic and synthetic-biology technologies change the picture.
- "Wrong side of history?" Seth's answer: "Our psychological biases are more likely to lead to false positives than false negatives." (p. 15) Compared with animals, "LLMs may be more similar to us in ways that do not matter for consciousness and less similar in ways that do." (p. 15)
- Closing image: *Frankenstein*: "the real Promethean sin of Frankenstein was not endowing his creature with life but with consciousness." (p. 15)

### Reviewer's notes

These are the reviewer's observations, not Seth's claims. They point to places where commentators may press; they do not judge the argument.

- **Reviewer's note — Table 1 versus §5.6 on IIT.** The table lists IIT among the examples for scenario 4, with the caveat "for cause-effect structure, not function". The §5.6 text says IIT does "not fall naturally along this continuum". Both statements are in the article; a reader should cite both.
- **Reviewer's note — what kind of claim §5 makes.** §5.0 requires a theory to give true *sufficient* conditions for consciousness. Biological naturalism, as corrected in §5.8, is a claim about *necessary* conditions. The article does not say what would be sufficient for consciousness in a living system (§4.5 leaves open what makes some living things conscious). This is consistent with Seth's stated aims, but it means the positive case limits where consciousness can occur rather than predicting where it will.
- **Reviewer's note — the step from "dynamical" to "non-computational" (§4.2).** Seth's answer to his own objection depends on reading predictive processing as a continuous physical process that a computer can only approximate. Note 25 says predictive processing in general is neutral on this. The article flags the question but does not argue it further.
- **Reviewer's note — relevance to this project.** §5.7's middle case (robots with real, non-biological homeostasis and allostasis, but no autopoiesis) is the closest the article comes to artificial agents like this project's, which receive an internal damage signal (nociception) and must keep internal variables in range. Seth places such systems between the scenarios rather than deciding their status.
