# AI consciousness — Part C4: twelve open peer commentaries on Seth's "Conscious artificial intelligence and biological naturalism"

## What this part covers (plain-language entry point)

In 2025 the neuroscientist Anil Seth published a long article in the journal *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* (BBS). He argues that today's AI is very unlikely to be conscious. His reasons: consciousness may not be a matter of running the right computer program (he doubts "computational functionalism", the view that the right computation is enough for a mind), and it may instead depend on properties of living things, such as keeping the body alive and stable (he calls his view "biological naturalism"). BBS then printed short replies ("commentaries") from other researchers.

This part reviews twelve of those replies, numbered e354 to e365. They pull in different directions. Two (Ramstead, Wiese) offer Seth extra support. Two (Richards and Agüera y Arcas; Schwitzgebel) argue that the living processes Seth names are themselves computational or can be built in ordinary machines. Solms argues that Seth's own position is not really "biological". Roelofs, Shevlin and Schlicht say that even if Seth is right about us, other forms of consciousness may exist, or we cannot know. Shanahan rejects the shared starting point that there is a hidden fact about consciousness. Schneider doubts that chatbots are conscious, but for different reasons than Seth. Rodriguez and Farahany propose experiments and policy steps.

**Source:** `docs/project/references/ai_consciousness/sources/Seth 2025 - Conscious artificial intelligence and biological naturalism (BBS with commentaries and response).pdf`, PDF pages 75–92 (the PDF page numbers equal the journal's printed page numbers in this file). Seth's own Author's Response begins on p. 92 and is reviewed separately (batch D).

**Related reviews in the sibling topic:** the Seth *preprint* is reviewed in `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/computational_functionalism_lit_review_C_biological_naturalism.md` (§5); Butlin et al. 2023, which several commentators discuss, is reviewed in `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/computational_functionalism_lit_review_D_theories_and_tests.md` (§6).

## e354. Ramstead (2026) — Computational neuroscience versus the Computational Theory of Mind: Why their difference matters for the possibility of artificial consciousness [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `ramstead2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 75–76 · **Field:** neuroscience / free-energy-principle modelling (UCL Queen Square Institute of Neurology)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.1 (computational functionalism defined), §3.2 (its appeal), §3.7 (simulation versus realisation); it describes itself as complementing Seth's "negative" arguments against computational functionalism.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** supports — it removes one popular line of evidence for AI consciousness, by an argument that does not depend on whether computational functionalism is true.
- **The argument in plain words:** Scientists can model almost anything on a computer. A model of a planet's orbit computes integrals, but nobody thinks the planet computes them. So the fact that a process can be modelled computationally says nothing about whether the process *is* a computation. Ramstead separates two things: *computational neuroscience* (a method that models the brain with computers) and the *Computational Theory of Mind* (a metaphysical claim that thinking is computing; "metaphysical" here means a claim about what something really is, not about how we describe it). To say a system "implements" a computation is only to say there is a systematic mapping between a physical description and an algorithm. Frameworks such as the free energy principle can show when the two descriptions are interchangeable, but this is an equivalence between descriptions ("epistemic"), not a fact about the thing itself. Hence even a complete computational model of conscious experience would give no evidence for computational functionalism, and so no evidence for conscious AI.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** a clean modelling-versus-metaphysics distinction, applied to the free energy principle itself — the framework Seth relies on is presented as a tool for relating descriptions, not as proof that brains compute. It suggests that part of the plausibility of conscious AI comes from confusing the two.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "when one simulates the orbit of Jupiter around the sun, one does not usually assume that either Jupiter itself or its orbit is literally computing an integral." (p. 75)
  - "the availability of computational models of consciousness provides no evidence for computational functionalism about consciousness" (p. 76)
- **Builds on:** Putnam (1967, 1975); Fodor (1975); Horsman et al. (2014) on when a physical system computes; Chirimuuta (2024) on analogical explanation; Ramstead et al. (2023) on Bayesian mechanics.

## e355. Richards & Agüera y Arcas (2026) — Life is inherently computational, and consciousness probably is too [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `richards2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 76–77 · **Field:** AI-and-ML and neuroscience (Mila, McGill, CIFAR; Google; Santa Fe Institute). Competing interests as declared: both authors are Google employees.

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.1 and §3.4 (what counts as computation), §3.3 (substrate independence), §3.7 (simulation versus realisation), §4.1–§4.3 (prediction, homeostasis, autopoiesis), and the conclusion (§5/§7) that real AI consciousness is unlikely on current trajectories.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** rejects — the living processes Seth ties to consciousness are themselves computations, so his theory is "in fact a computational theory".
- **The argument in plain words:** Seth is right that consciousness is grounded in life-related processes: prediction, homeostasis (keeping internal variables in a safe range) and autopoiesis (a system continually producing and maintaining itself). But his idea of computation is too narrow. Besides the Turing machine there is von Neumann's Universal Constructor, a machine made of the same symbols it reads and writes, and it is mathematically equivalent to a Turing machine. Kleene's recursion theorem shows programs can access their own code, which allows self-replication. Von Neumann saw that living things need a Universal Constructor to grow, maintain and copy themselves. So any autopoietic system is computational, and "life is inherently computational". Substrate does matter in engineering practice (modern AI needed GPUs), and whether an artificial kidney is "really" a kidney is a question of words. Evolution and technology both swap implementations freely (wings, eyes, flight). Consciousness, like flight, is functional: it supports self-care, planning, introspection, theory of mind and cooperation, which can be done purely in information. The authors speculate some current AI may be partly conscious and think conscious AI is likely.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** an alternative, *embodied* definition of computation (the Universal Constructor) under which autopoiesis counts as computing; a graded view of embodiment (prediction can be disembodied, homeostasis is more embodied, autopoiesis most); a direct challenge to the "philosophical zombie" possibility raised by Schneider and Chalmers.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Since a Universal Constructor is a computer, it follows that any system capable of autopoiesis must be computational." (p. 77)
  - "a perfect “simulation” of consciousness is consciousness, just as a perfect “simulation” of a calculator is a calculator." (p. 77)
  - "We would even speculate that some current AI systems, given their abilities to predict, model themselves and others, and self-correct, may already be partly conscious." (p. 77)
- **Builds on / argues against:** Church (1936), Turing (1936, 1937), Kleene (1938), von Neumann & Burks (1966), Adleman (1994), Graziano (2013); argues against Schneider (2021) and Chalmers (2022) on silicon zombies.
- **Reviewer's note:** the step from "living systems can carry out arbitrary computations" and "a self-replicating system contains a constructor" to "every process that matters in autopoiesis is a computation" is the load-bearing move; Seth's §3.7 distinction between simulating and realising a process (taken up by Wiese, e365) is the point where the two sides would meet.

## e356. Rodriguez & Farahany (2026) — Towards evidence-based governance of potentially conscious technologies [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `rodriguez2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 78–80 (references run onto p. 80) · **Field:** ethics-and-policy and law (Duke Cognitive Futures Lab; Duke Law). Declared interest: Farahany is on the advisory board of OpenBCI.

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §4.1 (predictive processing and allostasis), §4.3 (autopoiesis), §3.3 (the brain's "hardware" and "software" being inseparable / causal closure), and §6.1 (ethics of potentially conscious systems). It also engages Butlin et al. (2023), which Seth discusses.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — takes no side on the truth of biological naturalism, but argues the debate can and should be turned into testable predictions, and that policy should meanwhile respect all mainstream theories.
- **The argument in plain words:** Experts disagree, so credence in AI consciousness should be treated as a probability. Following Long et al. (2024), the chance an AI is conscious equals the credence that computational functionalism is true times the chance it is conscious *if* functionalism is true. The indicators proposed by Butlin et al. only affect the second term. So finding them in an AI will, the authors argue, *increase* disagreement among people with different views about functionalism. To reduce that disagreement, the authors propose experiments that target the metaphysical assumption directly, modelled on the recent adversarial collaboration between rival theories (Ferrante et al., 2025). Three examples: (1) vary how unpredictable the "punishment" stimuli are for brain organoids (lab-grown 3-D neural tissue) learning the game Pong, to test whether predictive processing serves allostasis (anticipatory regulation of the body); (2) compare organoids that make their own energy with organoids fed energy from outside, to test whether autopoiesis matters for recurrent processing; (3) decode speech with a neuroprosthesis in three conditions and see whether brain-wide "ignition" (the global workspace signature) collapses when motor parts are offloaded to the device. They admit committed partisans could explain away any result and recommend pre-registration.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** a formal probabilistic framing; the claim that consciousness indicators can raise expert disagreement; a concrete, organoid- and neuroprosthesis-based research agenda; a governance recommendation; the reminder that the question also arises for lab-grown human tissue, not just AI.
- **The formulas** (the PDF text extraction garbled the symbols; reconstructed here from the variable definitions on p. 78, with C = the AI system is conscious, CF = computational functionalism is true, Tᵢ = theory i is true, Q = a metaphysical statement such as "mind is substrate independent"):

$$
P(C) = P(CF)\times\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(T_i \mid CF)\times P(C \mid T_i, CF)
$$

$$
P(T_i) = P(Q)\,P(T_i \mid Q) + P(\lnot Q)\,P(T_i \mid \lnot Q)
$$

- **Key quotes:**
  - "Evidence supporting computational indicators of consciousness will paradoxically increase disagreement unless we first address underlying metaphysical disputes" (p. 78)
  - "ground policy decisions in the plausibility of all mainstream theories of consciousness." (p. 79)
- **Builds on:** Long et al. (2024); Butlin et al. (2023); Ferrante et al. (2025); Kagan et al. (2022); Osaki et al. (2024); Metzger et al. (2023); Oreskes (2021).

## e357. Roelofs (2026) — Substrates, computations, and abundant consciousness: Commentary on Anil Seth [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `roelofs2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 80–81 · **Field:** philosophy-of-mind (University of Texas at Arlington)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.3 (substrate independence/flexibility), the overall conclusion (§5, §7) that AI consciousness is unlikely, and the treatment of AI self-reports in §2 and §6.2.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — sets aside whether functionalism or biological naturalism is true and argues that artificial consciousness is still likely, "albeit in strange and unfamiliar forms" (p. 80).
- **The argument in plain words:** First objection: rejecting substrate independence only shows that a system made of different stuff would be *differently* conscious, not that it would lack consciousness. Its experiences might differ in quality, structure or valence (pleasantness), but still exist. Second objection: suppose we meet a system unlike us in material but like us in function, which says it is conscious and talks about what it likes and dislikes. If we are unsure which theory is right, the default should be to accept its self-reports. Reasons: precaution, the "Copernican" point that our own substrate should not be assumed special, and the fact that we credit other humans and animals with consciousness without a theory. The default can be defeated, for example if the system was built to mimic human talk about consciousness — which Roelofs thinks is the strongest current reason to doubt present AI, and which would apply equally to a biological system with the same history. Overriding the default by theory requires high confidence in that theory, not just preferring it. Behind all this lies the question of whether consciousness is abundant or sparse in nature; Roelofs favours panpsychism (the view that consciousness is a basic and widespread feature of matter).
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** the distinction between *absence* of consciousness and *different* consciousness; a default-acceptance rule for self-ascriptions; the "history-based defeater" (a system's design history can cancel the evidential value of its words); the abundant-versus-sparse framing; replacing "is it conscious?" with "what sort of consciousness is present?".
- **Key quotes:**
  - "we need a compelling argument to doubt a system’s self-ascriptions of consciousness, but not to accept them." (p. 80)
  - "we should still default to taking differences of substrate as indicating differences in consciousness, not its absence." (p. 80)
  - "what sort of consciousness is present in this system?" (p. 81)
- **Builds on:** Long et al. (2024); Schwitzgebel & Pober (manuscript) on the Copernican and mimicry arguments; Andrews & Birch (2023); Birch, Schnell & Clayton (2020); Chalmers (2015) and Seager (2006) on panpsychism.

## e358. Schlicht (2026) — Invited commentary: assessment or attribution of consciousness? [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `schlicht2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 81–82 · **Field:** philosophy-of-mind (Ruhr-University Bochum)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.1 and §3.3 (computational functionalism, medium independence), §4.1 (Seth's claim that predictive processing is embedded in its biological context; Schlicht quotes Seth's manuscript pp. 8 and 13), and Seth's engagement with the Butlin et al. (2023) indicator programme.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — agrees computational functionalism deserves scrutiny and that biology currently has "a slight advantage", but argues neither view can be shown true.
- **The argument in plain words:** Computational functionalism needs neural computation to be *medium independent* (not constrained by the material it runs in). Digital computation is medium independent by definition, but it is open whether neural computation is: Maley argues it is analog and therefore medium dependent; Piccinini says it is its own kind yet medium independent, which Schlicht calls premature. For the Butlin programme to work, theories such as Global Neuronal Workspace must supply markers that apply to non-biological systems. But in AI, the markers we use for humans and animals are absent (neural and first-person markers), ambiguous (reports, behaviour) or question-begging (computational markers). A workspace in a machine might give only "Ersatz broadcasting" (Block). Neuroscientific theories are well supported *as biological theories*, not as computational ones. Equally, Seth's reading of predictive processing as tied to life "seems idiosyncratic" to Schlicht: its computational core may be detachable. We have no test for medium dependence, so we cannot *assess* consciousness in AI; we can only *attribute* it, as Dennett's "intentional stance" does (treating a system as if it has a mind because that helps predict it).
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** the medium-independence literature (Maley, Piccinini, Drayson, Chirimuuta, Cao); a three-way classification of why markers fail for AI; the point that computational markers "do not pile up"; the practical-impossibility worry (a silicon brain equivalent might need "data centers the size of Asia"); the assessment-versus-attribution distinction.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "We are not epistemically justified to judge whether Biological Naturalism or Computational Functionalism is true." (p. 81)
  - "computational markers do not pile up and make conscious AI more likely." (p. 82)
  - "Possibility in principle is unverifiable if conscious AI requires data centers the size of Asia." (p. 82)
- **Builds on / argues against:** Butlin et al. (2023); Piccinini (2009, 2020); Maley (2023, 2025); Drayson (2025); Cao (2022); Chirimuuta (2022, 2025); Block (2008); Dehaene (2014); Dennett (1987); Bayne et al. (2024); Birch (2024); Schlicht (2025) on predictive processing.

## e359. Schneider (2026) — The error theory of LLM consciousness: there is no evidence that standard LLMs are conscious [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `schneider2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 83–84 · **Field:** philosophy-of-mind and AI (Florida Atlantic University, Center for the Future of AI, Mind and Society)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §2 (why we might wrongly think AI is conscious), §3.8 (intelligence versus consciousness), §6.2 (conscious-seeming AI); it explicitly offers reasons for scepticism that "do not depend on Biological Naturalism" (p. 83).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** extends — agrees that standard large language models (LLMs) are probably not conscious, but on independent grounds, and widens the list of AI systems that are serious candidates.
- **The argument in plain words:** An LLM trained on vast human text becomes a "crowdsourced neocortex": its internal concept map mirrors the people whose data trained it. That explains why, as models scale, their talk about consciousness and emotions sounds more human. This is an "error theory" — an account of why people wrongly conclude chatbots have inner lives. So consciousness-like behaviour, and even functional structures resembling a global workspace inside an LLM, should not count as evidence; Schneider rejects Birch's proposal that implicitly learned workspaces make an LLM a "sentience candidate". She still thinks the question should be tested, for example by giving LLMs consciousness tests while "boxed in" before they see human data about minds. Neuromorphic systems (chips built to work like neurons) are a "grey area". Biocomputers made partly of living neurons must be taken very seriously. And consciousness might arise at any level of the hardware-software "stack", including quantum effects.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** the "crowdsourced neocortex" error theory; the "boxing-in" test proposal and her ACT test; the idea that the *same* LLM might be conscious on one hardware and not on another; attention to neuromorphic, organoid-hybrid and quantum systems.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "an explanation of why people erroneously conclude that chatbots have inner lives." (p. 83)
  - "Because we know that neurons are the “right stuff” as a substrate of consciousness, we must take biocomputing very seriously." (p. 84)
  - "AI consciousness could arise at any level of the stack." (p. 84)
- **Builds on / argues against:** Schneider (2019, 2024); Birch (2024) (argues against); Anthropic circuit-tracing work (2025); Wei et al. (2022); Davies et al. (2021) on neuromorphic computing; Schneider & Bailey (forthcoming) "Superpsychism".

## e360. Schwitzgebel (2026) — Minimal autopoiesis in an AI system [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `schwitzgebel2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 84–85 · **Field:** philosophy-of-mind (University of California, Riverside)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §4.3 (autopoiesis as a mark of life relevant to consciousness) and §5 (Seth's brief mention of "living AI" as a route to real artificial consciousness).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** rejects (the autopoiesis step) — autopoiesis is a high-level functional concept, so requiring it does not rule out consciousness in standard computational systems.
- **The argument in plain words:** Imagine a solar-powered robot that moves toward light when its battery is low and uses predictive processing. It detects broken parts, orders replacements, checks them for compatibility, rejects fakes and installs them; redundant processors let it swap even its central processors. A plastic shell forms its boundary and is repaired when cracked. Schwitzgebel calls this minimally autopoietic: it maintains itself, keeps a boundary, renews its parts, and draws on outside energy. He answers objections: humans also depend on nutrients from outside; autopoiesis need not be multilevel; periodic rather than continuous replacement should not matter, and a continual-repair version can be imagined; reproduction is not needed for autopoiesis according to Maturana and Varela. He even suggests an ordinary desktop computer is arguably minimally autopoietic. He invites Seth either to say which missing feature matters and why, or to accept that such a robot could be the "living AI" he mentions. Maturana and Varela themselves said a system's organisation does not fix the properties of its components.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** a concrete engineering thought experiment; a list of candidate requirements (continuity, multilevel structure, self-manufacture, reproduction) each argued to be either inessential or buildable; textual evidence that the originators of "autopoiesis" allowed substrate flexibility.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "I suggest that standard AI systems could be, and perhaps some already are, minimally autopoietic." (p. 84)
  - "Autopoiesis is a high-level, functional concept. Nothing in the concept appears to require implementation in a particular substrate." (p. 85)
- **Builds on:** Maturana & Varela (1980), quoted on p. 85; Luisi (2014).

## e361. Shanahan (2026) — Metaphysical confusion and biological naturalism [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `shanahan2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 85–86 · **Field:** AI-and-ML and philosophy (Department of Computing, Imperial College London). Declared interest: part-time Google employee and Alphabet shareholder.

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §1 (Seth's stated assumption that there is a fact of the matter about whether something is conscious, not settled by social or linguistic consensus), and Seth's remark that we know our own consciousness and infer it in others; by extension the whole metaphysical framing, including §4.4.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** rejects the framing, keeps the contribution — Seth is "in the grip of dualistic intuitions", but his paper can be read as a valid proposal about how to talk about AI.
- **The argument in plain words:** How we use the word "consciousness" is a matter of social and linguistic agreement. The Wittgensteinian move is to stop asking what consciousness "is" and ask how words like "consciousness" and "awareness" are used. Once usage is spelled out, no philosophical problem remains; science can still study the link between word use, behaviour and brain processes, and its findings change usage (Shanahan's example: UK concern for farmed octopuses). Advanced AI puts this usage under pressure, and society will settle it by drawing on what is public: behaviour and the mechanisms behind it. The idea that consciousness is a private thing known only in one's own case is dualism (the view that mind is separate from the physical), and Wittgenstein's "beetle in a box" shows that such a private thing "cancels out" of language. Buddhist philosophy (Nāgārjuna) offers similar remedies. Seth's paper is therefore best read as a contribution to a society-wide conversation about how the language of consciousness should treat AI.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** a deflationary, use-based alternative to the realist starting point that Seth and most commentators share; Wittgenstein's private language remarks and Buddhist philosophy as resources; the suggestion that the AI question is about adapting shared concepts, not discovering a hidden fact.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "having spelt out how these words are used, there are no residual philosophical problems." (p. 86)
  - "it can equally be framed as an argument for how we should use words like “consciousness.”" (p. 86)
- **Builds on:** Wittgenstein (1953/2009), §§293, 309; Garfield (2015); Siderits & Katsura (2013); Shanahan (2010, 2024).

## e362. Shevlin (2026) — Aeroplanes also fly: Analytic functionalism and the possibility of machine consciousness [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `shevlin2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 87–88 · **Field:** philosophy and AI ethics (Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence, Cambridge)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.1 (computational functionalism), §3.8 (intelligence), §2 (the intentional stance leading to naïve attributions), and the positive case in §4 that consciousness depends on life's machinery.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — accepts that Seth undercuts the idea that *humanlike* consciousness comes "for free" with intelligence, but denies that features of biological consciousness rule out other forms in machines.
- **The argument in plain words:** Flight evolved separately in insects, pterosaurs, birds and bats, and each case is explained with fine biological detail. Yet aeroplanes and rockets fly too. Likewise, even if *biological* consciousness can only be explained with substrate-level detail, other kinds of consciousness lacking those details may exist. One might object that "flight" is a looser word than "consciousness"; Shevlin grants some difference but keeps the analogy. To unify such different cases he points to *analytic functionalism*: mental-state words are folk-psychology terms we use to predict and explain intelligent, goal-directed agents, not states defined by Turing computation. This need not deny that consciousness is real: it may be a "real pattern" (Dennett) with explanatory power. And "deep realism" about consciousness should itself be questioned because it invites dualism (he cites Shanahan, 2024). Current misattributions to LLMs reflect quirks like hallucination, not a basic failure of the intentional stance.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** the flight analogy for pluralism about kinds of consciousness; analytic functionalism (Lewis) as a third option between computational functionalism and biological naturalism; an ecological view of consciousness as a way of understanding how agents move through and interact with the world.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "it is premature to say that they will never instantiate consciousness in any form." (p. 87)
  - "we should be cautious not to extrapolate too quickly from contingent features of its realisation in beings like us to the wider and more inscrutable space of possible minds." (p. 88)
- **Builds on:** Lewis (1975); Dennett (1987, 1991); Dijkstra (1986); Shanahan (2024); Shevlin (2024, 2025).

## e363. Silberstein (2026) — Why current A.I. can't be conscious: It's not about predictive processing or the free energy principle; it's about the lack of self-organization and multiscale complexity that enable adaptability [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `silberstein2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 88–89 · **Field:** philosophy of science and cognitive science (Elizabethtown College)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3 overall (agrees with the rejection of computational functionalism), §4.1 (predictive processing, "controlled hallucination"), §4.2 (Seth's claim that free-energy minimisation in metabolism, autopoiesis and perception is "in some physical sense the same thing"), and §4.4 (the real problem versus the hard problem).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** supports the conclusion, rejects the positive grounds — AI is unlikely to be conscious, but the reason is lack of self-organisation and multiscale complexity, not lack of predictive processing or free-energy minimisation.
- **The argument in plain words:** Silberstein agrees with Seth's rejection of computational functionalism. But predictive processing runs into the hard problem (why any experience accompanies a function), and Seth admits it can be used as an ordinary algorithm. Setting the hard problem aside is fine for finding neural correlates, but not when deciding whether an artefact or alien is conscious; and if brains *cause* consciousness through active inference, Seth faces "spooky strong emergence". Predictive processing also does not explain why particular sensory signals come with particular experiences; that mapping is one-to-many. The identity of free-energy minimisation with predictive processing is "a category mistake": simple physical systems and single cells obey the free energy principle without being conscious. What conscious organisms share is that they are self-organising embodied agents directly coupled to their environment, with multiscale networks showing plasticity, degeneracy (different parts able to do the same job) and redundancy. The brain is a set of integrated multiscale dynamical networks, not a computer or "prediction machine". The substrate need not be biological, but it must support the same adaptability and learning.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** a different list of the biological properties that matter (plasticity, degeneracy, robustness, multiscale self-organisation); the claim that cognition and consciousness are inseparable, so a true AGI would be conscious and vice versa; an internal-consistency charge that Seth's brain-centred view cannot borrow enactivist language.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Minimization of free energy, active inference, and being conscious can all come apart." (p. 89)
  - "Such an identification is a category mistake." (p. 89)
  - "Such embodied agents need not be made of biological wetware, but it would have to be some other substrate that also maximizes the capacity for adaptability and learning" (p. 89)
- **Builds on:** Silberstein (2021, 2022, 2023); Silberstein & Chemero (2012); ecological psychology and enactivism (named, not cited individually).

## e364. Solms (2026) — Real artificial consciousness is, of course, incompatible with biological naturalism [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `solms2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 89–90 · **Field:** neuroscience and psychology (University of Cape Town). Declared interests: scientific advisory boards of Conscium and PRISM; research funded in part by Conscium. Solms was also a BBS reviewer of the target article.

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §5 (scenarios for real artificial consciousness — Seth's statement that it would need to be "living AI, though not necessarily carbon-based"), §4.3 (autopoiesis; Seth's remark that robots could have non-biological homeostasis and allostasis "but not autopoiesis"), and §6.1 (Seth's view that building real artificial consciousness should not be a goal).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** rejects — Seth's position is internally ambiguous, and once clarified its commitments are substrate independent.
- **The argument in plain words:** If a conscious AI need not be carbon-based, it need not be biological, which "negates the essence" of biological naturalism and sets aside Seth's points about neurons and metabolism. What remains — active inference, cybernetics, autopoiesis, the free energy principle — is not substrate dependent. Seth already grants non-biological homeostasis and allostasis to robots, so Solms only needs to show autopoiesis is substrate independent. He cites Friston's (2013) virtual self-organising systems: when their sensory, active and internal parts were "lesioned", the systems fell apart, which Friston described as evidence of autopoiesis. Solms reports that his team is building an agent driven by active inference that tries to meet its own existential needs, so it has endogenous goals and "skin in the game". Against Seth's ethical warning, he argues that consciousness science needs falsifiable tests, best done by trying to build conscious agents. His team is drafting agreed criteria and has publicly committed to stop once they are met; and it is better that pure scientists, not commercial interests, do this work.

- **Key quotes:**
  - "Surely this negates the essence of the term “biological naturalism,”" (p. 89)
  - "there is nothing substrate dependent about active inference, cybernetics, autopoiesis, and the free energy principle" (p. 90)
  - "So, it is best that it is created by pure scientists like us, “for its own sake,” rather than by commercial interests" (p. 90)
- **Builds on:** Friston (2013); Haugeland (1998); Chalmers (1995); Solms (2021); Solms & Shock (in preparation).

## e365. Wiese (2026) — What can the free energy principle tell us about artificial consciousness? [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `wiese2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 90–92 (title and author on p. 90; abstract and body on pp. 91–92; the manifest's start page is 91) · **Field:** philosophy-of-mind (Ruhr University Bochum)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §4.1 (predictive processing entangled with homeostasis), §4.2 (free-energy minimisation "in some physical sense" the same as homeostatic processes), §3.7 (simulation versus realisation); Wiese also cites Seth's "sect. 4.0" on avoiding biopsychism and a "sect. 4.5" on multisensory integration — section numbers of the version he read, not in the section list used here.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** extends — finds a gap in Seth's inference from the free energy principle to substrate dependence, and supplies an extra premise that closes it.
- **The argument in plain words:** Seth's route to substrate dependence has two steps: in living things, predictive processing is entangled with homeostasis; and under the free energy principle, minimising free energy *is* homeostasis. But this link may be contingent: evolution favours reusing life-sustaining machinery for cognition, while engineered systems need not. Seth also avoids biopsychism (the view that all living things are conscious), so some homeostasis is not consciousness. The free energy principle gives two equivalent descriptions of some self-organising systems, one physical and one informational, and the informational computations can run on digital computers. So, as it stands, the argument must *presuppose* substrate dependence. Wiese's remedy: the difference between simulating and realising a phenomenon lies in which causal relations are present. In Conway's Game of Life, "cells" die from overcrowding in the model, but in the computer no cell-representing part causes another's change; by hand, the human moving tokens is the cause. Consciousness is causally effective, and in conventional hardware the internal causal relations among mental processes are represented, not reproduced. The free energy principle can identify those causal relations, so it does support substrate dependence without begging the question.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** a diagnosis that Seth's free-energy argument is circular without an extra premise; a causal criterion for the simulation–realisation distinction, independently motivated (Kohár, 2024, on "virtual causation"); the Game of Life illustration; a graded verdict for robots with conventional hardware (they instantiate the information processing and affect behaviour, but lack the internal causal structure, so at most simulate experience).
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Instead of justifying a form of substrate-dependence, it must presuppose that consciousness is substrate-dependent." (p. 91)
  - "a mere simulation will not replicate all relevant causal relations that are required for realising the phenomenon." (p. 91)
  - "the free energy principle does support the view that consciousness is substrate-dependent, without begging the question." (p. 92)
- **Builds on:** Wiese (2024); Kohár (2024); Gardner (1970).

**Direct clashes inside this batch.**

1. **Is "simulating" a process the same as having it?** Richards & Agüera y Arcas say a perfect simulation of consciousness *is* consciousness, as a simulated calculator is a calculator. Ramstead says the ability to model a process computationally is no evidence that it computes. Wiese says a simulation lacks the causal relations of the thing simulated (his Game of Life example). This is the central disagreement of the batch, and it maps onto Seth's §3.7.
2. **Is autopoiesis substrate dependent?** Three commentaries say no, by different routes: Richards & Agüera y Arcas (autopoiesis requires a Universal Constructor, which is a computer), Schwitzgebel (a self-repairing robot is minimally autopoietic) and Solms (Friston's virtual systems show autopoiesis; Solms's team is building such an agent). Wiese's causal criterion, and Ramstead's modelling-versus-metaphysics distinction, are the main resources in this batch for resisting them: the question becomes whether a robot or simulation *realises* autopoiesis or only represents it.
3. **What does the free energy principle (FEP) establish?** Wiese: it supports substrate dependence, but only with an added causal premise. Silberstein: identifying free-energy minimisation with predictive processing is a category mistake, since non-conscious systems obey the FEP. Solms: the FEP is substrate independent, which undermines Seth. Ramstead: the FEP relates descriptions and says nothing on its own about what a system really is.
4. **Is there a hidden fact about consciousness at all?** Shanahan says no (use-based deflationism), and Shevlin partly agrees (questioning "deep realism", citing Shanahan). Roelofs, Schlicht, Wiese and Rodriguez & Farahany all treat it as a factual question, though Schlicht thinks we cannot answer it and can only *attribute* consciousness.
5. **Current AI.** Richards & Agüera y Arcas think some current AI may be partly conscious and that conscious AI is likely. Schneider and Silberstein think current LLMs are not conscious. Roelofs thinks the best current reason to doubt it is a "history-based defeater" (the systems were built to imitate human talk), which is close to Schneider's error theory. Both Roelofs and Schneider locate the doubt in the *training history*, not in the substrate.

**Points of agreement.**
- Several commentators accept Seth's negative case against computational functionalism, or a version of it (Ramstead, Silberstein, partly Schlicht and Shevlin), while disputing his positive biological case.
- "Different, not absent": Roelofs and Shevlin independently argue that even if biology matters for *our* consciousness, a different substrate might give a different form of consciousness rather than none. Silberstein allows non-biological substrates that match life's adaptability.
- Testing and evidence: Rodriguez & Farahany (organoid and neuroprosthesis experiments), Schneider ("boxed-in" tests), Solms (agreed build-and-test criteria) and Schlicht (the lack of tests for medium independence) all treat the absence of decisive tests as the field's main bottleneck.
- Organoids and biocomputers recur as the hard middle case (Rodriguez & Farahany, Schneider).

**Recurring themes for the field page.** simulation versus realisation; substrate independence versus medium independence; autopoiesis as functional or material; the role and limits of the free energy principle; self-reports and their defeaters; abundant versus sparse consciousness; deflationary (use-based) versus realist framings; ethics of deliberately building conscious systems (Solms versus Seth's §6.1); governance under persisting disagreement (Rodriguez & Farahany).
