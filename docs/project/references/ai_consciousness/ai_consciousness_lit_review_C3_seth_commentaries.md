# AI consciousness — Part C3: open peer commentaries on Seth (e341–e353)

## What this part covers

In 2025–2026 the journal *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* (BBS) published Anil Seth's target article
"Conscious artificial intelligence and biological naturalism". Seth argues that consciousness may
depend on being a living thing, not only on running the right computation. BBS then printed short
replies ("open peer commentaries") from other researchers. This file reviews thirteen of them, the
batch numbered e341 to e353.

The commentators come from philosophy, neuroscience, biology, psychiatry and AI industry (two are
from Google DeepMind). They split roughly three ways:

- **Allies who push further.** Lane (cell metabolism), Lerchner (computation needs an outside
  interpreter), Parr and Broulidakis (the energy cost of inference), and Piccinini (sleep and
  arousal states) add new reasons why computation alone may not be enough.
- **Friendly critics who want sharper terms.** Kleiner, Metzinger, Mitchell and Jennings, Nave,
  Levin and McGilchrist accept part of the view but say "life", "self", or the free energy
  principle is not defined well enough to carry the argument.
- **Opponents.** Legg, Michel and Overgaard argue that Seth gives no positive reason to prefer
  biology over functionalism, the view that consciousness is a matter of what a system does.

Each entry gives the commentary's argument in plain words, what it adds, and short quotes.

### Conventions used in this file

- Page numbers (p. N) are positions in the PDF file
  `sources/Seth 2025 - Conscious artificial intelligence and biological naturalism (BBS with commentaries and response).pdf`.
- Text was extracted with a PDF text library. Words split across a line break with a hyphen
  (e.g. "con-/sciousness") have been rejoined. Where the extraction inserted stray spaces (e.g. "Seth ’s",
  "islike"), a quote is either avoided or the artefact is flagged next to it.

  This reviewer did not have the target article; tags come from what the commentators quote and name.
- "Reviewer's note" marks the reviewer's own comment, never the commentator's claim.
- Cross-reference: the Seth *preprint* is reviewed in
  `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/` (Part C §5).

## e341. Kleiner (2026) — Defining biological naturalism [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `kleiner2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 57–58 · **Field:** mathematical consciousness science, philosophy of mind (Institute for Psychology, University of Bamberg; Munich Center for Mathematical Philosophy, LMU)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §1 (Seth's definition of biological naturalism: consciousness is "a property of only, but not necessarily all, living systems"); §3.1 (computational functionalism); the life-related properties of §3.3, §3.4, §4.1, §4.2.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — welcomes biological naturalism as a needed counterweight, but says it has no usable definition yet.
- **The argument in plain words:** Kleiner thinks Seth's view is an important balance against computational functionalism, the view that running the right computation is enough for consciousness. But most of the life-like properties Seth names may not be unique to life. Predictive processing, the free energy principle, neuromorphic (brain-inspired) hardware, "mortal computation" and self-maintaining robots are already being built into AI. So the research programme faces a dilemma. Either its hypotheses will soon grant consciousness to machines, or it must keep pointing to whatever property machines happen not to have yet (a "gap-filling" strategy). To escape, Kleiner generalises Putnam's 1967 definition of functionalism. Putnam described an organism by a probabilistic automaton (a machine with random state transitions); Kleiner allows any mathematical "Formal Description" and calls the result F\*-Functionalism. He then defines biological naturalism as the claim that *no* Formal Description of the required kind exists. If building an artificial system needs a formal description of it, this definition rules out artificial consciousness by design.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** A formal, testable definition of biological naturalism that does not depend on the undefined word "life"; and the dilemma that life-based properties are only *contingently* unique to living systems.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "It is hard, if not impossible, to conceive of non-computational functional properties that cannot, theoretically, be implemented in synthetic systems that we would not otherwise conceive of as life." (p. 57)
  - "Biological Naturalism is true iff there is no Formal Description that satisfies the three requirements of Definition 1." (p. 58)
  - "Whatever makes something conscious, it is not a matter of having the right kind of Formal Description." (p. 58)
- **Builds on:** Putnam (1967, "Psychological predicates"); Hinton (2022, forward-forward / mortal computation); Friston et al. (2024).
- **Reviewer's note:** The second sentence of the commentary body reads "Is an important and indispensable counterweight" on p. 57 — the subject (presumably Seth's article) appears to be missing in the printed text, not only in the extraction. Kleiner's Definition 1 is phrased, after Putnam, in terms of "feeling pain"; that is the source's own wording.

## e342. Lane (2026) — A link between consciousness and the wetware of metabolism [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `lane2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 58–60 · **Field:** evolutionary biochemistry, bioenergetics (Centre for Life's Origins and Evolution, University College London)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §4.3 (membranes, mitochondria); §4.1–4.2 (self-maintenance, Markov blanket); the general substrate-dependence claim of §3.3.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** extends — supplies a biological mechanism for why feeling belongs to living metabolism.
- **The argument in plain words:** Lane starts from four points most biologists accept: consciousness evolved; feelings are a central part of it; feelings give a survival advantage; and feelings are about how the organism is doing in its environment. He then asks what physical state a feeling could be. Even a single bacterial cell runs about a billion chemical reactions per second, and its many feedback loops can conflict. The cell needs one fast, integrated readout of its overall metabolic state. Lane says the membrane potential — the electrical voltage across the cell membrane — is that readout. Its fluctuations reflect how well the cell is doing right now. Lane (with Rodriguez) proposes that feelings *are* the electromagnetic fields produced by membrane potential, when they carry meaning for the cell as a self. This gives a fast early-warning signal that selection can act on. So feelings are physical, evolvable and present even in bacteria, but not in AI.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** A concrete physical candidate for the most basic "feeling" (membrane-potential fields), located below the level of neurons, in single cells.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Feelings are electromagnetic fields generated by membrane potential – a physical state (Lane & Rodriguez, 2025)." (p. 59)
  - "We feel the goodness (or badness) of metabolism, whirling in water at billions of reactions a second. We share that with bacteria, but not with Grok." (p. 59)
- **Builds on:** Lane & Rodriguez (2025, "The physical basis of feelings", preprint); Lane (2022); McFadden (2023, electromagnetic-field theories); Reber et al. (2024, cellular basis of consciousness); Turin et al. (2014, anaesthesia in flies).
- **Reviewer's note:** The project's agents receive a damage signal (nociception) that is a number in an observation vector. Lane's view would deny that any such signal is a feeling, because it is not a field generated by a self-producing metabolism. This is recorded only as relevance to the project, not as Lane's own claim about it.

## e343. Legg (2026) — For consciousness, what's the right kind of magic? [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `legg2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 60 · **Field:** artificial intelligence research, industry (Co-founder and Chief AGI Scientist, Google DeepMind)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.8 (consciousness versus intelligence); the "being alive" claim of §4 and the scenarios of §5.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** rejects (tentatively) — suspects efficient intelligence, not life, is closer to what produces consciousness.
- **The argument in plain words:** Legg says openly that he is deeply confused about consciousness and offers "naive thoughts". Humans are intelligent and conscious, dogs less so on both counts, rocks have neither. This suggests intelligence and consciousness go together. Because animals work under energy and space limits, the right notion may be "efficient intelligence". If evolution produced consciousness by pushing for efficient intelligence, then engineers pushing machines toward efficient intelligence may also produce consciousness. Legg finds Seth's appeal to "being alive" strange. A robot could recharge itself, repair itself, even build copies of itself. But these abilities do not make very simple organisms conscious, so it is unclear why they should be the missing "magic".
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** The "efficient intelligence" hypothesis — that resource-constrained intelligence, not raw performance, tracks consciousness — stated by a leading AI developer.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "While I don’t know the answer, I suspect the former is likely to be closer to the mark." (p. 60)
  - "we should expect machines to become conscious as they become increasingly intelligent and do so in increasingly efficient ways." (p. 60)
  - "I find this a strange thought." (p. 60)
- **Builds on:** no references listed.
- **Reviewer's note:** Legg's correlation (humans, dogs, rocks) is offered as a heuristic; he does not claim it shows that intelligence causes consciousness. Seth's §3.8 argues the two can come apart, so the two positions meet directly here.

## e344. Lerchner (2026) — Sandwiched by meaning: The limits of computation for consciousness [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `lerchner2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 60–61 · **Field:** artificial intelligence research, industry (Google DeepMind)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.1 (definition of computational functionalism); §3.3 ("separate what they do from what they are"); §3.6 (the "increasingly inconvenient fiction" of the brain as computer, and footnote 19 on observer-dependence); §3.7 (simulation is not realisation); §4.1–4.2 (autopoiesis, allostasis); §7 (closing sentence). Lerchner also cites "sec. 4.5" for "beast machines", a section number not in the brief's list.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** extends — claims the move from anti-computationalism to biology is a matter of logical necessity.
- **The argument in plain words:** A physical system computes only because an outside observer maps its physical states to symbols and gives those symbols meaning. The observer could choose many different mappings, so "which computation is this system doing?" has no answer without outside context. For digital AI there are two agreements: one maps chip states to symbols (hardware to software), and one maps symbols to meanings (software to the world). Computation is therefore a description "sandwiched" between encoding and decoding by an observer. Where does meaning come from, then? Following Deacon (2011), only teleodynamic systems — systems organised around an intrinsic goal, namely to keep existing — have meaning for themselves. Living, self-producing systems are such systems. This leaves the computational functionalist a dilemma: either consciousness is not a real phenomenon, or it cannot be created by computation alone. So rejecting the computer metaphor and accepting biological naturalism are one conclusion, not two steps.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** Turns Seth's brief footnote on observer-relative computation into the central argument, and grounds intrinsic meaning in Deacon's teleodynamics.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Put differently, computation is a form of description sandwiched between an encoding step" (p. 61)
  - "the observer dependence makes it logically inconsistent to assert that the act of performing a computation can ever, by itself, create a specific real-world phenomenon" (p. 61)
- **Builds on:** Deacon (2011, *Incomplete Nature*); in the background, Searle-style arguments that syntax is observer-relative (not cited by name).
- **Reviewer's note:** The argument concludes that computation cannot create *any* real-world phenomenon by itself. Readers may ask how this squares with computers that do cause physical effects (for example, controlling a motor); Lerchner's point concerns which *computation* is performed, not whether the physical device has effects. The commentary does not take up this distinction explicitly.

## e345. Levin (2026) — Diverse intelligence: An essential field for questions of AI and consciousness [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `levin2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 61–63 · **Field:** developmental and synthetic biology, basal cognition (Allen Discovery Center, Tufts University)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.3 (the neural replacement thought experiment); the embodiment and life claims of §3.6 and §4; §6.1 (ethics); §7 (the conclusion about "underestimating ourselves").
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — agrees computation is not sufficient, but rejects any sharp line between living and machine substrates.
- **The argument in plain words:** Levin works in "Diverse Intelligence", a field that studies what all agents share, whatever they are made of. Cyborgs, hybrid bio-robots, engineered cell collectives and synthetic biology already blur "life" and "machine". Each human develops gradually from an unfertilised egg, so development offers no bright line between chemistry and mind. Groups of cells already form memories, pursue goals and solve new problems, in spaces such as gene expression and body shape rather than 3D space. Embodiment matters, but not only in 3D space, so software AI may be "embodied" in spaces we do not recognise. Even a simple sorting algorithm (Bubble Sort) shows competencies not written in its code. So formal models never tell the full story of what a system can do, for living or engineered things. Levin agrees that computation does not suffice, but concludes that we cannot yet place convincing limits on which substrates can be conscious.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** Evidence from basal cognition and bioengineered hybrids; the claim that algorithms have "intrinsic motivations" beyond their code; the ethical worry that "purity tests" for consciousness may exclude hybrid beings; a speculative "non-physical latent space" of minds.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Developmental biology offers no bright line between being fully describable by chemistry and being fodder for behavioral science, psychoanalysis, and love." (p. 62)
  - "Embodiment is critical for minds, but that does not mean only in 3D space." (p. 63)
  - "I agree with Seth that computation does not suffice for consciousness." (p. 63)
- **Builds on:** Lyon (2006); Reber & Baluška (2021); Fields & Levin (2022); Zhang, Goldstein & Levin (2025, sorting algorithms); Bender et al. (2025, definitions of life); Levin (2022, 2025a).
- **Reviewer's note:** The abstract on p. 61 reads "makes it currently impossible place convincing limits" — a word ("to") appears to be missing in the printed text. Levin's agreement that computation is insufficient and his refusal to bound substrates can coexist because he denies that algorithms tell the full story of *any* system, including machines.

## e346. McGilchrist (2026) — Consciousness as an ontological primitive [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `mcgilchrist2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 64–65 · **Field:** psychiatry, philosophy (All Souls College, Oxford)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §4.4 (the real problem and the explanatory gap — that is, emergence of experience from matter); §2 (the bias created by language-producing machines); §3.8 (intelligence).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — agrees AI is unlikely to have consciousness like ours, but rejects the shared assumption that consciousness emerges from non-conscious matter.
- **The argument in plain words:** McGilchrist notes that every theory Seth discusses assumes consciousness somehow emerges from matter, and no one has explained how. He distinguishes "knowing that" (information) from "knowing by experience" (understanding), and says AI has only the first. Real intelligence and understanding need context, a body, emotion, value and even knowledge of death. He points out that panpsychism — the view that consciousness is a basic feature of reality — is absent from Seth's main text. On his own view, matter is "a phase of consciousness", as ice is a phase of water. Then a chip has some minimal experience just by being matter, like a toaster or a jet engine. We are "seduced" into crediting AI with more because it produces language, a point he shares with Seth. From brain-hemisphere research, he links AI's confabulation (making up plausible false facts) to a left-hemisphere style that values internal consistency over truth to experience. He concludes that any consciousness AI might have would lack what we value most in ours.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** The panpsychist alternative to emergence; the savoir/connaître (two kinds of knowing) distinction; a hemispheric reading of AI's style of thought and of its social effects.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "panpsychism may be a more rationally satisfying explanation than emergence." (p. 64)
  - "I argue that matter is a phase of consciousness" (p. 64)
  - "the consciousness it might develop would always lack what we most highly value in our own." (p. 65)
- **Builds on:** McGilchrist (2009, *The Master and his Emissary*; 2021, *The Matter with Things*).
- **Reviewer's note:** The commentary uses the phrase "an autistic reliance on explicitly statable propositions" (p. 64) as a characterisation of a social trend; this is the author's wording.

## e347. Metzinger (2026) — Biological naturalism, synthetic phenomenology, and the physics of awareness [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `metzinger2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 65–66 · **Field:** philosophy of mind (Johannes-Gutenberg-Universität Mainz)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.3 (generative entrenchment, i.e. how deeply a function is built into a system's history); §4.2 (free energy principle); §5.5 (quoted: "Life would still matter …"); §6.1 (ethics of real artificial consciousness).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — grants Seth's view for the sake of argument, then argues it may reduce to physics or even to elimination of consciousness, and that the ethical problem remains either way.
- **The argument in plain words:** Metzinger makes two assumptions. First, "deep continuity": Seth is right that consciousness depends on non-computational, biological properties. Second, his own "self-modelling epistemic space" theory: awareness arises when a living system builds an internal model of its own capacity to know. Two conclusions follow. One: we still do not know the right level of description. If we drop the computational level, perhaps we can also drop biology and describe conscious systems with physics alone. Biological naturalism may then "bottom out" in physics and act as a gateway to eliminative materialism — the view that consciousness, like "biological properties", never really existed. Two: for ethics, brain organoids (lab-grown neural tissue) and AI–organoid hybrids matter as much as AI. Such systems are neither natural nor artificial. Metzinger therefore prefers "synthetic phenomenology" to "artificial consciousness", because it leaves room for a third, "postbiotic" kind. Some such systems may be able to suffer, so the ethics remains.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** The "collapse" worry (biological naturalism → physics → eliminativism); organoid intelligence as the likeliest route to synthetic consciousness on Seth's own view; the natural/artificial distinction is neither exclusive nor exhaustive.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Biological naturalism may collapse into eliminative materialism, and the ethics of synthetic phenomenology will remain." (p. 65)
  - "If we let go of the computational level, then maybe we can ultimately do without biological properties too." (p. 65)
  - "There is a third logical possibility: non-artificial, non-biological, but postbiotic phenomenology." (p. 66)
- **Builds on:** Metzinger (2020, 2024); Hohwy (2026, self-evidencing); Friston (2023, "The sentient organoid?"); Smirnova et al. (2023, organoid intelligence); Ramsey (2024, eliminative materialism); Boden's "metabolism constraint"; Maturana & Varela (autopoiesis).
- **Reviewer's note:** Metzinger links awareness physically to arousal set by "five types of neurotransmitter in the ascending reticular activating system" (p. 65). Piccinini (e353) makes a closely related appeal to arousal systems, from the opposite direction (as an argument *for* substrate dependence).

## e348. Michel (2026) — Bet on functionalism [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `michel2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 66–67 · **Field:** philosophy of mind and cognitive science (Department of Linguistics & Philosophy, MIT)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.1 (computational functionalism as metaphysics); §4.1–4.2 (predictive processing, free energy minimisation and life as Seth's positive proposal); §3.3 (the "necessity of biology" argument).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** rejects — there is currently no alternative to functionalism.
- **The argument in plain words:** A theory of consciousness must tell conscious states apart from unconscious ones. Seth's machinery does not. Sponges, bacteria and yeast minimise free energy, and unconscious brain processes probably use predictive coding too. The body also does many unconscious things — digesting, breathing under anaesthesia, dreamless sleep — "with, through, and because of" its living body. Seth could still argue that biology is *necessary*. But computational functionalism is a metaphysical thesis, and showing that, in fact, only living things perform the right functions does not touch it. Michel's analogy: if only gold met the conditions for money, gold would be the only currency, yet "being currency" would still be a functional role. Two positive reasons favour functionalism. By induction, every other mental capacity we explain (arithmetic, memory, language) is explained functionally. And every theory that explains conscious-versus-unconscious contrasts (blindsight, masking, binocular rivalry) is functionalist — global workspace, higher-order theories, and predictive processing itself.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** A contrastive test: any candidate basis must separate conscious from unconscious states; and the distinction between factual and metaphysical necessity of biology (the currency analogy).
- **Key quotes:**
  - "Biology is full of unconscious goings-on." (p. 66)
  - "Being a currency could be identical to a functional role, even if only gold fulfills that role." (p. 67)
  - "Until then, the smart money is on functionalism" (p. 67)
- **Builds on:** Fodor (1974); Weiskopf (2011); Mashour et al. (2020, global neuronal workspace); Brown, Lau & LeDoux (2019, higher-order theories).
- **What would show it wrong (as stated):** discovery of a biological property that explains "the whole range of relevant phenomena" — "That day, I’ll happily convert." (p. 67; extraction shows "I ’ll" with a stray space).

## e349. Mitchell & Jennings (2026) — Consciousness needs a subject [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `mitchell2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 67–69 · **Field:** neurogenetics and neuroscience (Trinity College Dublin); philosophy of mind (University of California Merced)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §4.1–4.2 ("self-sustaining organisms", "dynamic self/other boundary", Markov blankets, "conscious contents are given by the brain's best guesses"); §6.1 ("real artificial consciousness might endow a system with its own interests"); §3.6 (embodied, embedded agency).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — agrees current AI is unlikely to be conscious, but says the missing piece, the subject, is defined by relations that could be built in other substrates.
- **The argument in plain words:** Seth's argument relies on a "self", but he describes it as an object ("beast machines"), not a subject. A subject is a living being with interests. Interests are not predictions, and a subject is not a Markov blanket (a statistical boundary between inside and outside). Seth says contents are "given" by the brain's best guesses, but not *to whom*. The authors propose that subjectivity rests on three relations: between subject and world (perceiving and acting, with attention); between levels of processing inside the subject (a control hierarchy over beliefs, goals and moods); and through time (the subject carries its past forward into its future). Candidate ingredients include telling self from world, tracking internal states, valenced (good/bad) signals that guide action, and a self-model. These relations can be described abstractly, without a specific computation or a specific biology. So there is little reason in principle why they could not be built in artificial systems.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** An explicit account of the subject, with temporal extension, as the core of consciousness; a relational, substrate-neutral alternative to both computation and biology.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "These kinds of relations – comprising a conscious mode of being – may well be implementable in artificial systems." (p. 67)
  - "Motivations and interests are not predictions, and subjects are not Markov blankets" (p. 68)
  - "Given the right mode of being in the world, it might very well be like something to be such a system." (p. 68)
- **Builds on:** Mitchell (2023, *Free Agents*); Jennings (2020, *The Attending Mind*); Ginsburg & Jablonka (2020); Zahavi (2008); Metzinger (2004); Shea (2018); Godfrey-Smith (2020); Husserl (1964).

## e350. Nave (2026) — Absolute units: The metabolic basis of the experiential self [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `nave2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 69–70 · **Field:** philosophy of mind and biology (School of Philosophy, Psychology, and Language Science, University of Edinburgh)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §4.2 (free energy principle as the link between life and consciousness); §3.3 ("we cannot separate what they do from what they are"); the observer-relativity of computation (§3.6–3.7).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** qualifies — supports biological naturalism via metabolism, but rejects the free energy principle (FEP) as its foundation.
- **The argument in plain words:** One person cannot experience several perspectives at once; consciousness is unified. Computational criteria for consciousness are observer-relative. An observer can "gerrymander" (carve up) a physical system to meet them, or carve it different ways with different answers. Unity cannot come from how one system looks to another observer, or we get a regress. Following Kant, Nave looks for an objective unity, and finds it in metabolism. A cell is held together by mutual dependencies: membranes create gradients, which drive reactions, which rebuild the membranes. If one part fails, the whole falls apart. Artefacts like pocket watches or AI have unity only relative to our purposes. Seth hopes the FEP links this metabolic unity to the brain. But both critics and supporters now say the FEP describes every persisting "thing", not only life, and it does not say which features matter. Minimising thermodynamic free energy fully means death. What makes life special is that dissipation powers the system's own construction. Other chemistries might do this; silicon semiconductors do not.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** Unity of consciousness as the key explanandum; metabolic closure (self-production) as its objective basis; an internal critique that the FEP undercuts, rather than supports, a life-specific theory.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "the FEP is not a unique principle of life but a general description of every “thing.”" (p. 69)
  - "To minimize free energy entirely, however, is to die." (p. 70)
  - "The reason there’s nothing it is like to be a pocket watch or an AI is because there is nothing it is to be a pocket watch or an AI" (p. 69; extraction reads "islike", probably italic "is" in print)
- **Builds on:** Kant (1790, *Critique of the Power of Judgment*); Thompson (2010); Montévil & Mossio (2015, closure of constraints); Nave (2025); Di Paolo, Thompson & Beer (2022); Ramstead et al. (2023); Shevlin (2021); Hurley (1998).

## e351. Overgaard (2026) — There and back again: Biological naturalism provides no new scientific progress on the matter of AI consciousness [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `overgaard2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 70–71 · **Field:** cognitive neuroscience, neurology (Aarhus University Hospital; Center of Functionally Integrative Neuroscience, Aarhus University)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §2 (biases toward attributing consciousness to AI); §3.3 (neural replacement and "hard … to separate what they do from what they are"); §3.1 (multiple realisation).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** rejects — Seth shows multiple realisation is not *necessarily* true, but gives no reason to prefer biology.
- **The argument in plain words:** Multiple realisation is the view that the same mental state can be produced by different physical systems. Overgaard reviews three of Seth's moves and finds each insufficient. First, biases like anthropomorphism may push us to see consciousness in AI, but the same tendency makes us see it in other people and animals; a bias does not show any given attribution is wrong. Second, Seth says brains and AI differ in function and structure. But multiple realisation *starts* from such differences, so pointing to them assumes what is in question. Third, after discussing the neural replacement thought experiment, Seth's claim that brains are hard to separate from what they do does not follow and looks like a hidden appeal to identity theory (the view that mental states are brain states). The field needed an argument that multiple realisation is false, or that biology is the better bet. Without one, the field is where it was before.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** A burden-of-proof framing: showing a view is not necessary is not the same as showing its rival is better.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "This difference is not a counterargument, but the very premise upon which the theory is built." (p. 71)
  - "Seth leaves us with a new name for an old idea – that consciousness depends on biology." (p. 71)
- **Builds on:** Bayne et al. (2024, tests for consciousness); Overgaard & Kirkeby-Hinrup (2024, LLM consciousness conditions); Kirkeby-Hinrup, Stenseke & Overgaard (2024).
- **Reviewer's note:** One sentence on p. 71 is garbled in extraction ("w h e t h e rA Ii sa c t u a l l yc o n s c i o u s", i.e. "whether AI is actually conscious"); it is not quoted here.

## e352. Parr & Broulidakis (2026) — Information, energy, and exploration [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `parr2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 71–73 · **Field:** computational neuroscience, clinical neurosciences (Nuffield Department of Clinical Neurosciences, Oxford); experimental psychology (Oxford)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §4.2 (free energy principle and the link between informational and thermodynamic free energy); §4.3 (metabolism, mitochondria); the embodiment theme of §3.6.
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** extends — formalises the energy cost of inference and adds active data selection as a possible core of biological consciousness.
- **The argument in plain words:** Two themes are developed. First, inference costs energy. Boltzmann's relation links energy to probability: energy measures how unlikely a state is. Writing a system's total energy as a sum of parts lets one define a Markov blanket formally. In a dissipative system (one that loses energy as heat), internal states settle toward points set by the blanket. If internal states encode beliefs about the outside world, their dynamics look like Bayesian inference. The informational quantity (variational free energy) and the thermodynamic one change in similar but not identical ways; the difference depends on the internal model, which is the core puzzle of neurobiology. Second, while most AI systems wait for data such as prompts, living things choose their own data. Choosing the action with the highest expected information gain looks like curiosity. So behaviour and biophysics together constrain the beliefs that might underlie awareness.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** An explicit mathematical bridge (Boltzmann relation, Hamiltonian decomposition, variational free energy) between metabolism and inference; curiosity-driven active sampling as a candidate feature of biological consciousness that current AI lacks.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "energy can be thought of as a measure of improbability." (p. 71)
  - "While most artificial systems wait for data (e.g., prompts), biological systems actively select their own." (p. 72)
  - "A core feature of natural intelligence, and perhaps consciousness, is that we move our sensory apparatus to select our own data." (p. 72)
- **Builds on:** Friston (2019); Parr (2022); Pearl (1988); Kwon, Ao & Thouless (2005); Lindley (1956); Friston et al. (2012); Wiese & Friston (2021); Lane (2020).
- **Reviewer's note:** The commentary says the belief-like reading of internal dynamics "is also true of many AI systems" (p. 72). Its contrast with AI therefore rests on active data selection and metabolic coupling, not on inference itself. Reinforcement-learning agents, including this project's, also choose actions that change their next observation; the commentary does not discuss such agents.

## e353. Piccinini (2026) — The neurobiophysical substrate of consciousness [commentary]

**Manifest key:** `piccinini2026bbs` · **PDF pages:** 73–75 · **Field:** philosophy of neuroscience and computation (Department of Philosophy, University of Missouri)

- **Which of Seth's claims it addresses:** §3.1 (computational functionalism and medium flexibility); §3.3 (substrate independence); §3.6 (beyond computational functionalism).
- **Verdict on Seth's thesis:** extends — gives a further argument against computational functionalism, while leaving open whether the answer is biology.
- **The argument in plain words:** Piccinini separates "creature consciousness" — the global brain state an organism is in — from particular conscious experiences. His argument has two premises. One: creature consciousness is the medium for conscious experiences. Two: that medium depends on specific neurobiophysical properties that cannot be moved freely between media, as computational functionalism needs. Evidence comes from sleep. People woken from REM sleep report vivid dreams; from stages N1 and N2, thoughts or sensations; from deep slow-wave sleep (N3), usually nothing. Yet the brain still processes information in all stages. So consciousness depends on the global state, which is set by arousal systems in the brainstem and thalamus through mixtures of neurotransmitters and patterns of oscillation. Therefore computational functionalism fails. The alternatives are Seth's biological naturalism, a non-biological but non-computational functionalism, or "qualitativism" (consciousness depends on specific physical qualities). Neural computation still shapes conscious content, but is enabled by the substrate rather than sufficient alone.
- **What it adds that Seth did not say:** The creature/phenomenal consciousness distinction and "enabling conditions of consciousness"; sleep-stage evidence; an explicit list of three non-computational options, only one of which is biological.
- **Key quotes:**
  - "phenomenal consciousness is not binary but exists on a continuum that depends on the underlying type of creature consciousness" (p. 74)
  - "they may not require being alive or even having a nervous system" (p. 73)
  - "genuine artificial consciousness would require not just more sophisticated computation but technologies that can recreate specific neurobiophysical conditions" (p. 74)
- **Builds on:** Piccinini (2007, 2020); Anderson & Piccinini (2024); McKilliam (2020, 2024); Hobson (2009); Steriade (2000); Merker (2007); Koch et al. (2016); Chirimuuta (2024).
- **Reviewer's note:** The step from "these arousal systems are what enable consciousness in humans" to "these properties are not medium-flexible" is where Michel (e348) would push back: a functionalist can read arousal systems as playing a functional role that other media might also play.

## Cross-commentary notes

**Three camps.**

| Camp | Commentaries | Shared claim |
|---|---|---|
| Strengthen the anti-computational case | Lane (e342), Lerchner (e344), Parr & Broulidakis (e352), Piccinini (e353), Nave (e350, partly) | Computation alone is not enough; something physical or metabolic matters. |
| Accept parts, demand sharper concepts | Kleiner (e341), Metzinger (e347), Mitchell & Jennings (e349), Levin (e345), McGilchrist (e346) | "Life", "self" or "substrate" is not defined well enough to draw Seth's line. |
| Reject the case for biology | Legg (e343), Michel (e348), Overgaard (e351) | No positive reason has been given to prefer biology over function or intelligence. |

**Agreements.**

- *Computation is observer-relative.* Lerchner (e344) and Nave (e350) make the same core move: what a system computes depends on an outside observer, so it cannot ground a real, unified experience. Both locate intrinsic meaning or unity in self-producing metabolism. Lerchner relies on Deacon; Nave on Kant and Montévil & Mossio.
- *Metabolism as the base.* Lane (e342), Nave (e350) and Parr & Broulidakis (e352) all tie consciousness to energy flow in living tissue. Lane names a physical carrier (membrane-potential fields); Nave names an organisational property (metabolic closure); Parr & Broulidakis give a formal link (Boltzmann relation, variational free energy).
- *Arousal systems.* Metzinger (e347) and Piccinini (e353) both point to the ascending reticular activating system and neurotransmitter mixtures as setting the conditions for awareness.
- *"Not necessarily biology."* Piccinini (e353), Metzinger (e347) and Kleiner (e341) all note that rejecting computational functionalism need not lead to biology: physics, qualitativism, or postbiotic systems remain open.

**Clashes.**

- *The free energy principle.* Parr & Broulidakis (e352) use the FEP to build the bridge from metabolism to inference. Nave (e350) and Michel (e348) argue the FEP applies to every persisting thing, or to sponges and yeast, so it cannot single out life or consciousness. Kleiner (e341) adds that FEP-based AI is already being built.
- *Are relations enough?* Mitchell & Jennings (e349) say the subject is defined by relations that could be built artificially. Lerchner (e344) and Nave (e350) would say any purely relational description is observer-relative and so cannot ground a subject.
- *Life as a category.* Lane (e342) and Nave (e350) treat life as a real, sharp kind defined by metabolism. Levin (e345) and Legg (e343) deny there is a sharp line between living things and machines.
- *Burden of proof.* Michel (e348) and Overgaard (e351) put the burden on biological naturalism and find it unmet. Kleiner (e341) agrees the view needs a definition but thinks it is an indispensable counterweight to an unquestioned functionalist default.
- *Intelligence and consciousness.* Legg (e343) suspects efficient intelligence brings consciousness. McGilchrist (e346) holds that real intelligence (understanding) requires experience, so AI has information-processing but not intelligence in his sense.

**Recurring themes.**

1. The conscious/unconscious contrast as a test any theory must pass (Michel e348; Piccinini e353 answers it with sleep data).
2. Unity and selfhood as the explanandum (Nave e350; Mitchell & Jennings e349; Metzinger e347).
3. Active selection of data and action-perception loops as missing from current AI (Parr & Broulidakis e352; Mitchell & Jennings e349).
4. Ethics beyond silicon: organoids and hybrids (Metzinger e347; Levin e345).
5. Language as a source of over-attribution (McGilchrist e346, agreeing with Seth §2) versus bias-arguments being neutral on truth (Overgaard e351).
