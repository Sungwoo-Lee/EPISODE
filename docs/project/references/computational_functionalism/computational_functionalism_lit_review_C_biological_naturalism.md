# Computational Functionalism Field History — Part C: The Biological Challenge

**Topic folder:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/`
**Part of:** a multi-file, deliberately neutral review of how the idea of *computational functionalism* developed — the thesis that mental states, possibly including conscious ones, are constituted by the computations a system performs, so that running the right computation in any material at all would suffice.
**This part:** 5 papers, reviewed one at a time in the order given by the batch assignment, 2026-09-21. 1 further work is named but not reviewed because no PDF is held.
**Reviewer:** literature-reviewer

## What this part is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question.** Suppose you could copy, step for step, everything a human brain computes, and run that copy on a computer. Would the copy feel anything? Computational functionalism says yes: what matters is the computation, not what the computation is made of. The papers in this part all say — in different ways and with different degrees of confidence — *not so fast, the stuff might matter*. That family of positions is what the field calls **biological naturalism**: the view that consciousness depends on properties of living systems that a simulation of those systems would not automatically have.

**Why this part exists.** A history of computational functionalism cannot be written from its defenders alone. The strongest, most sustained modern opposition does not come from dualism (the claim that minds are non-physical); it comes from biology. These five works argue that metabolism, self-maintenance, embodiment, the body's regulation of itself, and specific brain architecture are each doing work that a program running the same input-output pattern would not be doing. Their arguments are the pressure that has shaped what functionalists now have to say.

**The five works, in one line each.**
- **Godfrey-Smith (2016 / 2014 talk), "Mind, Matter, and Metabolism."** Living matter at the scale where metabolism happens — billionths of a metre, surrounded by water, in constant thermal churn — behaves nothing like a machine, and the philosopher's stock character, the "non-living functional duplicate" of a person, may not be coherent, because *being metabolically alive is part of a human's functional profile*.
- **Seth and Tsakiris (2018), "Being a Beast Machine."** Selfhood is built out of the brain's control of the body's internal state — heartbeat, blood chemistry, gut — rather than out of information processing about the outside world. Their slogan: we experience ourselves as selves because of, not in spite of, being machines made of flesh.
- **Cleeremans and Tallon-Baudry (2022), "Consciousness Matters."** Feeling something is *worth something to you*, and that is what makes it useful. Their argument: every conscious experience carries value for the one having it; things with value guide behaviour; so consciousness has a function. The consequence they draw for machines is that the obstacle is not computing power but that nothing matters to a program — "the reward itself is not rewarding *for* them" — and they suspect that fixing this ties consciousness closely to being alive. (This entry sits least comfortably under "biological naturalism"; see the cross-paper notes.)
- **Aru, Larkum and Shine (2023), "The Feasibility of Artificial Consciousness Through the Lens of Neuroscience."** Three arguments that today's large language models are unlikely to be conscious: they lack the brain's recurrent thalamocortical loop structure, they have no continuous self-sustaining physiological embedding, and they do not have an organised drive-and-value system rooted in a body.
- **Seth (2025), "Conscious Artificial Intelligence and Biological Naturalism."** The book-length statement of the position. Seth argues that computational functionalism is a widely *assumed* rather than argued view, digs up the assumptions it rests on, and puts forward the alternative that consciousness depends on being alive, while insisting that the honest verdict is uncertainty rather than a proof either way.

**A caution for the synthesis.** Two of the five PDFs are not the version of record. Godfrey-Smith's is the 2014 conference talk that was later split into two separate publications; Aru et al.'s is the author preprint of the *Trends in Neurosciences* paper; Seth's is a preprint of the *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* target article. Details are in each entry's opening line and in its `PDF version` field.

**Neutrality note.** This document does not adjudicate. Where a paper's opponents would object, the objection is reported as the paper itself reports it, or flagged as an open point — not settled.

## Reading conventions

- **Tiers.** `[tier: full]` entries carry Phase 1 (plain-language overview), Phase 2 (graduate-level deep dive — for these argumentative papers, a numbered argument reconstruction) and a section-by-section backbone appendix. `[tier: short]` entries were read in full from the PDF but are summarised in five parts: Claim · Method/type · Key result or argument · Place in the field's history · Relevance.
- **Page citations** use the page numbers printed on each PDF where the PDF is a published version, and PDF page numbers (marked "PDF p.") where it is a preprint or talk version without journal pagination.
- **No invented mathematics.** None of these five works turns on a derivation. Where a paper states a formal quantity (for example integrated information, Φ) it is reported as the paper states it and not re-derived.
- **Vocabulary.** Each author's own terms are kept:
  - *Computational functionalism*: the thesis that performing the right computations is sufficient for having a mind, so mind is substrate-independent.
  - *Substrate independence*: the corollary that the physical stuff implementing the computation is irrelevant.
  - *Multiple realizability*: the claim that the same mental state can be realised by many different physical arrangements.
  - *Biological naturalism*: the label (from Searle, taken up and redefined by Seth) for the view that consciousness is a biological phenomenon caused by, and realised in, living systems.
  - *Indicator properties* (Seth): features of a system that raise or lower the credence that it is conscious, short of being necessary or sufficient conditions.
  - *Proto-cognition*, *proto-subjectivity* (Godfrey-Smith): minimal sensing-and-responding that keeps a system alive; the minimal point-of-view that goes with maintaining one's own boundary.
  - *Interoception* (Seth & Tsakiris; Cleeremans & Tallon-Baudry): the sensing and regulation of the body's internal physiological state.
  - *Beast machine* (Seth & Tsakiris): their deliberate inversion of Descartes' *bête machine* — selfhood arises *because* we are flesh machines regulating ourselves.
- **Project vocabulary.** In this project's own artificial agents the internal damage signal is **nociception**, never "pain". "Pain", "suffering" and "consciousness" in this document refer only to the human and animal phenomena these papers discuss.

## 1. Godfrey-Smith (2016) — Mind, Matter, and Metabolism [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Godfrey-Smith 2016 - Mind matter and metabolism.pdf`

**Version caveat — read this first.** This PDF is **not** the published *Journal of Philosophy* article (113(10): 481–506, DOI 10.5840/jphil20161131034). Its own first page states: "This is the original NYU talk version. The material here has been split into two papers for publication. 'Mind, Matter, and Metabolism,' based mostly on the early sections, is forthcoming in the *Journal of Philosophy*, while 'Animal Evolution and the Origins of Experience,' based mostly on section 5, will appear in David Livingstone Smith's *How Biology Shapes Philosophy*" (PDF p. 1). So sections 1–4 below correspond roughly to the paper the DOI points at, and section 5 to a different publication. The talk has no journal pagination; citations here are PDF pages. Anyone quoting this for publication must check the wording against the *Journal of Philosophy* text.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction — the problem in plain terms.** Godfrey-Smith asks what biology — the nature of life itself, and the evolutionary history of animals — contributes to philosophy of mind, in particular to questions about qualia, consciousness and the "explanatory gap" (the apparent impossibility of explaining why physical processes feel like anything). His larger ambition is a chain of three links that each make sense: physical processes → living activity → proto-cognitive and cognitive processes → subjective experience. He says explicitly that the paper is "a first sketch and some stages are more filled out than others" (PDF p. 2).

His historical diagnosis is the paper's hinge. Once, *life* was the bridge concept between mental and physical — Aristotle's nutritive, sensitive and rational souls, and later the emergentists. Two developments removed it. First, computers and AI made some cognition look mechanisable in a non-living system, so "Computation, rather than life, became the crucial bridging concept between mental and physical" (PDF p. 2). Second, the concept of life itself deflated: biology textbooks now describe what living systems *do* — metabolise, develop, reproduce, behave — without taking a stand on what life *is*. Both developments were reasonable, he says, "but I think that some of what has resulted is a wrong turn" (PDF p. 3).

**Key findings and moves.**
- **Matter at the metabolic scale behaves unlike a machine.** Metabolism happens at the nanometre scale, surrounded by water. Drawing on Peter Hoffman's *Life's Ratchet* (2012), he describes a "molecular storm": unending spontaneous motion needing no external power, large molecules struck by water molecules "trillions of times per second", ubiquitous electrical charge. Crucially, "The parts of a cell that do things in the usual sense ... are subject to forces that are much stronger than the forces they can exert. The way things get done is by biasing tendencies in the storm, nudging random walks in useful directions" (PDF p. 4). Peter Moore's conclusion is quoted approvingly as a position on the table: "Macromolecular Devices Are Not Machines" (PDF p. 4).
- **This is probably not an accident.** Hoffman's claim, quoted: "The nanoscale is the only scale at which machines can work completely autonomously" (PDF p. 5). And there probably never were simple metabolisms: real chemistry has side-reactions, so "The evolution of life was a matter of channeling and taming this sea of interactions, not taking a few simple interactions and stringing them together" (PDF p. 5). The transition was "not from simple to complex, but from disorderly to orderly."
- **A new reply to Leibniz's mill.** Leibniz (Monadology §17) invited us to walk inside a thinking machine enlarged to mill size and observe that we would find "only parts that push one another", never a perception. Godfrey-Smith's response: "Leibniz's imagined mill is a macro-scale dry-land object. An aqueous nano-mill would be a very different place" (PDF p. 7). Inside a cell we would see not push-pull but "a storm of activity biased by charge and shape", "more a matter of 'herding molecular cats'". The apparent antipathy between the mental and the physical "is much reduced" — though, he notes, our immediate imaginative response "would surely tend towards panpsychism, if anything. That would be another mistake."
- **Life and proto-cognition come as a package, or nearly so.** All uncontested minds are in living systems; all known metabolically living systems do some sensing and responding that keeps them alive. E. coli chemotaxis even uses memory — comparing chemical levels now against a few seconds ago, then swimming straight or tumbling (PDF p. 9). He is careful about how tight the link is: it would "take a lot" to show that maintaining a viable metabolism *requires* proto-cognition, and it would be "a mistake to trivialize the connection by saying that self-maintenance itself is proto-cognitive" (PDF p. 10).
- **The move that matters most for computational functionalism.** "The idea of a non-living 'functional duplicate' of a living system like a person, routinely appealed to by philosophers, is quite problematic. There might one day be functionally similar non-living systems, to some appreciable degree, but **metabolic activity is part of the 'functional' profile of a human agent**" (PDF pp. 11–12, emphasis added). Note the form of the objection: it does not say functionalism is false. It says the functionalist has been drawing the boundary of "the function" too narrowly.
- **Biopsychism named and weighed.** He puts on the table the view "that all and only systems that are alive, in the metabolic sense, have subjective experience", reviving Haeckel's 1892 term *biopsychism* (PDF p. 13). It sits between panpsychism (everything feels) and views on which only complex brains feel. He is explicit about his own position: "I don't think biopsychism is insane, though I don't believe it." Its advantage over the "other radically generous views" is that a living system "maintains itself, controls its boundaries, and thus has a kind of proto-subjectivity", and there are no very simple living systems to be embarrassed by.
- **Animal transitions.** Four steps that plausibly matter for experience: the **cytoskeleton** (shape change, so richer behaviour); **multicellularity** (specialisation of sensing, integrating, acting); **nervous systems** (with two working definitions of "neuron", broad and narrow, from his co-authored work with Jékely and Keijzer); and the **Cambrian**, where Michael Trestman's *complex active bodies* (CABs) appear — articulated appendages, many degrees of freedom, distal senses such as true eyes, distal-sense-guided mobility, and object manipulation (PDF p. 18). From then on "the mind evolved in response to other minds."
- **Latecomer versus transformation.** Recent consciousness science — dual-stream vision (Milner & Goodale), global workspace (Baars, Dehaene), working-memory theories (Prinz) — shows much cognition with no felt side, which motivates a **latecomer view**: experience arrives only with late-evolving machinery for unification. Godfrey-Smith offers the **transformation view** instead: those late features "greatly affect the nature of subjective experience, but they don't bring it into being" (PDF p. 22). His argument is the intrusive character of sudden pain and of Derek Denton's "primordial emotions" (thirst, air hunger), which have an "imperious" role.

**Initial takeaway.** The paper's contribution to this field's history is not a knock-down argument against computational functionalism; it is a *re-description of the physical*. The standard mind-body intuition pump — brain as machine, mind as its program — imports a picture of matter borrowed from dry macroscopic machinery. Godfrey-Smith argues that the actual material basis of our mental activity looks nothing like that, and that if you fix the picture, both the anti-materialist arguments and the easy assumption of substrate independence lose some of their grip. The one sharp claim he does press is that "functional profile" is being used too thinly: metabolism is part of ours.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive — Argument Reconstruction

The talk contains four distinguishable arguments. Each is reconstructed here in the author's own terms, with what it is meant to establish and the replies the text anticipates.

#### Argument A — The historical-diagnosis argument (§2)

- **A1.** Historically, life served as the bridging concept between the mental and the physical (Aristotle; the emergentists Spencer, Lewes, Dewey, Broad).
- **A2.** Two twentieth-century developments dislodged it: (i) computers and AI showed that "some aspects of cognition are mechanizable in principle, and in a non-living system", and "given that there seems a real possibility that such a system might capture all of mentality, there can't apparently be too close a link between life and mind" (PDF p. 2); (ii) the concept of life deflated into a cluster concept, so it became "less appealing to use life as a load-bearing concept" (PDF p. 3).
- **A3.** Both developments were individually reasonable.
- **A4.** Nevertheless the joint result — computation replacing life as the bridge — is "a wrong turn" (PDF p. 3).
- **Meant to establish:** that computational functionalism's displacement of biology was a historical contingency rather than a discovery, and so is open to revisiting.
- **What it does *not* establish, by the author's own framing:** A2(i) is stated as what the AI work "seemed to show". The argument is diagnostic; the case for A4 comes from Arguments B and C.

#### Argument B — The molecular-storm argument against machine imagery (§3)

- **B1.** In real cells, metabolism occurs at the nanometre scale in aqueous solution.
- **B2.** At that scale matter behaves in a characteristic way: spontaneous unending motion; bombardment by water molecules trillions of times per second; ubiquitous charge; and — the load-bearing premise — the forces on a functional part far exceed the forces it can exert, so work is done by *biasing random walks*, "getting a consistent upshot out of vast numbers of mostly meaningless changes" (PDF p. 4).
- **B3.** On Moore's definition — a machine is a thing "where low-level interactions are predictable and parts are tightly coupled" — B2 describes something non-mechanistic: "Macromolecular Devices Are Not Machines."
- **B4.** These features are close to non-negotiable for life, not incidental: side-reactions make simple metabolisms implausible (Orgel; Szathmáry; Koonin & Martin are cited), and the nanoscale is arguably "the only scale at which machines can work completely autonomously" (Hoffman, quoted PDF p. 5).
- **B5.** Therefore, arguments in philosophy of mind that trade on the imagined un-mental character of the physical — conceivability arguments in Kripke and Chalmers; Leibniz's mill — are trading on an image of matter that is not the matter our minds are made of. "Many of the features of the physical that seem un-mental are not present" (PDF p. 6).
- **Meant to establish:** a *deflation of anti-materialist intuitions*, plus a warning shot at substrate independence. Note who the primary target is here: **B5 is aimed at anti-materialists, not at functionalists.** Godfrey-Smith is explicit that he does not need this material to reject conceivability arguments; his standard reply is Nagel's distinction between "sympathetic" imagining (imagining *being* something) and "perceptual" imagining (imagining *seeing* it), which he formulates in Bayesian terms in a footnote: "the evidence (in imagination) is equally likely given the truth and the falsity of materialism, so the prior probabilities, whatever they were, remain unchanged" (PDF p. 6, n. 4).
- **Anticipated reply, and his answer.** Reply: the enlarged-cell tour would push us towards panpsychism, which is worse. Answer: "That would be another mistake" (PDF p. 7); explaining perception still "requires working at a different level of description from the intracellular".
- **A striking concession.** Asked whether Leibniz might have been right *if* our brains had contained scaled-down macro-style mechanisms, he answers: "I suspect that the answer is moot (or a faint yes for Leibniz): our brains could not have turned out to be that way" (PDF p. 7). Taken at face value, this concedes something a computational functionalist would reject — that push-pull mechanism might genuinely be insufficient for experience — while defusing it as counterfactual.

#### Argument C — Metabolism belongs inside the functional profile (§4)

- **C1.** Every uncontested case of a system with a mind is a living system; and every known metabolically living system engages in proto-cognitive activity — "sensing and responding to events, perhaps in minimal ways, and doing so in a way that helps keep the system alive" (PDF p. 8).
- **C2.** What is distinctive about proto-cognition is not logic or communication as such. Gene-regulatory cascades have "a quite strongly computational character, with cascades of interactions that can be described in terms of ands, ors and nots" (PDF p. 8) — but computation alone is not the right category, because "computation also does not have any essential connection to life" and "A complicated collection of ands and if-thens with no metabolic point to them would not be the same sort of thing" (PDF p. 9). The distinctive thing is sensing and responding *in the service of self-maintenance*: "implementing a distinction between states and outcomes that are sought and maintained and other states and outcomes that are avoided. Boundaries between the system and its environment are controlled" (PDF p. 12).
- **C3.** Hence *proto-subjectivity*: simple organisms "are subjects and have a point of view in a richer sense than, say, a digital camera, which also senses and responds and computes, but does not control its boundaries and maintain a metabolism" (PDF p. 12). He is careful that this does not imply "first-person felt experience".
- **C4.** In organisms like us, the boundary between information-processing and metabolism is "very porous, not really a line at all" (PDF p. 11). Biological structures change from being used, are affected by what other parts are doing, and do not repeat: "If you watch how the same neuron responds to the same stimulus, it does not simply reproduce the same pattern of firing, but behaves slightly different each time" (PDF p. 11, citing Wu et al. 1994 and Gal et al. 2010). This is "not merely 'noise,' but raw material on which adaptive plasticity can be built."
- **C5.** Therefore the philosopher's non-living functional duplicate is "quite problematic", because "metabolic activity is part of the 'functional' profile of a human agent" (PDF pp. 11–12).
- **Meant to establish:** not that a machine could never have a mind, but that the *thought experiment* which assumes we can hold function fixed while removing life is ill-formed. This is the paper's single most consequential sentence for the computational-functionalism debate, and its form deserves emphasis: it is an **immanent** critique. It grants functionalism's framework and contests the inventory of functions.
- **Anticipated replies acknowledged in the text.** (i) *Computation is the real bridge, and it appears both inside and outside life* — he calls this "a reasonable point" and concedes it "shows that describing the biological role of computation, in an ordinary sense of that term, is not enough" (PDF p. 8); his response is to specify the narrower category in C2 rather than to deny the point. (ii) *Proto-cognition may be a quick and natural add-on to metabolism rather than inseparable from it* — he states this alternative fairly and declines to settle it: "there is both theoretical and empirical uncertainty about how closely proto-cognitive activities and metabolism are connected" (PDF p. 11).

#### Argument D — Biopsychism, and the transformation view (§5)

Two distinct arguments live here.

**D-i. The functionalist route to biopsychism** (PDF pp. 13–14).
- **D1.** Suppose the mental has a qualitative side and a cognitive side, both admitting of degree.
- **D2.** Suppose, with "a simple form of functionalism", that the qualitative "is just what the cognitive feels like from the inside", so the two are proportional.
- **D3.** Proto-cognition is present in the simplest metabolising systems, including bacteria (from C1).
- **D4.** Therefore minimal subjective experience should be found in very simple living things — *biopsychism*: all and only metabolically living systems have subjective experience.
- **D5.** Our inability to imagine this is a fact about our sympathetic imagination, not evidence against it: "that's our fault, not the fault of biopsychism."
- **Note the dialectical shape.** This is a *functionalist* argument reaching a *biological* conclusion, and Godfrey-Smith advances it without endorsing it ("I don't think biopsychism is insane, though I don't believe it"). He explicitly contrasts it with Tononi and Koch's integrated information view, which he characterises as holding that "all systems containing interactions that can be described in terms of information flow have some sliver of consciousness — even a simple non-living switching device" (PDF p. 13), and asks what tells against biopsychism "other than sheer weirdness".

**D-ii. Transformation versus latecomer** (PDF pp. 20–23).
- **D6.** Late-twentieth-century work assumed proportionality between cognitive and qualitative richness (Lycan's "Form, Function, and Feel", 1981, is named as exemplar).
- **D7.** Recent work rejects proportionality: much sophisticated processing has no felt side — early vision, unconscious language processing, the dorsal visual stream (Milner & Goodale), with global workspace (Baars, Dehaene) and working-memory (Prinz) theories identifying the special machinery that does have one.
- **D8.** The **latecomer view** concludes that subjective experience arrives only when that unification machinery evolves.
- **D9.** The **transformation view** concludes instead that the late machinery "greatly affect[s] the nature of subjective experience, but ... [doesn't] bring it into being"; it modifies earlier, simpler experience, "including pushing some stuff into the background" (PDF p. 22).
- **D10.** Evidence offered for D9: the intrusive, "imperious" character of sudden pain and of Denton's primordial emotions (thirst, air hunger). "Do you think that those things ... only feel like something because of the sophisticated cognitive processing in mammals that has arisen late in evolution? I doubt it."
- **Anticipated reply, stated and answered.** Reply: subjective experience needs a *subject*, and that needs psychological unity, which is exactly what the late machinery supplies. Answer: simpler animals have the relevant unity already "in virtue of ... status as a real sensorimotor unit, the locus of coordination between perception and action", firmed up by the Cambrian changes; and for a biopsychist, "metabolic life itself creates non-arbitrary units that are plausible subjects in a minimal sense" (PDF p. 23).
- **A terminological commitment worth recording**, because it recurs across this corpus. Godfrey-Smith prefers the older three-way division (qualia / consciousness / intentionality) to the now-standard equation of qualia with phenomenal consciousness, which he attributes to "writers who happen now to be at NYU (Nagel, Block, Chalmers)" (PDF p. 14). On his usage one can ask whether squid feel pain without asking whether squid are conscious. Papers in this corpus that use the merged vocabulary are therefore asking a narrower question than he is.

**No formalism.** The talk contains no equations. The only quantitative claims are reported empirical ones (collision rates in the molecular storm; archaeal swimming speeds near 400–500 body-lengths per second versus about 20 for E. coli and for a cheetah, PDF p. 10, n. 6).

### Appendix: Section-by-Section Backbone

1. **Header and contents (PDF p. 1).** NYU Modern Philosophy Conference, 2014. Explicit note that the material was split into two publications. Five-section outline.
2. **§1 Introduction (PDF pp. 1–2).** Goal: a picture in which the basis of living activity in physical processes, of proto-cognition in living systems, and of subjective experience in cognitive processes all make sense. Working through the links "will make a difference; things won't look the way they do when we just ask: how can consciousness exist in a physical system?" Self-described as a first sketch. Footnote 1 places the project in a tradition including Thompson's *Mind in Life* (which "has influenced this paper") and Deacon's *Incomplete Nature*.
3. **§2 Changing views of life and mind (PDF pp. 2–3).** Aristotle's souls as a bridge; Descartes' mechanistic life isolating the mind-body problem; emergentist revivals. The rise of computers and AI makes computation the bridging concept. Separately, the concept of life deflates into a cluster concept; textbooks are "relaxed" about life in a way they are not about other biological concepts. Verdict: reasonable developments, but "some of what has resulted is a wrong turn."
4. **§3 Matter at the scale of metabolism (PDF pp. 3–7).** The life cluster has a metabolic side and a reproduction-and-evolution side; asking which is primary is "a non-question". Metabolism at the nanoscale in water: Hoffman's molecular storm; work done by biasing random walks; Moore's "Macromolecular Devices Are Not Machines". No known simple metabolisms; the origin transition was disorderly-to-orderly, not simple-to-complex, because real chemistry has side-reactions. How contingent is this? Hoffman: the nanoscale is the only scale for autonomous machines; life "could not have arisen in a dry-land macroscopic realm". Application to conceivability arguments (Kripke, Chalmers) and to Leibniz's mill (quoted in full, PDF p. 7): the aqueous nano-mill is not the dry mill; the apparent antipathy of mental and physical is "much reduced"; a panpsychist reaction would be "another mistake". Closing concession that Leibniz might have been right about scaled-down macro-mechanism, which our brains anyway are not.
5. **§4 Life and cognition (PDF pp. 8–12).** Two generalisations: all uncontested minds are alive; all known living systems are proto-cognitive. Bacteria as the test case, split into gene-regulatory control (boolean-like cascades) and everything else (E. coli chemotaxis with short-term memory, contrasted with cruder magnetotaxis). The concession that computation "does not have any essential connection to life", and the narrower replacement category: sensing and responding *with a metabolic point*. Mycoplasma discussed and set aside as secondarily reduced parasites. Theoretical and empirical uncertainty about how tight the metabolism–proto-cognition link is; two candidate views (natural-addition versus inextricable). Multicellular cell-to-cell signalling as a further case. Porousness of the information/metabolism line in brains (hemo-neural hypothesis; trial-to-trial neural variability as raw material for plasticity). The non-living functional duplicate is "quite problematic"; metabolic activity is part of the functional profile. Proto-subjectivity versus the digital camera. Aristotle revisited: less gap between nutritive and sensitive soul than supposed.
6. **§5 Transitions in animal life (PDF pp. 12–23).**
   - *Framing (pp. 12–15).* The general points about life apply to bacteria and plants as much as to us, so continuity questions matter. Survey of "radically generous" views: full panpsychism (Strawson, Goff, sympathy from Chalmers) and Tononi–Koch integrated information. **Biopsychism** introduced (Haeckel 1892): all and only metabolically living systems have experience. Motivated by simple functionalism plus proto-cognition; not endorsed. Terminological preference for the older qualia/consciousness split; squid-pain example; Denton's primordial emotions as candidates for the most basic states that feel like something.
   - *5.1 Cytoskeleton (p. 15).* Controlled internal fibres allow shape change, hence richer behaviour and richer worthwhile sensing.
   - *5.2 Multicellularity (pp. 15–16).* Animals ~900 mya; differentiation of sensing, integrating and acting; almost all animals have nervous systems.
   - *5.3 Nervous systems (pp. 16–17).* ~700–800 mya, dates uncertain. Broad definition of neuron (electrically excitable cell influencing another by electrical or secretory means) versus narrow (adds specialised projections). Under the narrow definition, "all and only organisms with neurons also have muscle cells". First nervous systems may have served internal coordination and control of metabolism and development rather than real-time behaviour; Ediacaran bodies (no legs, antennae, complex eyes, shells, spines, claws; little or no predation) support this.
   - *5.4 Sensorimotor complexity and CABs (pp. 17–19).* The Cambrian: predation, arms races, image-forming eyes. Trestman's complex active bodies, quoted as a five-item cluster. Associative learning may date from here (Ginsburg & Jablonka 2010). "The mind evolved in response to other minds", and new bodies evolved in response to other minds.
   - *Figure and summary (pp. 19–20).* A tree of animal life with red shading for sensorimotor and cognitive complexity, offered as "a rough candidate map of the evolution of subjective experience", with explicit caveats. Two transitions emphasised overall: metabolic life, and animals with rich sensorimotor arcs.
   - *Closing: latecomer versus transformation (pp. 20–23).* Rejection of the proportionality assumption (Lycan 1981 as exemplar); dual-stream vision, global workspace, AIR theory. Latecomer view stated at its strongest, including the charge that "vague talk of 'gradients' in this area does not take seriously what we have been learning". Transformation view offered as the alternative, argued from the imperious intrusion of pain and primordial emotions. Unity objection stated and answered via sensorimotor unithood. Final footnote: Ingle's rewired frogs, which behave as if part of the visual world is mirror-reversed and part normal; Milner and Goodale say "the puzzle disappears", to which Godfrey-Smith replies, "What does it feel like to be a frog in this situation? Does it not feel like anything?"

**Deep-dive selection.** §3 and §4 were expanded in Phase 2 because they carry the two moves the later literature inherits — the re-description of living matter (which Seth 2025 cites in the same role) and the widening of "functional profile" to include metabolism, which is the strongest immanent challenge to computational functionalism in this batch. §5's biopsychism passage was included because it is the clearest statement in the corpus of a functionalist argument running *towards* a biological conclusion.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2016 (PDF is the 2014 talk version) · philosophy-of-mind
- Question it asked: What do the physical nature of metabolism and the evolutionary history of animal life contribute to the mind-body problem, and was it a mistake for computation to replace life as the bridge between mental and physical?
- Position in one line: Living matter at the metabolic scale is not machine-like, proto-cognition and self-maintenance come together in all known life, and therefore metabolic activity should be counted as part of a human agent's functional profile — which makes the philosopher's non-living "functional duplicate" problematic rather than routine.
- Stance on computational functionalism: restricts — it accepts a functionalist framework but argues the inventory of functions has been drawn too narrowly, excluding metabolism; it stops short of claiming that no non-living system could have a mind.
- Responds to / builds on: Hoffman, *Life's Ratchet* (2012); Moore, "How should we think about the ribosome?" (2012); Thompson, *Mind in Life*; Deacon, *Incomplete Nature*; Leibniz, *Monadology* §17; Kripke and Chalmers on conceivability; Nagel on sympathetic versus perceptual imagining; Haeckel (1892) for "biopsychism"; Tononi and Koch on integrated information; Strawson, Goff on panpsychism; Trestman (2013) on complex active bodies; Jékely, Keijzer & Godfrey-Smith on early neural evolution; Milner & Goodale, *Sight Unseen* (2005); Baars and Dehaene on the global workspace; Prinz's AIR theory; Lycan, "Form, Function, and Feel" (1981); Denton et al. (2009) on primordial emotions; Ginsburg & Jablonka (2010); Wu et al. (1994) and Gal et al. (2010) on neural variability
- Key move: the claim that "metabolic activity is part of the 'functional' profile of a human agent", together with the aqueous-nanoscale reply to Leibniz's mill, and the naming of *biopsychism* as the option between panpsychism and brain-based views
- Evidence type: conceptual analysis (with biological evidence marshalled in support; no new data)
- Testability: indirectly. The paper names empirical questions whose answers would move its argument — what the lower limits are on control-related activity in a real metabolism, i.e. whether a viable metabolism can exist with no proto-cognitive adjustment of activity to conditions; whether present-day metabolisms descend from simple ones or only from disorderly-complex ones; what early nervous systems in the Ediacaran actually did (internal coordination versus input-output); and whether associative learning dates from the Cambrian. It proposes no experiment of its own and offers no empirical test that would discriminate the transformation view from the latecomer view.

## 2. Seth & Tsakiris (2018) — Being a Beast Machine [tier: short]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Seth and Tsakiris 2018 - Being a beast machine.pdf`

**Version caveat.** This is the **accepted author manuscript** of the *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* article "Being a Beast Machine: The Somatic Basis of Selfhood" (2018, 22(11): 969–981, DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2018.08.008). It carries no journal pagination; citations below are PDF pages. It is complete: main text, Boxes 1–5, Outstanding Questions, Glossary and references are all present.

**Claim.** Experiences of being a self are built out of the brain's *control of the body's internal physiological state*, not out of information processing about the world. The authors call the mechanism **instrumental interoceptive inference**: the brain runs a predictive model of the body's internal condition and uses it primarily to *regulate* that condition, not to find out what it is. The title inverts Descartes. For Descartes, non-human animals were *bête machines* — flesh machines whose flesh was irrelevant to whether anything was experienced. Seth and Tsakiris argue instead that "we perceive the world around us, and ourselves within it *because of, and not in spite of*, the fact that we are beast machines" (PDF p. 14).

**Method / type.** A theoretical position piece (*TiCS* Opinion format), synthesising predictive-processing accounts of interoception, mid-twentieth-century cybernetics, and the free energy principle. No new data. Existing evidence is marshalled from body-ownership work (the rubber-hand illusion; asomatognosia and somatoparaphrenia), developmental interoception research, and computational psychiatry.

**Key argument, in steps.**
1. **Perception is prediction.** The brain encodes a generative model — prior beliefs as probability distributions — and perceptual content is carried by top-down predictions, with bottom-up prediction errors weighted by their inferred *precision* (inverse variance); optimising precision weighting is what attention amounts to (PDF pp. 4–5).
2. **Prediction errors can be cancelled by acting** (*active inference*). In the interoceptive case, this happens through autonomic reflexes — "intero-actions" (PDF p. 5).
3. **The point of having a brain is staying viable.** Ashby's *essential variables* — blood pressure, heart rate, blood sugar — must stay within bounds. His *ultrastability* handled excursions by random re-parameterisation, which the authors call "inefficient and biologically implausible" (PDF p. 6). Model-based control does better, because the essential variables are hidden causes that must be inferred, and because models permit *anticipatory* control: "Waiting for tightly regulated quantities like blood acidity to exceed their bounds before engaging compensatory responses may be lethal" (PDF p. 7). This is the move from homeostasis to **allostasis**, regulation through change.
4. **Epistemic versus instrumental inference.** Epistemic actions improve the model (find out more); instrumental actions exploit the model to regulate. Crucially, "there is no necessity that predictive models most useful for control are those that represent sensory signals and their causes in an action-independent, veridical manner" (PDF p. 9). Useful perception may be systematic misperception.
5. **This explains two features of how selfhood feels.** *Non-objecthood*: you experience a mug as having a back because your model encodes epistemic predictions about what you would see if you rotated it; interoceptive inference carries few such predictions, so "we do not experience 'being a body' in terms of the spatial arrangement of our internal organs as objects" (PDF p. 10). *Stability over time*: because essential variables must be held in tight ranges, the priors on them are precise, producing an adaptive "self-change-blindness" — "we will be better able to maintain our physiological and psychological integrity and identity over time if we do not (expect to) perceive ourselves as continually changing" (PDF p. 12).
6. **Three inversions.** (i) Content flows top-down, not bottom-up. (ii) Interoceptive inference is *not* a generalisation of visual predictive coding; rather "perceptual content in all modalities, including modalities such as vision, is a consequence or generalisation of a fundamental imperative towards physiological regulation" (PDF p. 13). (iii) The life–mind inversion, stated against Descartes and La Mettrie.

**Place in the field's history.** This is the paper where the predictive-processing programme — itself an unmistakably computational framework — is turned against substrate independence. The authors state the target explicitly: "This view pushes back against popular views of mind and self as substrate-independent forms of information processing" (PDF p. 13), and they graduate the claim: "At minimum, it suggests that mind and self cannot be understood without deep appreciation of the constraints and opportunities afforded by embodiment and allostasis. More radically, it underpins a strong continuity between life, mind, and consciousness" (PDF p. 13), citing Godfrey-Smith's *Complexity and the Function of Mind in Nature* (1996), Thompson's *Mind in Life* (2007) and Friston's "Life as we know it" (2013). It is the direct precursor of Seth's later, explicitly philosophical statement of biological naturalism (entry 5 below) and of his book *Being You*. Two distinctions it raises are load-bearing for later debate:
- **"Being a model" versus "having a model"** (Box 4, PDF p. 20). The free energy principle and the Conant–Ashby good regulator theorem ("every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system", 1970) only require that a system *behave* as if model-based; process theories like predictive coding require that neural states explicitly encode a generative model's sufficient statistics. The authors suggest the difference may matter for what selfhood is like, and note that it "recalls an old debate in cognitive science as to whether systems explicitly represent properties of their environment, or whether they merely act as if they do." This is precisely the implementation question that the philosophy-of-computation batch of this corpus takes up.
- **The open question posed to AI.** Among the Outstanding Questions: "If perceptions of self and world arise from fundamental imperatives towards allostatic regulation, what are the prospects for artificial intelligence and machine consciousness? Would a robot need to be 'alive' in order to be ascribed, or to ascribe itself, with selfhood?" (PDF p. 23). The paper poses this; it does not answer it.

**Relevance.** For a history of computational functionalism, the paper's significance is the *form* of its challenge rather than its strength. It does not argue that computation is the wrong category — its entire apparatus is computational. It argues that the computations that matter are **control computations over the organism's own viability**, which is a claim about what the system must be doing and for what, not about what it is made of. Whether that is compatible with substrate independence is left open here: a machine that genuinely had essential variables to defend would seem to satisfy the account, and the paper does not rule this out.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2018 · consciousness-science
- Question it asked: What makes experiences of *being a self* the way they are — non-object-like, and stable over time — and what does the answer imply about the relation between life and mind?
- Position in one line: Selfhood is grounded in instrumental (control-oriented) interoceptive inference serving allostatic regulation of physiological essential variables, so that experiences of embodied selfhood arise because of, not in spite of, our nature as flesh machines.
- Stance on computational functionalism: restricts — it uses computational (predictive-processing) machinery throughout but explicitly "pushes back against popular views of mind and self as substrate-independent forms of information processing", tying perceptual content to a living organism's regulatory imperative; it stops short of asserting that no artificial system could qualify, listing that as an outstanding question.
- Responds to / builds on: Descartes (*bête machine*) and La Mettrie (1748); Ashby on essential variables and ultrastability; Conant & Ashby (1970) good regulator theorem; Powers (1973) perceptual control theory; Friston's free energy principle and active inference; Craig (2003) on interoception; Seth (2013) interoceptive inference; Seth, Suzuki & Critchley (2011); Barrett & Simmons (2015); Pezzulo, Rigoli & Friston (2015); Clark (2013) and Hohwy (2013) on predictive processing; O'Regan & Noë sensorimotor contingency theory; Metzinger (*Being No One*, 2003) and Blanke & Metzinger (2009) on minimal phenomenal selfhood; Botvinick & Cohen (1998) rubber-hand illusion; James (1890) on "the same old body always there"; Godfrey-Smith (1996); Thompson (2007); Friston (2013) "Life as we know it"; Fotopoulou & Tsakiris (2017); Stephan et al. (2016); Barrett et al. on depression as an allostatic disorder
- Key move: the *beast machine* inversion of Descartes, together with the epistemic-versus-instrumental inference distinction used to explain why the embodied self does not feel like an object, and the "being a model versus having a model" distinction (Box 4)
- Evidence type: position piece (theoretical synthesis; no new data)
- Testability: yes, and the paper lists its own tests in an Outstanding Questions box — the neurophysiological signatures and localisation of interoceptive predictions and prediction errors; whether the proposed phenomenological difference between epistemic and instrumental inference can be tested in a non-interoceptive modality such as vision; whether allostatic targets are set points or ranges; developmental predictions about carer–infant interoceptive coupling (with the Infant Heartbeat Task named as an instrument); and competing computational-psychiatry accounts of depression that "future work employing computational psychiatry methods could arbitrate among". The paper also flags what is currently missing: "direct empirical evidence for interoceptive inference (e.g., interoceptive prediction errors) remains scarce", and "empirical evidence for interoceptive sensory attenuation remains to be found".
- PDF version: accepted manuscript

## 3. Cleeremans & Tallon-Baudry (2022) — Consciousness Matters [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Cleeremans and Tallon-Baudry 2022 - Consciousness matters.pdf` — the **published open-access version** of "Consciousness matters: phenomenal experience has functional value", *Neuroscience of Consciousness* 2022(1): niac007, pp. 1–11, in the special issue "Consciousness science and its theories". Received 1 May 2021, revised 4 February 2022, accepted 14 March 2022. Page citations below are journal pages.

**A note on fit.** This paper was assigned to the biological-naturalism batch, and it does end up close to the batch's conclusion — but it arrives there from a different direction, and for one stretch it argues in a direction that helps functionalism rather than hindering it. What it primarily is, is an argument that phenomenal consciousness *has a function*. Readers of the synthesis should treat it as a hinge paper rather than a straightforward member of the biological camp.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction — the problem in plain terms.** The question is: what is consciousness *for*? A long tradition says: nothing. Either *conscious inessentialism* (anything done consciously could in principle be done unconsciously) or *epiphenomenalism* (consciousness exists but causes nothing). Cleeremans and Tallon-Baudry find this implausible on its face, and open with the line that carries the whole paper: "Why would we do anything at all if the doing was not doing something to us?" (p. 1).

Their answer is a hypothesis they call **phenomenal worthiness**: subject-level experience — "what it feels like" — has *intrinsic value*, and because things with value guide behaviour, consciousness has a function.

**Key claims.**
- **Feeling something is worth something to you, over and above whatever biological good it does.** Their standing illustration is the difference between being fed intravenously and eating a meal. Both restore blood nutrient levels; only one is tasted. Or take a slug that assigns each food item a value equal to its calories; now add the hedonic experience of the food, and you have a second value that "can be quite different, and even wholly independent from the value associated with caloric and nutrient contents" (p. 2). Their proposal, stated flatly: "all subjective experiences have intrinsic value."
- **"Intrinsic" is used in the philosopher's sense** — good non-derivatively, as opposed to money, which is good only because of what it buys (they cite Lee 2019 and Zimmerman 2019). And value here can be *negative*: they adopt Lee's "neutral view" over Siewert's and Seager's "positive view", so that unpleasant experiences still count as valued rather than as counterexamples.
- **The *access* versus *phenomenal* distinction is called misleading.** Block's (1995) division between access-consciousness (the functions — global availability, report, reasoning) and phenomenal consciousness (the feel) is, they argue, "fundamentally misleading, precisely because it dissociates function from phenomenal experience" (p. 2, p. 8).
- **Three literatures, one construct.** Decision-making has "value" but has "rather neglected" its subjective aspect; emotion research has valence and arousal; consciousness research has qualia, richness and vividness but almost no notion of value at all. The authors argue all three are circling the same thing.
- **The AI diagnosis, stated twice and sharply.** "All reinforcement learning algorithms are driven by 'rewards', but such rewards typically only have extrinsic value — they define what the system will do with respect to extrinsic constraints, but they have zero value 'for' the system (i.e. AlphaGO zero) itself" (p. 3). And: "the problem with contemporary AI systems is not with further augmenting their computational prowess, but rather to design them in a way that they 'want things for reasons of their own'" (p. 7).
- **What the hypothesis buys.** Four explanatory payoffs are claimed: flexible and creative behaviour that evolution cannot explain (BASE jumping; crows sledging on a roof); internal drive and motivation ("Seeking new conscious (hence valuable) experiences thus constitutes a drive to engage in behaviour that does not need to be justified by further reasons", p. 6); the *unity* of consciousness, since a common value currency lets vastly different experiences be compared in one space; and a re-centring of the *experiencing subject*, quoting Frege: "It seems absurd to us that a pain, a mood, a wish, should rove about the world without a bearer, independently. An experience is impossible without an experiencer" (p. 8).
- **Not dualism.** The paper closes by anticipating the charge and rejecting it: "The short answer is an emphatic 'no'" (p. 9). They assume *phenomenal realism* and *phenomenal efficacy*, and lean on Christian List's (2019) argument that a mental state can be a difference-making cause precisely because it is *realization insensitive* — the same mental state can be realised by different neural patterns — which blocks Kim's causal exclusion argument.

**Initial takeaway.** For this corpus, the paper matters in two opposite directions at once. It is *helpful* to functionalism because it insists consciousness is a matter of function, and its anti-epiphenomenalism argument runs through multiple realizability — the very premise classical functionalism was built on. It is *unhelpful* because the function it identifies is one the authors think current computational systems conspicuously lack: nothing matters to them. And the authors' own guess about why is biological: "it also suggests a close association between consciousness and life, with consequences for the possibility of artificial consciousness or of consciousness in lab-grown brains" (p. 7), with the question of intrinsic motivation "connected to issues of life and death, i.e., to existence, and thus to life itself" (p. 6, citing Seth 2021).

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive — Argument Reconstruction

#### The main argument

- **P1.** Either phenomenal experience has a function, or it does not. The "does not" branch is conscious inessentialism or epiphenomenalism, including Dennett's and Frankish's "user illusion", or consciousness as an evolutionary spandrel (p. 1).
- **P2.** *Intrinsic value* is value a thing has non-derivatively, for its own sake — the terminus of a chain of "but why is that good?" questions (Lee 2019; Zimmerman 2019, quoted at p. 2).
- **P3.** *All* subjective experiences have intrinsic value (p. 2). Value may be positive or negative (Lee's neutral view), so unpleasant experience is not a counterexample.
- **P4.** "Things that have value typically have a function and contribute to guiding behaviour" (p. 2).
- **C1.** Therefore phenomenal consciousness has a function.
- **C2.** Therefore the hard problem becomes "a problem about function", and thus tractable by the ordinary tools of cognitive and computational neuroscience (abstract; p. 9).

Note the shape of C2 against Chalmers's own formulation, which the paper quotes: the hard problem is hard "precisely because it is not a problem about the performance of functions ... The problem persists even when all the performance of all the relevant functions is explained" (p. 8). The paper's strategy is not to solve the hard problem on Chalmers's terms but to deny its framing — if phenomenality *is* the function, there is no residue.

#### Support for P3, by evidence stream

The paper treats P3 as the load-bearing premise and supports it four ways.

**(i) Thought experiments (philosophical).**
- *Siewert's (1998) zombification offer.* You keep every non-phenomenal good and skill, including the ability to report on your mental states, but nothing feels like anything. Would you accept, for a large enough reward? The authors take the near-universal refusal as evidence for intrinsic value.
- *Kriegel's (2019) hardened version.* Choose between zombification and a life that "will skew constantly towards the unpleasant". Their intuition: most would take the unpleasant life — so "(moderately) unpleasant phenomenal experience is better than none whatsoever" (p. 3). This is the move that makes P3's neutral construal of value do real work.
- *Nozick's experience machine, re-purposed.* Plugged-in hallucination versus zombiehood: "it would be folly not to choose to get plugged in."
- Summary formulation: "most of us would probably prefer to be a poor conscious agent than a rich zombie" (p. 3).

**(ii) Decision-making.** Subjective value in this literature is a scalar on a common scale, "subjective only inasmuch as it is subject-dependent" (p. 3). The authors press a gap: models specify how values are compared, but "the specific computations that drive the reward function ... remain largely unspecified". Two wines with identical calories, one liked and one not — "No rule of evolution specifies or even constrains this subjective taste value." They adopt Juechems and Summerfield's (2019) **reward paradox**: "value must be inferred by the agent, not conferred by the world", and their gloss that value is "ontologically subjective but epistemically objective." Their proposed resolution: "the key for a reward to have 'intrinsic' value is precisely subjective experience, for in its absence, there is nobody for whom the reward means anything" (p. 3).

**(iii) Emotion.** Emotional behaviours run on evolutionarily constrained survival circuits with a limited repertoire — freezing, aggression, avoidance — but human emotions also involve rich feelings, and the two "can predict different outcomes" (p. 4). The BASE-jumping case is the wedge: danger-avoidance alone says never jump; the practice exists, so "risky behaviour can be accounted for, but only *if the subjective experience of risky behaviour has an intrinsic value*" that can override the evolutionarily selected safe option. Phenomenal states, on this view, "answer to a subjective rather than objective fitness function" (citing Hesp et al. 2021).

**(iv) Conscious perception — the one place they point to data.** The claim is that *every* act of conscious perception is automatically accompanied by a value assignment, even for nominally neutral objects. Supporting work cited: Barrett and Bar (2009) on affective prediction during object perception; Lebrecht et al.'s (2012) **micro-valence**, on which teapots, umbrellas and telephones elicit automatic affective judgements; and Lebreton et al. (2009), where the brain assigns preference values to faces, houses and paintings even when the task (age judgement) is unrelated to preference measured in a separate session. The authors' own assessment of this evidence base is candid: "while the evidence thus far is scant" (p. 4). Their prediction is stated explicitly and is testable: "value assignment is an intrinsic feature of consciousness and ... it occurs automatically. *Automatic value assignment is precisely what endows conscious perceptual experience with a function*" (p. 4).

#### The four claimed consequences

1. **Flexible, creative behaviour** (pp. 5–6). Conscious agents pursue experiences for their own sake: music, sledging, jokes, new recipes, experiential tourism. "Subjective experience gives value to what has no intrinsic value as defined by the rules of physics or biology." This buys "additional degrees of freedom" and explains even counter-adaptive action.
2. **Internal drive and motivation** (p. 6). The money-you-cannot-spend thought experiment, with a footnote to the clicker game *Universal Paperclips*. Applied to algorithms: "The only reason they act at all is because they are programmed to maximize a reward function, but paradoxically, the reward itself is not rewarding 'for' them." Clinical support offered: patient TN, cortically blind from bilateral V1 damage, navigated an obstacle-filled corridor successfully (de Gelder et al. 2008), yet "the lack of subjective experience deprived TN of the willingness to engage in navigation" in daily life. Converse support: psilocybin, where people seek intensified perception with "neither apparent meaning nor function". They note that empowerment (Klyubin et al.) "comes close to formalize the core idea that information is intrinsically valuable" but that without phenomenal experience "it remains unclear how to quantify its value beyond basic exploration of a space of possibilities."
3. **Unity, and possibly access** (p. 7). A common value currency makes heterogeneous experiences comparable ("would you rather go for a walk with friends or stay home and read?"), which "probably contributes to the unity of consciousness". They then invert the standard ordering: "perhaps it is in virtue of the fact that all our experiences take place in a unified phenomenal space that functional features such as global accessibility emerge, rather than the opposite" — a direct challenge to global-workspace-style accounts on which access explains unity.
4. **The experiencing subject** (pp. 7–8). Value is worth *for someone*, matching Nagel's definition of consciousness. They note the convergence of emotional-self (Damasio), bodily-self (Blanke & Metzinger) and perceptual-consciousness (Park & Tallon-Baudry) accounts on the monitoring of bodily signals from joints, muscles and viscera, citing Seth & Tsakiris 2018 — the link to [entry 2](#2-seth--tsakiris-2018--being-a-beast-machine-tier-short). They are careful to add the deflationary caveat themselves: "many feeding, regulatory or protective behaviours can be accounted for without introducing the notion of consciousness."

#### The zombie-exclusion sub-argument

Separately from the main argument, the paper endorses Austen Clark's (2000) reasoning that some patterns of access entail phenomenality (p. 8): a system reporting a colour difference either says "the answer just comes to it" (blindsight) or is non-inferentially aware of *which modality* it used — and in the latter case "must say that there is something it is like to see the difference rather than e.g. to smell it." Clark's conclusion, endorsed here: zombies are excluded, because "honest reports of genuine direct, non-inferential access to acts of perceptual difference detection imply the presence of genuine phenomenal differences."

They apply the same subject-centred complaint to the Cohen–Dennett versus Fahrenfort–Lamme dispute over a disconnected but functioning colour area V4: "What could it possibly mean for an isolated cortical region to 'experience' anything at all?" (p. 8).

#### The anti-epiphenomenalist coda, and why it matters to this corpus

To pre-empt the charge of dualism, they adopt List's (2019) level-of-description analysis (p. 9). Kim's causal exclusion argument says that if every mental state is realised by a physical state, the physical state does all the causing and the mental state is idle. List's reply, quoted here, turns on **realization insensitivity**: "When a higher-level difference-making relation is realization insensitive, it is not accompanied by a matching difference-making relation at the lower level", and so "It is a person's intentional mental states that are normally the difference-making causes of the person's actions, not the underlying physical states of the brain and body."

For a history of computational functionalism this is worth flagging, because it is *multiple realizability used as a premise* — the same premise Putnam and Fodor used to launch functionalism — deployed here to defend the causal efficacy of the phenomenal. The authors' conclusion is that conscious mental states are "causally efficacious in a way that cannot be wholly reduced to their biological foundations", while still being "entirely caused by and supervene on the biological activity of the brain."

#### Limitations the paper states about itself

Neutrality requires recording these, since they are the authors', not a reviewer's objections.
- The whole piece is offered as "mostly discussion and (informed) speculation" (p. 2).
- The perceptual evidence for automatic value assignment is "scant" (p. 4).
- Ginsburg and Jablonka's Universal Associative Learning — their leading candidate evolutionary marker — "does not appear to require subjective experience per se", and Miller's sexual-selection account works without phenomenality: "the peacock's tail is itself an illustration of how sexual selection can operate without appealing to phenomenal experience" (p. 5).
- Most reward-based decision-making and reinforcement-learning modelling succeeds without invoking conscious experience at all (p. 6).
- And the concession most relevant to a project studying aversive signals: "our proposal does not readily assign a satisfactory role to some negative experiences such as chronic pain" (p. 9).

**No formalism.** The paper states no equations. Value is discussed as a scalar or possibly multi-dimensional quantity, following the decision-making literature, but no model is written down.

### Appendix: Section-by-Section Backbone

1. **Abstract (p. 1).** Reverses the classical view that experience is an epiphenomenon. Proposes that subject-level experience has intrinsic value, and that this value explains motivation and preference. Names the hypothesis: *phenomenal worthiness*. Suggests phenomenal experience is "a mental currency of sorts" enabling comparison of vastly different experiences in a common subject-centred space, which "readily explains the fact that consciousness is 'unified'". Concludes that the hard problem becomes more tractable, "since it can then be reduced to a problem about function."
2. **What is the function of consciousness? (pp. 1–2).** Notes how little neuroscience and psychology have written on the topic (citing Seth 2009). Lays out the no-function options: conscious inessentialism, epiphenomenalism, the user illusion (Dennett, Frankish), the spandrel. Siewert's zombification offer. Block's A/P distinction introduced and immediately criticised as misleading; Frankish quoted: "a conceptual distinction does not necessarily mark an ontological one." The thesis stated: phenomenal experience has a function because it has intrinsic value.
3. **The intrinsic value of subjective experience (pp. 2–3).** Intravenous feeding versus a meal; the slug thought experiment; "all subjective experiences have intrinsic value". Intrinsic versus derivative value (Lee; Zimmerman; the money example; the altruism regress). Solms quoted: "There can be no such thing as a feeling that you do not feel." ## 4. Aru, Larkum & Shine (2023) — The Feasibility of Artificial Consciousness Through the Lens of Neuroscience [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Aru et al. 2023 - The feasibility of artificial consciousness through the lens of neuroscience.pdf`

**Version caveat.** This is the **author preprint** of the paper published in *Trends in Neurosciences* 2023, 46(12): 1008–1017 (DOI 10.1016/j.tins.2023.09.009). It has no journal pagination; the authors' own sequential page numbers (1–17, then references) are used below and marked "PDF p.". The three figures are present as captions describing the artwork; Figure 3 in particular is an ellipse diagram whose content is fully given in its caption. Box 1 is present.

**A note the synthesis should carry.** This paper is frequently cited as a flat denial that machines could be conscious. It is not. The authors state three disclaimers in their own words — they are not arguing that the mammalian brain is the only architecture that could support consciousness, not arguing that consciousness can only arise in living systems, and not claiming that consciousness cannot be captured within software at all. Their claim is about present-day large language models and about how far we are from understanding what would be needed. Quoting them out of those hedges misrepresents the position.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction — the problem in plain terms.** Talking to a large language model is persuasive: "Ask it a question, and it will provide you with an answer that drips with the kinds of nuance we typically associate with conscious thought" (PDF p. 2). Three systems neuroscientists ask whether that impression should be trusted, and answer no — not because conversation is a bad test in principle, but because the things neuroscience has actually tied to consciousness in mammals are all absent from these systems.

They narrow the question first. "Consciousness" covers *level* (whether you are conscious at all, the neurologist's sense), *content* (what you are conscious of, the psychologist's sense), and within content, *phenomenal* versus *cognitive/access* aspects. Their target is phenomenal consciousness: "whether machines can experience the world phenomenally" (PDF p. 5).

**The three arguments.**
1. **The *Umwelt* argument.** Jakob von Uexküll's term for the slice of the world an organism can perceive, paired with James Gibson's *affordances* — the information pragmatically available for action. Human vision spans roughly 380–740 nm, hearing 20–20,000 Hz; bees see ultraviolet, some snakes detect infrared. What is the *Umwelt* of a language model? Only binary-coded token patterns: "Text and strings of keystrokes on a keyboard are simply no match for the dynamic complexity of the natural world ... In this regard, the information stream provided to LLMs is more different from the one presented to humans than ours is from bats" (PDF p. 6) — a pointed inversion of Nagel.
   The argument is then strengthened against an obvious reply. *Could you not simply give the model richer inputs?* The authors concede you could, and say it would not be enough: "our Umwelt and conscious experience are not determined solely by sensory input. For example, consider lying in a floating tank where, despite a lack of normal sensory experiences, consciousness does not extinguish. This underscores the notion that having an Umwelt presupposes an inherent subjective perspective — that is, an agent to begin with" (PDF p. 6). Affordances depend on the agent's motivations and goals. Conclusion: "simply adding massive data streams to future AI systems will not lead to consciousness", and "Perhaps, to be conscious, the external world must be integrated with the internal needs and processes" (PDF pp. 6–7).
2. **The architecture argument.** Whatever the disagreements between consciousness theories, most place the dense, re-entrant *thalamocortical* network at the centre — cortical areas, cortico-cortical connections, and higher-order thalamic nuclei with diffuse cortical projections — along with the ascending arousal systems. The authors run through Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (a frontoparietal workspace that integrates then broadcasts), Integrated Information Theory (integration mainly in posterior cortex), binding-by-synchrony, and then their own preferred account, **Dendritic Integration Theory** (DIT), in which consciousness is tied to *thick-tufted layer 5 pyramidal neurons* whose two dendritic compartments process categorically different information — basal for externally-grounded input, apical for internally-generated input — and which burst-fire when the two coincide, gated by higher-order matrix-type thalamus. The verdict: "the architecture of present-day LLMs is devoid of features from each of these theoretical proposals: there is no LLM equivalent of dual-compartment pyramidal neurons, nor a centralised thalamic architecture, nor a global workspace, nor the many arms of the ascending arousal system" (PDF p. 9).
3. **The biological-organisation argument.** Architecture alone cannot be the whole story, because "the structure of the thalamocortical system does not change when we are in deep sleep or undergo anesthesia, yet consciousness disappears" (PDF p. 10). DIT supplies the missing process: anaesthetics functionally decouple the apical and basal compartments while leaving the neurons anatomically intact and able to fire, and this coupling is controlled by *metabotropic receptors* — "a special type of receptor not usually modeled in artificial neural networks" (PDF p. 11) — possibly under higher-order thalamic control. Widening out: living organisms are "constantly in the process of self-maintenance across several levels" and have an "organizational complexity ... that is not captured within present-day computer software" (PDF p. 12). The Krebs cycle is offered as an analogy, with the disclaimer attached: cellular respiration "is not compressible into software as processes like cellular respiration need to happen with real physical molecules. Note that our aim is not to suggest that consciousness requires the Krebs cycle, but rather to highlight that perhaps consciousness is similar: it cannot be abstracted away from the underlying machinery" (PDF p. 13). Their image for the worry: "It might be impossible to 'biopsy' consciousness and remove it from its organizational dwellings."

**Initial takeaway.** The paper's real target is an *assumption*: "This idea contradicts most of today's theories of consciousness which assume that consciousness can be captured on the abstract level of computation" (PDF p. 13). The authors do not refute that assumption; they argue it is an assumption rather than a finding, and that modern AI is the occasion to notice it. Their summary verdict on current systems is that LLMs "may be trapped in a compelling simulation of the signatures of consciousness, without any conscious experience", because those signatures "are implicitly embedded within the language that humans use to describe the richness of our conscious experience" (PDF pp. 14, 14).

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive — Argument Reconstruction

#### Preliminary: the question is narrowed twice

- **N1.** From "consciousness" generally to *phenomenal* consciousness (PDF p. 5). Level-versus-content and phenomenal-versus-cognitive distinctions are drawn, and the cognitive/access side is set aside.
- **N2.** From "could any machine be conscious?" to "are present-day LLMs conscious, and will they soon be?" The conclusion is correspondingly scoped: "LLMs are not conscious, and will likely not be conscious soon" (PDF p. 14).

They also reject conversational evidence as the test: interactions with LLMs "are not formal objective measures of consciousness and do not constitute prima facie evidence for conscious agency" (PDF p. 4). This matters because the entire popular debate is conducted on that evidence.

#### Argument 1 — Impoverished *Umwelt*, and why enrichment would not fix it

- **1.1.** An organism's *Umwelt* (von Uexküll) is the perceptually available slice of the world; its *affordances* (Gibson) are the action-relevant information within it. Both are set by the organism's body and brain.
- **1.2.** An LLM's inputs are binary-coded token patterns fed to a transformer. Although neuronal spikes are also arguably digital, "the information stream fed to the LLMs in question is highly abstract, and hence does not itself make robust contact with the world as it is in any way" (PDF p. 6).
- **1.3.** *Anticipated objection, granted:* "there is nothing stopping the input of future AI systems from being much more enriched", and such systems could in principle have an *Umwelt* broader than ours (PDF p. 6).
- **1.4.** *Reply:* an *Umwelt* presupposes an agent for whom it is an *Umwelt*, and affordances depend on the agent's own motivations and goals. Evidence offered: sensory deprivation in a flotation tank does not extinguish consciousness.
- **1.5.** Therefore "consciousness does not arise merely from data", and richer input streams alone will not produce it.
- **1.6.** Consequence for consciousness science, stated as a challenge to the field rather than only to AI: "many theories take the classic information processing view where consciousness 'pops out' at some elaborated stage of stimulus processing. The contrast between biological consciousness and LLMs demonstrates that we must reconsider our assumptions. Perhaps, to be conscious, the external world must be integrated with the internal needs and processes" (PDF p. 7).

Note the dependency: 1.4 is doing all the work, and it is asserted with one observation and three citations rather than argued at length. A functionalist reply would be that whatever "being an agent with motivations and goals" amounts to is itself a functional matter that could be implemented — a reply the paper does not take up here, though its third argument is aimed at it.

#### Argument 2 — Architectural mismatch

- **2.1.** The only systems we confidently attribute consciousness to are brains in bodies; the relevant systems-level features are shared across mammals and arguably some invertebrates (PDF p. 4).
- **2.2.** Theories of the neural basis of consciousness converge on the thalamocortical network plus arousal systems as the substrate of *conscious integration* — the fact that consciousness is unified despite distributed processing — while disagreeing about the mechanism:
  - *Global Neuronal Workspace Theory*: a frontoparietal workspace integrates from local processors and globally broadcasts; broadcast marks the conscious/non-conscious line.
  - *Integrated Information Theory*: integration mainly in posterior cortex.
  - *Binding-by-synchrony*: high-frequency inter-areal synchronisation (noted as "less influential today").
  - *Dendritic Integration Theory*: global integration also depends on local integration within single layer 5 pyramidal neurons, whose basal compartment carries externally-grounded and apical compartment internally-generated information; during conscious states the two are coupled, enabling system-wide integration.
- **2.3.** Present-day LLMs have none of the structures these theories name — no dual-compartment pyramidal neurons, no centralised thalamus, no global workspace, no ascending arousal system.
- **2.4.** *Explicit hedge:* "we are not arguing that the mammalian brain is the only architecture capable of supporting conscious awareness", but "the evidence from neurobiology suggests that very specific architectural principles (i.e., more than simple connections between integrate-and-fire neurons) are responsible for mammalian consciousness" (PDF p. 9).
- **2.5.** Therefore, "According to the best evidence from nature ... we are hesitant to ascribe phenomenal consciousness to present-day LLMs, which are topologically extremely simple in comparison."

**A three-way map the synthesis should keep.** In asking whether future systems could incorporate integration, the authors lay out the field's options cleanly (PDF pp. 9–10):
- On **GNWT**, integration "offers a relatively straightforward implementation", some AI systems already have workspace-like components, and "an artificial system with a computationally equivalent global workspace ought to be considered conscious by proponents of this theory."
- On **IIT**, "it is impossible for a software-based AI system to achieve consciousness because computer software does not have real cause-effect power necessary for integrating information."
- Their own position is a stated **third option**: "consciousness might be implementable in principle, but it might require a level of computational specificity that is beyond the present-day (and perhaps future) AI systems."

That third option is the paper's actual thesis, and it is a claim about *specificity*, not about substrate. This is the cleanest statement in the batch of a position that restricts computational functionalism without rejecting it.

#### Argument 3 — Consciousness may not be abstractable from biological organisation

- **3.1.** Architecture is insufficient: the thalamocortical system is structurally unchanged in deep sleep and anaesthesia, and even local gamma-band responses in primary sensory cortex can persist while consciousness is gone (PDF p. 10). So process, not just structure, distinguishes conscious from unconscious brains.
- **3.2.** DIT's candidate process, with empirical support: multiple anaesthetic agents "lead to functional decoupling between the two compartments" while leaving neurons anatomically intact and able to fire action potentials, so "the top-down feedback component cannot influence processing". The coupling is controlled by metabotropic receptors, with suggestive evidence that higher-order thalamus controls their activity (PDF p. 11). The authors flag that metabotropic receptors are "not usually modeled in artificial neural networks."
- **3.3.** Even this may be too coarse: current explanatory concepts — global workspace, integrated information, recurrent feedback, dendritic integration — may "miss the necessary computational details required to satisfactorily explain consciousness ... perhaps we simply do not have yet the right mathematical and experimental tools" (PDF p. 12). Figure 3 renders this as nested ellipses: the space of all possible computations exceeds the computations we understand; our understanding of biological computation partially overlaps it; AI computations overlap biological ones only partially; and therefore "a priori, there is little reason to think that the computations of present-day AI systems are related to computations underlying consciousness."
- **3.4.** Living systems are "constantly in the process of self-maintenance across several levels of processing" and have cross-level *organizational complexity* absent from software. AI models neural-circuit and network-level computation but "do not simulate the processes within the neurons. This is a reasonable abstraction if one assumes that consciousness occurs at the level of interactions between neurons. However, the truth is that we do not know whether that assumption is correct" (PDF p. 12).
- **3.5.** The Krebs-cycle analogy, with the disclaimer attached as above: cellular respiration cannot be run in software because it "need[s] to happen with real physical molecules"; the suggestion is only that consciousness may be *similar* in not being abstractable from its machinery.
- **3.6.** *Explicit hedges, twice:* "here we are not arguing that consciousness can only arise in living systems", and "we are not claiming that consciousness cannot be captured within software at all. Rather, we emphasize that we have to at least entertain the possibility that consciousness is linked to the complex biological organization underlying life, and thus any computational description of consciousness will be much more complex than our present-day theories suggest" (PDF pp. 12–13).
- **3.7.** Conclusion, conditional in form: "It might be that although AI systems (at least to some extent) mimic their biological counterparts on the level of network computations, in these systems we have abstracted away all the other levels of processing that causally contribute to consciousness in the living brain. Hence, we have abstracted away consciousness itself" (PDF pp. 13–14).

#### Box 1 — "Skin in the game", and the ethical corollary

The paper's moral argument is short and worth stating exactly, because it is the part most likely to be contested. The authors "predict that LLMs do not (and will not) have experiences that can be considered suffering in any sense that should matter to human society" (PDF p. 15). The reasoning, borrowing Taleb's phrase:
- An LLM "could, in principle, claim in a conversation that it does not want to be shut down, but an LLM does not have 'skin in the game', as there is no real consequence to the software when it is actually shut down."
- A biological system "has something to lose on several levels: it cannot stop living, as otherwise it will die", quoting Hans Jonas: "The organism has to keep going, because to be going is its very existence."
- The stake is nested: "If cellular respiration stops, the cell dies; if cells die, organs fail; if organs fail, the organism will soon die."
- Hence "not having the capacity for phenomenal consciousness would preclude suffering and therefore personal investment."
- A further, unusual consequence is drawn about law: penalties from fines to incarceration presuppose personal investment, so without it "these disincentives will not be taken seriously by injured parties and would therefore likely destabilize the rule of law."
- They also state the opposing view fairly before rejecting it: some argue we should be cautious about AI suffering because "some experiences could be negative and that in this case the AI could also suffer."

Note the direction of the final inference in that chain: they reason *from* the absence of phenomenal consciousness *to* the absence of suffering, while using the absence of skin in the game as an argument for the absence of phenomenal consciousness. Whether that is a circle or two independent supports depends on how one reads the Box; the text does not disambiguate it.

**What the paper offers positively.** It is explicit that the argument is "Rather than representing a deflationary account": understanding the topological differences should advance machine learning (by mimicking brain organisation) and neuroscience (by learning how simple distributed systems process elaborate information streams) (PDF pp. 14–15).

**No formalism.** The paper states no equations. Figure 3's ellipse diagram is a qualitative Venn-style illustration, not a formal construction, and is not re-derived here.

- Year · community: 2023 · biology-and-neuroscience (systems neuroscience, addressed to the AI-consciousness debate)
- Question it asked: Should the conversational competence of large language models lead us to think they are, or soon will be, phenomenally conscious?
- Position in one line: No — their input stream is disembodied and presupposes no agent, their architecture lacks every structure that mammalian consciousness research implicates (thalamocortical loops, arousal systems, dual-compartment layer 5 pyramidal neurons, a global workspace), and consciousness may not be abstractable from the multi-level self-maintaining organisation of living systems; so present-day systems most likely mimic the *signatures* of consciousness without the thing itself.
- Stance on computational functionalism: restricts — the authors explicitly decline to say that only brains or only living systems could be conscious, or that software could never be conscious; their stated position is the third option, that consciousness "might be implementable in principle, but it might require a level of computational specificity that is beyond the present-day (and perhaps future) AI systems", and that the field's assumption that consciousness can be captured at an abstract computational level is an assumption rather than a result.
- Responds to / builds on: von Uexküll (*Umwelt*); Gibson (affordances); Nagel (the bat, inverted); Dehaene and Baars (Global Neuronal Workspace Theory); Tononi and colleagues (Integrated Information Theory, including its claim that software lacks real cause-effect power); binding-by-synchrony; Larkum's Dendritic Integration Theory and the apical-basal decoupling work on anaesthesia; Hans Jonas on the organism; Taleb ("skin in the game"); Seth, Baars & Edelman (2005), Edelman & Seth (2009), Birch, Schnell & Clayton (2020) on animal consciousness; the AI-consciousness literature it disputes (including workspace-implementation proposals and arguments that LLMs may be conscious)
- Key move: the *Umwelt*-of-an-LLM framing ("the information stream provided to LLMs is more different from the one presented to humans than ours is from bats"), the catalogue of consciousness-implicated brain features absent from transformer architectures, and the "skin in the game" argument that a system with no stake in its own continuation cannot suffer
- Evidence type: review / position piece (no new data; empirical support drawn from anaesthesia and dendritic-physiology literature)
- Testability: yes, at one remove. The paper's neurobiological claims are directly checkable and partly already tested — that anaesthetic agents functionally decouple apical and basal compartments of layer 5 pyramidal neurons while leaving spiking intact, that this coupling is controlled by metabotropic receptors, and that higher-order thalamus may control that receptor activity; and that consciousness can vanish while thalamocortical structure and primary-sensory gamma responses persist. These make Dendritic Integration Theory falsifiable and give the architecture argument an empirical anchor. The broader claim — that consciousness may not be abstractable from multi-level biological organisation — is offered as a possibility to be entertained, with no proposed test, and the authors say as much.
- PDF version: preprint (author version of the *Trends in Neurosciences* article)

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction — the problem in plain terms.** As AI gets more capable, people increasingly ask whether it might be conscious. Seth's answer has three parts. First: much of the intuition that it might be comes from psychological biases rather than evidence. Second: the belief that computation could suffice for consciousness — *computational functionalism* — is an assumption the field makes rather than a result it has established, and there are good reasons to doubt it. Third: there is a positive alternative, *biological naturalism*, which he defines here as "the claim that consciousness is a property of only (but not necessarily all) living systems" (PDF p. 17), and which he builds from predictive processing, cybernetics, autopoiesis and the free energy principle rather than from any appeal to vitalism.

His overall verdict is deliberately probabilistic rather than a proof: "real artificial consciousness is unlikely along current trajectories, but becomes more plausible as AI becomes more brain-like and/or life-like" (abstract). And the paper's closing sentence states its motivation: "If we sell our minds too cheaply to our machine creations, we not only overestimate them — we underestimate ourselves."

**Key moves.**
- **Intelligence is not consciousness.** Intelligence is "what a system can do" — Legg and Hutter's "ability to achieve goals in a wide range of environments". Consciousness is Nagel's: "for a conscious organism, there is something it is like to be that organism". Seth treats consciousness as synonymous with awareness, and deliberately avoids "sentience" as ambiguous between mere responsiveness and necessarily valenced experience.
- **Three biases plus one.** *Anthropocentrism* (reading the world in human terms), *human exceptionalism* (humans as distinct and superior), *anthropomorphism* (projecting human properties outward), plus a "technophilic temptation" to see a Kurzweilian singularity approaching. Together they produce the "royal road" picture in which smarter machines cross a threshold into consciousness. His sharpest illustration is linguistic: we say language models "hallucinate" when they produce falsehoods, which implicitly attributes experience to them; "It would be more appropriate to say that they 'confabulate'" (PDF p. 4).
- **Computational functionalism defined and scoped.** "the view that computations of some kind are sufficient to instantiate consciousness", derived from functionalism, the claim that mind depends on what a system does rather than what it is made of. Seth fixes "computation" to mean *Turing* computation unless qualified, since that is what conventional AI runs on, and notes that not every function is Turing-computable — the halting problem, continuous variables, stochastic elements, deterministic chaos. He flags that Butlin, Long and colleagues' influential review of conscious AI "confine themselves to theories and models that assume computational functionalism, and explicitly acknowledge that their conclusions depend on whether this assumption holds" (PDF p. 6).
- **A new term: substrate *flexibility*.** Seth argues that unrestricted substrate independence is implausible — "some physical substrates might not be up to the job" — and so introduces *substrate flexibility* for the weaker claim "that (conscious) mental states are not tied to carbon-based biological substrates — that they might be realisable in some other, but not necessarily all, types of material" (PDF p. 7). Conscious AI requires substrate flexibility to hold at least for silicon.
- **The neural-replacement thought experiment, and four lines of reply.** Replace neurons one at a time with functionally identical silicon parts; consciousness should not plausibly fade or dance; so accept silicon flexibility. Seth reports Block's charge that it *begs the question* (if consciousness is substrate-dependent, something *would* happen), notes that "the change of experience does not always entail the experience of change", then gives plausibility arguments from Godfrey-Smith (metabolic constraints, electromagnetic fields, fine-grained timing will make the replacement behave differently inside and out), from Rosa Cao (evolved mental functions are *generatively entrenched* — thick dependencies that constrain alternative implementations; nitric oxide diffusing freely across membranes; spikes that serve a homeostatic role by preventing build-up of reactive oxygen species, so that spiking "cannot be decoupled from its metabolic foundations"), and from Derek Shiller (a liberal functionalism would attribute mind to a bucket of water carved up the right way, so constraints on what counts as a part are needed, and current neural networks do not meet them). His summary is careful: "These plausibility arguments do not disprove silicon substrate flexibility, but they do challenge assumptions that it must be the case. Brains seem to be the kind of thing for which it is hard and perhaps impossible to separate what they do from what they are" (PDF p. 9).
- **Mortal computation.** The argument Seth says he finds most provocative "because it is based on limitations arising from within a computational view of mind" (PDF p. 10). Standard Turing computation is *immortal*: software outlives any hardware instance, which requires constant error correction to keep 1s as 1s, at growing energetic cost. Brains are highly energy-efficient, so if they compute at all, their computations are likely *mortal* — inseparable from the wetware running them — which removes the substrate flexibility conscious AI needs.
- **Simulation is not realisation.** "Nothing gets wet in a weather forecasting computer" (PDF p. 14). Simulating a mechanism realises the target property only if computation is sufficient for that property — which is exactly what is at issue. He also anticipates the standard rejoinder that a simulated rainstorm would feel wet to a simulated agent, and rejects it: "The objection doesn't work because it presupposes that computation is sufficient for experience."
- **The positive case, built from two directions.** *From predictive processing*: perception is the brain's best guess, "controlled hallucination"; interoceptive inference is *instrumental* (control-oriented) rather than epistemic; so "the evolutionary driver for predictive processing as a general mechanism underlying conscious perception may have been a fundamental biological imperative for allostasis — for staying alive" (PDF p. 16). *From the free energy principle*: living systems resist dispersion of their states; free energy is a tractable bound they can actually minimise; this runs all the way down into *autopoiesis* — self-production and self-boundary-maintenance (Maturana and Varela), which computers and factories lack, being merely *allopoietic*, "other producing". "For autopoietic systems there really is no way to separate what they are from what they do" (PDF p. 17).
- **What biological naturalism is not.** Not biopsychism (not all life is conscious): "just as there can be wind without a storm, there can be life without consciousness" (PDF p. 27). Not carbon chauvinism: non-carbon living systems might qualify. Not neo-vitalism.
- **Five scenarios**, from *naïve along for the ride* through *theory-based computational*, *substrate-dependent computational* (including a "computational biological naturalism" on which only living systems can implement the relevant computations), *substrate-dependent weak* (non-computational functional properties), to *substrate-dependent strong* (consciousness depends on intrinsic properties of living material). Seth's own case is drawn to the last: "conscious AI would need to be 'living' AI."
- **Ethics, split in two.** *Real* artificial consciousness would risk "a mass inauguration of new forms of suffering" that we might not even recognise as suffering; developing it for its own sake "should not be an explicit goal". *Conscious-seeming* AI is far more likely and raises its own problems: impressions of machine consciousness may be cognitively impenetrable, like the Müller-Lyer illusion, leaving a forced choice ("West World scenario") between distorting the circle of moral concern and "brutalising our own minds."

**Initial takeaway.** This is the most systematic statement in this batch, and the only one that takes computational functionalism itself as its direct object rather than a particular AI system. Its rhetorical structure is worth noting because it shapes how the rest of the field responds: Seth repeatedly declines the stronger claim available to him. He does not argue that conscious machines are impossible; he argues that the assumption they are possible has been doing unearned work, and he offers a positive rival with, in his own summary, symmetric epistemic standing: "Simply noting that all known examples of conscious systems are biological and alive suggests that biological naturalism is no more speculative than computational functionalism" (PDF p. 30).

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive — Argument Reconstruction

The paper contains a negative case (§§2–3), a positive case (§4), a taxonomy (§5) and an ethical argument (§6). Seth himself splits it this way at PDF p. 22: "My case against conscious AI has two parts ... If the negative arguments are on track, conscious AI will not be possible for the current substrate-independent trajectories of AI. If the positive arguments are on track, then real artificial consciousness will only be possible if we create machines that are also in some relevant sense alive."

#### The dependency chain that organises everything

- **D1.** Conscious AI (on conventional digital hardware) requires that **computational functionalism** be true — that computations of some kind suffice for consciousness.
- **D2.** It additionally requires **substrate flexibility** extending to silicon — that the relevant computations can actually be implemented there.
- **D3.** "Computational functionalism is sufficient for substrate flexibility. But it is not necessary" (PDF p. 8). One can have some substrate flexibility without computationalism; and conversely, evidence of substrate flexibility supports computational functionalism, because computationalism explains it.
- **D4.** Therefore attacking either D1 or D2 undermines conscious AI, and the two attacks reinforce one another.

#### Negative case, part 1 — the debunking argument (§2)

- **N1.** Anthropocentrism plus human exceptionalism make us treat distinctively human capacities — above all language — as bundled with consciousness. Seth traces this to Descartes, "for whom the immaterial, rational mind both endowed humans with the kind of consciousness that mattered, and separated humans from other animals."
- **N2.** Anthropomorphism makes us project human properties onto systems displaying those capacities. Useful as Dennett's intentional stance; misleading when it licenses attributions that yield poor explanations.
- **N3.** A technophilic bias adds urgency: exponential-change intuitions ("From any point on an exponential curve the future looks impossibly steep, while the past looks irrelevantly flat"), plus "a posthuman desire to transcend the limits of biological mortality by uploading one's consciousness."
- **N4.** Therefore the prevalence of the belief that AI is on a road to consciousness is substantially explained without its being true. **Note the argument's exact scope**, stated by Seth: "we are freed from assuming that making machines smarter will inevitably make them conscious, even though we may become more susceptible to judging them as being conscious" (PDF p. 4). This is a debunking of the intuition, not a refutation of the thesis — and Seth says so by immediately moving to the deliberate-design alternative.

#### Negative case, part 2 — against computational functionalism and substrate flexibility (§3)

**(a) Scoping computation (§3.1).** Turing machines; the Church–Turing thesis; universal Turing machines as the basis of conventional AI; McCulloch and Pitts, with Kleene's result (flagged in a footnote as strictly showing equivalence to finite-state automata, becoming Turing-equivalent only with unbounded storage). Non-Turing-computable functions exist. Hence *non-computational functionalism* (Piccinini) is a live option: mental states might depend on non-computational functions such as continuous electromagnetic fields, fine-grained timing, freely diffusing neurotransmitters, or functions defined by material properties (digestion, circulation, metabolism). And "it could be that some aspects of mind are computational, but not consciousness". A footnote distinguishes a "modest" physical Church–Turing thesis (anything a physical system *computes* is Turing-computable), which he accepts as compatible with his argument, from a "bold" version (anything a physical system *does* is Turing-computable), which he rejects. He also warns against using "information processing" as a synonym for computation, since Shannon information "is best thought of as a descriptive method for quantifying uncertainty" and can be applied to anything.

**(b) Diagnosing the appeal (§3.2).** Functionalism answered real problems with behaviourism, identity theory and dualism, and accommodated multiple realizability — with the caveat Seth stresses twice, that "multiple realisability does not imply that any mental state can be implemented in any physical system." Computational functionalism's specific appeal is traced to "a mathematical marriage of convenience between a specific view of computation — the Turing machine — and a drastic simplification of the messy multiscale biological complexity of the brain — the artificial neural network." The inference he rejects: "The fact that mental processes can often be usefully described in terms of computation is not sufficient to conclude that the brain actually computes." Quoted from Oshan Jarrow: "if we keep making the assumption without returning to it, the question itself begins to disappear."

**(c) Against neural replacement (§3.3).** Reconstructed above. Two structural points deserve isolating. First, Block's objection is that the thought experiment *assumes what it sets out to show*. Second, the plausibility objections are explicitly **nomological, not logical** — they concern what can happen given the laws and contingencies of this world. Seth defends that choice in a footnote aimed at conceivability arguments generally: "it is hard to draw useful conclusions about the world we live in from 'just imagining' situations for which there are good reasons to believe cannot (nomologically) be the case ... The (in)famous zombie argument suffers similarly." The same objections, he notes, apply to Chalmers's dancing-qualia argument.

**(d) Broader computation (§3.4).** Mortal computation; Piccinini's *neural computation* involving both continuous and discrete signalling; Kirkpatrick's *biological computation*; and from the engineering side analogue computation, neuromorphic hardware (spiking chips, analogue approaches), and biological neural networks interfaced with conventional I/O. The generalisation: "It is plausible that the more analogue or neuromorphic hardware becomes, the less substrate flexibility there is." The footnote example is memorable and apposite: Adrian Thompson's genetic-algorithm-optimised FPGAs exploited spatial relationships to the chip's power supply and, in some configurations, ambient room temperature — function welded to a particular physical layout. Seth then closes the escape route: yes, a Turing machine could *simulate* mortal computations, "But the computations themselves would be different, and the simulations could not be guaranteed to be exact"; and redefining computational functionalism for these broader senses "would be weakened, given the diminished substrate flexibility."

**(e) Separation of scales (§3.5) — the paper's one genuinely empirical lever.** Software's substrate flexibility rests on a strong separation of scales: engineers build bridges without quantum mechanics. Recently developed measures formalise *informational closure* and *causal closure*: a macroscopic variable or coarse-graining is closed when observations of it (informational) or interventions on it (causal) suffice to predict or determine the higher-level outcome, so that lower-level knowledge adds nothing. "This is guaranteed by design for (properly functioning, conventional) computers. But it would be a mistake to assume that these forms of closure hold for complex systems generically. In particular, thanks to the generative entrenchment inherent in evolutionary design, they may be partially or wholly absent in biological systems" (PDF p. 11). And then the payoff: "In practice, such measures may help empirically determine whether, or to what extent, neural dynamics can be abstracted away from finer-grained levels of description." This converts a philosophical dispute about multiple realizability into a measurable question about a given neural system — the most testable proposal in this batch.

**(f) Beyond computational functionalism (§3.6).** Stated concession first: "The arguments so far do not disprove computational functionalism. But they do render it less plausible, and less appealing." Other arguments are gestured at, not pressed — Searle's syntax/semantics distinction (with a footnote spelling out its observer-dependence form: if software's functions require an observer to attribute semantics, computational functionalism would make consciousness observer-dependent, "which it isn't"), intentionality, and Penrose's Gödel argument. Then the positive picture of the brain that motivates suspicion: "When we look inside a brain, we do not find anything like a sharp distinction between 'mindware' and 'wetware' of the kind we find between hardware and software in a computer ... A single neuron is a complex biological system in and of itself, busy maintaining its own integrity and regenerating the conditions for its own material existence. The metaphor of the brain as a computer is an increasingly inconvenient fiction" (PDF p. 12). Non-computational functionalism, dynamical systems approaches, 4E cognitive science and neurophenomenology are surveyed as the alternatives — with the honest caveat that "they are in general non-committal about whether implementing the dynamical principles proposed by a given theory, in some alternative material, would be sufficient to instantiate consciousness", and that they face the same substrate-flexibility problem once the in-principle independence of computation is given up.

**(g) Simulation / implementation / realisation (§3.7).** A three-way distinction the synthesis should carry verbatim, since much of the corpus's confusion lives here.
- *Simulate*: abstract a mechanism as a Turing computation and run it. "Any mechanism can be simulated, though these simulations may only be approximate (and may be wildly inaccurate)."
- *Implement*: realise finer-grained or non-computational aspects in some other way — neuromorphic engineering, synthetic biology — "The limit of this approach is a physically identical replication."
- *Realise (instantiate)*: whether simulating or implementing actually produces the property in question.
The key asymmetry: "For a simulation of X to be guaranteed to realise X, one would have to be confident that computation is sufficient for X", which is trivially true when X is itself a computation and obviously false for rainstorms and digestion. Whereas "implementing the neurobiological mechanisms underlying consciousness, at the right level of granularity, would by definition realise consciousness."

**(h) The parallel argument about intelligence (§3.8).** Deliberately asymmetric, and often missed. "Because intelligence is broadly about doing, sufficiently accurate simulations may count as realisations, even if underlying mechanisms and intrinsic properties are different." So language models really do mimic aspects of language and computers really do play chess. "But for consciousness to be instantiated computationally in any form, one needs to assume computational functionalism." He does allow a provocative possibility on the intelligence side too, via Michael Levin: some intelligence-relevant organisation might "always escape functionally adequate simulation or implementation in alternative substrates."

#### Positive case — biological naturalism (§4)

**(a) The predictive-processing route (§4.1).** Perception as approximate Bayesian inference implemented as prediction-error minimisation down a hierarchy of generative models; action as another way to minimise error (*active inference*); conscious contents as the brain's top-down best guesses, the "controlled hallucination" formulation. Extended inward: emotion and mood as *interoceptive* inference. The critical distinction is again epistemic versus instrumental: "Instrumental inference prioritises control over discovery. In instrumental inference, prior beliefs can serve as targets or set points." This yields a phenomenological pay-off: visual experience has spatiality and object-relatedness, matching epistemic prediction's job of figuring out what is there; emotional experience is shaped primarily by valence, matching control-oriented prediction's job of allostatic regulation.
From this he derives **epistemic biological naturalism**: "human conscious experiences can only be understood in light of our nature as living, self-sustaining organisms" (PDF p. 17), with a speculative extension — a minimal "ground state" of conscious experience that is "an inchoate, shapeless, formless feeling of simply 'being alive'", scaffolding all other contents.
The escalation step: the imperative to stay alive "doesn't bottom out at any particular 'implementation level' in our biology", since even single neurons maintain their own integrity (the homeostatic-spiking finding again). "If there is no clear boundary separating the substrate from the processes and dynamics that arise from it, then it is harder to motivate the intuition — central to computational functionalism — that the substrate does not matter." He explicitly borrows the "skin in the game" framing from **Aru et al. (2023)** — see [entry 4](#4-aru-larkum--shine-2023--the-feasibility-of-artificial-consciousness-through-the-lens-of-neuroscience-tier-full) — and then reaches **autopoiesis**: cells "do not merely implement functions, they also continuously regenerate their own material basis (self-production) and integrity (self-identification)", where computers and factories are *allopoietic*.

**(b) The free-energy route (§4.2).** Run in the opposite direction, from thermodynamics up to experience. The chain, as the paper states it (no derivation is attempted here, per the brief's no-invented-mathematics rule):
1. Autopoietic systems actively resist the dispersion of their internal states that the second law would otherwise mandate, so they must occupy low-entropy states far from equilibrium. Seth corrects the usual gloss: "It is better to say that living systems take advantage of the second law, since it is the transformation of low entropy fuel/food into high entropy products through metabolism that enables living systems to remain in non-equilibrium (quasi) steady-states."
2. A system cannot directly measure its own entropy. Variational free energy is a measurable upper bound (strictly, per his footnote, an upper bound on *surprisal*, whose long-term average under non-equilibrium steady-state assumptions is entropy), and is a function of sensory signals, hence accessible.
3. Under a Gaussian (Laplace) assumption, free energy can be read as sensory prediction error — with the footnoted precision that "prediction errors are associated with free energy gradients, rather than with free energies per se."
4. Minimising free energy is equivalent to maximising the evidence lower bound, so posterior beliefs approach optimal ones and sensory evidence for the model is maximised. Hohwy's phrase is adopted: organisms "self-evidence".
5. Two physics results are invoked to deepen the metabolism/inference link: the **Jarzynski equality**, which bounds average thermodynamic work between states by the free energy difference, applying specifically to non-equilibrium processes and so "endow[ing] processes like metabolism with a free energy formulation"; and the **Landauer principle**, of which Jarzynski's equality is described as a generalisation, placing a lower bound on the thermodynamic energy needed to erase one bit — hence "a minimal thermodynamic cost to inference in predictive processing." Sengupta and colleagues are cited for the result that minimising variational free energy under active inference yields both metabolic and inferential efficiency: "when predictions are accurate, thermodynamically costly state changes are not necessary."
6. Conclusion, stated with its own hedge: the free-energy minimisation "driving metabolism, autopoiesis, and perception is not just metaphorically equivalent, but is in some physical sense is the same thing. That said, there is still debate over exactly what this equivalence means, and the free energy principle itself, at least in its larger claims and its relation to autopoiesis, also remains much debated" (PDF p. 19, with five citations to critics).
**The reflexive objection Seth raises against himself:** does this not make his own account computational, since predictive processing is a "Bayesian dance of prediction and prediction error"? His answer: "Predictive processing in biological systems is a dynamical and (plausibly) substrate-dependent process, grounded in minimisation of a continuous quantity (free energy) ... In this sense, predictive processing becomes a dynamical, non-computational theory ... This process can be abstracted computationally, but these algorithms are pale shadows of the embodied and embedded processes unfolding in biological brains and bodies" (PDF pp. 19–20). Whether that reply succeeds is exactly the point on which the paper's critics can be expected to press; the article does not argue it further.

**(c) Supporting lines (§4.3).** Nick Lane on bacterial membrane potentials — dynamically maintained by metabolism and providing "an integrated, valenced, and action-oriented read-out of the physiological condition of the organism in relation to its environment" — with the membrane as a Markov blanket and mitochondria (evolutionarily derived from bacteria) proposed to generate electromagnetic fields relevant to neuronal firing and possibly to anaesthesia. Michael Levin's xenobots and anthrobots — self-organising assemblages of amphibian or human cells with competences unlike their evolutionary history, e.g. "motile anthrobots made from normally sessile human tracheal cells" — with Seth's own caveat that "there is not yet any direct connection to consciousness here". What they show, in his reading: "Instead of a computational rigidity, there is an excitable fluidity to the matter of life."

**(d) Answering the "isn't this just identity theory?" worry (§4.4).** Via his "real problem" approach: as we get better at explaining, predicting and controlling properties of consciousness in terms of mechanisms, "the sense of mystery about how consciousness relates to matter will dissolve" — with the historical analogy of vitalism dissolving, and an acknowledged objection to that analogy (Chalmers 2003). "If features of being alive explain (and predict and control) features of being conscious, then a necessary relation between the two becomes more plausible." The point of this section is to show that biological naturalism can be an *explanatory* thesis rather than a brute identification.

**(e) Two strengths of the view, distinguished (§4.5).** An **epistemic** version (consciousness can only be *understood* in light of our living nature — "the easiest to defend, though it relies on accepting predictive processing as a useful explanatory framework") and an **ontological** version (consciousness is in some stronger way dependent on the living substrate). Both are offered as positive arguments rather than as fallback positions. Three explicit disavowals close the section: not biopsychism, not carbon chauvinism, no neo-vitalism.

#### The taxonomy (§5) — five scenarios

Reconstructed from the section text; Table 1's cells are an unextractable image, though its caption notes that broadening "computation" beyond Turing "may blur" scenarios 3 and 4, and that "Biological naturalism falls within scenarios 4 and 5 (and potentially 3, for 'computational biological naturalism')".

| # | Scenario | Claim | Seth's assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Naïve along for the ride** | Consciousness is computation and will emerge as AI gets smarter, perhaps at AGI | "mostly grounded in psychological biases and in underexamined assumptions ... so is implausible" |
| 2 | **Theory-based computational** | Computation suffices, but consciousness must be deliberately designed in per a computational theory — global workspace, higher-order thought, attention schema | Still requires computational functionalism *and* silicon substrate flexibility; Butlin et al. (2023) is named as the best overview |
| 3 | **Substrate-dependent computational** | Consciousness is computational, but only certain substrates can run the relevant computations (e.g. mortal computation) | Contains "computational biological naturalism" — only living systems can implement the relevant computations, a phrase Seth credits to Chalmers, and relates to Wiese's "weak computational functionalism". Challenge: it gives up the multiple-realizability motivation that made computationalism attractive, and "Nor are there theories of consciousness which, to my knowledge, leverage this scenario" |
| 4 | **Substrate-dependent, weak** | Consciousness depends on substrate-dependent *non-computational* functional properties — continuous processes, field effects, stochastic effects, fine timing. Still a clear separation of substrate and function | A "weak" biological naturalism lives here, compatible with dynamical readings of predictive processing and the free energy principle; electromagnetic-field theories also fall here. Seth finds it "more appealing than the 'computational biological naturalism'" |
| 5 | **Substrate-dependent, strong** | Consciousness depends on intrinsic properties of its material biological basis — either because the relevant functions include the substrate by definition (as digestion requires real food), or, in Searle's original spirit, bypassing functions altogether | **Seth's own position**: "My case for biological naturalism is drawn towards this scenario ... conscious AI would need to be 'living' AI" |

A point of intellectual honesty worth recording: Seth states that his case is "agnostic between the two interpretations" of scenario 5, "because apparently intrinsic properties at one level might always be understandable in terms of patterns of functional organisation at more fine-grained levels. But if supposedly intrinsic metabolic, excitable, and autopoietic properties of living systems can be underwritten by finer-grained functional descriptions ... then so be it. Life would still matter" (PDF p. 25).

**Theories off the continuum (§5.6).** *Integrated Information Theory* is singled out as the one theory explicitly stating sufficient conditions: a conscious experience is identical to the cause-effect structure unfolded from a substrate at a maximum of irreducible integrated information. Its implication is distinctive — "If IIT is right, conventional AI is not on a path to consciousness, but there is also nothing special about life", since a feedforward network has zero consciousness while a physically recurrent one has non-zero consciousness "regardless of what it is made out of". *Quantum* theories are noted as controversial; Seth observes that even if quantum effects matter for biological consciousness (anaesthesia is mentioned), "this could be a contingent property of biological consciousness, rather than a necessary property of consciousness in general", and he flags "quantum computational functionalism" as a further option.

**Embodiment (§5.7).** Its relevance is scenario-dependent, which is the section's point: "If computation is sufficient for consciousness, then virtual bodies and virtual environments — or non-biological robotic bodies — should also be sufficient." Under substrate dependence, embodiment may be necessary to enable the relevant organisation, and "physically embodied robots could have energetic and structural integrity requirements entailing real though non-biological forms of homeostasis and allostasis (but not autopoiesis)" — a concrete, and for an artificial-agent project a directly relevant, intermediate case. He notes a live counter-case for his own view: cerebral organoids typically lack embodiment and embeddedness. On agency, he distinguishes the "agentic AI" sense (acting toward goals) from the biological sense in which "the relevant goals are typically endogenous, rather than being exogenously imposed", suggesting AI agents may be limited to "weak, observer dependent forms of agency".

**Self-assessment (§5.8).** "My case is not watertight and may well be wrong in the details, or wrong altogether." The taxonomy's stated purpose is twofold: to stop us assuming conscious AI is inevitable, and to underline the urgency of understanding biological consciousness — "Even if biological naturalism turns out to be false, in terms of sufficient conditions, a biological perspective may still be needed to understand the nature of consciousness in humans and other animals."

#### The ethical argument (§6)

The organising distinction is between AI that *is* conscious and AI that merely *seems* conscious, "The latter are more likely than the former, and different ethical issues arise in each case."

**Real artificial consciousness (§6.1).** Consciousness plausibly confers moral status; with it comes the potential for suffering; so creating it "risks a mass inauguration of new forms of suffering", compounded because "we might not even recognise this suffering as suffering, if it expresses itself in ways poorly aligned with our anthropocentric biases." Designing out negative valence would be hard to guarantee and might not help, since arguably any consciousness confers moral status. Further: real artificial consciousness "might endow a system with its own interests and values, beyond those installed by the system's designers", complicating value alignment both practically and morally. Conclusion: "The development of real artificial consciousness for its own sake should not be an explicit goal ... it is surprising how often 'conscious AI' is treated as a holy grail." He acknowledges the countervailing consideration honestly — some AI failure modes (rapid generalisation, metacognition, small-data learning) overlap with abilities tied to consciousness, so implementing consciousness-associated *functions* may be genuinely useful — and proposes the line be drawn at implementing them "in ways sufficiently different from the human (or more generally biological) case", while noting that where the line falls depends on one's theory. A footnote records the most restrictive position available, Metzinger's call for a moratorium on research involving synthetic phenomenology.

**Conscious-seeming AI (§6.2).** The claim that changes what follows: impressions of machine consciousness may be *cognitively impenetrable*, like the Müller-Lyer illusion, which "will always look different, even when you know they are the same length ... whatever we know or believe, conscious-seeming AI systems are likely inevitable, for all of us." The resulting **"West World" dilemma**: if we believe a system is not conscious but cannot stop feeling that it is, either we extend moral status to it — "we distort the circle of human moral concern, diverting attention and resources from other things that legitimately deserve them — including other humans and non-human animals" — or we withhold it and "risk brutalising our own minds", a Kantian worry about coarsening moral sensibility. Named risks: exploitation of psychological vulnerabilities, erosion of digital privacy, and "conscious-seeming LLM-powered avatars of dead friends or relatives, trained on their personal data", with "an extraordinary naïveté about the psychological importance of grief and forgetting". Named benefits: easier interaction, accessibility, individualised therapy, possibly loneliness. One mitigation suggested: treating language models as role-playing, to induce psychological distance, "but this strategy might only get us so far."

**The historical-record worry, stated and answered (§7).** "our unfortunate track record of withholding moral status from conscious things ... Will withholding attributions of consciousness to AI leave us once more on the wrong side of history?" His answer: "I believe the situation here is different. Our psychological biases are more likely to lead to false positives than false negatives. Compared to non-human animals, LLMs may be more similar to us in ways that do not matter for consciousness, and less similar in ways that do" (PDF p. 30).

A terminological note for the corpus: the Turing test is "a test of intelligence, not a test of consciousness", and the analogous test for consciousness is called a **Garland test**, after Alex Garland's *Ex Machina* (attributed to Shanahan 2016), with Schneider (2019) cited as one proposal.

**The closing image.** Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein*, usually read as a warning against creating life: "the real Promethean sin of Frankenstein was not endowing his creature with life, but with consciousness. It was the capacity of his creation to endure, to suffer, to feel jealousy and misery and anger that gave rise to the horror and the carnage."

**On formalism.** The free-energy material (§4.2) is the only quantitative content, and the article states its relations in prose with footnoted technical qualifications rather than displaying equations. Per the brief, nothing has been derived or symbolised here beyond what the text states: free energy as an upper bound on surprisal whose long-term average is entropy; free energy as a lower bound on model evidence, so that minimising it maximises the evidence lower bound; prediction errors as associated with free-energy *gradients* under the Gaussian assumption; the Jarzynski equality bounding average non-equilibrium work by a free-energy difference; and the Landauer bound on the energy to erase one bit.

## Cross-paper notes for the synthesis

These are observations that cut across the five entries. They are descriptive, not adjudications.

### 1. Almost nobody in this batch actually rejects computational functionalism

Four of the five entries are recorded as `restricts` rather than `rejects`, and the fifth is a borderline case. Each author explicitly declines the stronger claim available to them:
- Godfrey-Smith: metabolism belongs *inside* the functional profile — an immanent correction to functionalism, not its rejection. "There might one day be functionally similar non-living systems, to some appreciable degree."
- Seth & Tsakiris: the entire account is built from predictive processing and the free energy principle; whether a robot would need to be alive is listed as an *outstanding question*.
- Aru, Larkum & Shine: three explicit disclaimers — not only the mammalian brain, not only living systems, not impossible in software.
- Seth (2025): "The arguments so far do not disprove computational functionalism."
The unanimity of the hedge is itself a historical fact worth the synthesis stating. The biological challenge, in its 2016–2025 form, is a challenge to an *assumption*, framed in terms of plausibility and credence rather than proof. Where the earlier corpus (Searle, batch B) argued from a thought experiment to a conclusion, this batch argues from biological detail to a lowered prior.

### 2. The one paper that sits awkwardly in this batch

**Cleeremans & Tallon-Baudry (2022)** is primarily a paper about whether consciousness has a *function*, and its central anti-epiphenomenalism argument runs *through* multiple realizability (List's realization insensitivity against Kim's causal exclusion) — the founding premise of functionalism. It arrives at a biological-sounding conclusion ("a close association between consciousness and life") almost as an aside, via the observation that reinforcement-learning rewards have "zero value 'for' the system". A synthesis that files it simply as an anti-functionalist paper will misread it. It is better read as a bridge between the biological batch and the theories-of-consciousness batch.

### 3. A citation chain runs through the batch, and should be drawn as one

- Godfrey-Smith (2016) → cited by Seth (2025) §3.3 as the source of the "differences on the inside" objection to neural replacement, alongside Bechtel & Mundale (1999) and Cao (2022). Also cited by Seth & Tsakiris (2018) — but for the *earlier* book *Complexity and the Function of Mind in Nature* (1996), not for the metabolism paper.
- Seth & Tsakiris (2018) → cited by Cleeremans & Tallon-Baudry (2022) for the visceral grounding of the experiencing subject, and by Seth (2025) §4.5 for the "beast machines" formulation.
- Aru et al. (2023) → cited by Seth (2025) §4.1 and §5.7 as the source of the "skin in the game" framing.
- Thompson's *Mind in Life* (2007, not held) is cited by **all** of Godfrey-Smith (2016, "has influenced this paper"), Seth & Tsakiris (2018) and Seth (2025). It is the batch's missing hub.
The batch therefore forms a genuine lineage, not five parallel papers, and Seth (2025) is its explicit convergence point.

### 4. Three distinct biological claims are in play, and they are not equivalent

The synthesis should not merge them.
- **Metabolism / self-maintenance** (Godfrey-Smith; Aru et al.'s third argument; Seth's autopoiesis). Claim: what a living system does cannot be separated from its continual regeneration of itself.
- **Allostatic regulation / interoception** (Seth & Tsakiris; Seth 2025 §4.1; touched by Cleeremans & Tallon-Baudry's "experiencing subject"). Claim: the *reason* there is perception at all is control of the body's viability, and this shapes what experience is like.
- **Specific neural architecture** (Aru et al.'s second argument: thalamocortical loops, ascending arousal, dual-compartment layer 5 pyramidal neurons). Claim: particular circuit features, not life as such, are what consciousness needs.
The third is compatible with substrate flexibility in a way the first two are not — a machine with the right circuits would qualify. Aru et al. hold the second and third together; Seth (2025) subordinates architecture to the first.

### 5. Two papers disagree, implicitly, about what Integrated Information Theory implies

Both characterise IIT accurately but use it differently. Aru et al. cite it as holding "it is impossible for a software-based AI system to achieve consciousness because computer software does not have real cause-effect power". Seth (2025) §5.6 draws the complementary consequence: "If IIT is right, conventional AI is not on a path to consciousness, but there is also nothing special about life" — a physically recurrent network is conscious "regardless of what it is made out of". Both readings are correct; they emphasise opposite halves. Godfrey-Smith (2016), writing earlier, characterises the Tononi–Koch position as near-panpsychist, on which "even a simple non-living switching device" has a sliver of consciousness. A synthesis drawing on batch D (theories of consciousness) should check all three against the IIT sources directly.

### 6. The most reusable distinctions produced by this batch

For a diagram-heavy synthesis page, these are the concepts that carry their own explanatory weight:
- **Substrate independence versus substrate flexibility** (Seth 2025): unrestricted versus "some other, but not necessarily all" materials.
- **Simulation versus implementation versus realisation** (Seth 2025 §3.7): "Nothing gets wet in a weather forecasting computer."
- **Mortal versus immortal computation** (Hinton, via Seth 2025 §3.4): an argument against substrate flexibility *from inside* computationalism.
- **Causal and informational closure** (Barnett & Seth 2023; Rosas et al. 2024, via Seth 2025 §3.5): the one proposal in the batch that could *measure* how far a system's higher-level dynamics can be abstracted from its substrate.
- **Epistemic versus instrumental inference** (Seth & Tsakiris 2018; Seth 2025 §4.1): discovery versus control, used to explain why the felt self is not object-like.
- **Autopoietic versus allopoietic** (Maturana & Varela, via Seth 2025 §4.1): self-producing versus other-producing systems.
- **Being a model versus having a model** (Seth & Tsakiris 2018, Box 4): behaving as if model-based versus explicitly encoding a generative model.
- **Umwelt and affordances** (Aru et al. 2023 §4): the perceptually available slice of the world, and the action-relevant information in it.
- **"Skin in the game"** (Aru et al. 2023, Box 1): a system with no stake in its own continuation.
- **Biopsychism versus biological naturalism** (Godfrey-Smith 2016; Seth 2025 §4.0): all living systems are conscious, versus only (but not necessarily all) living systems are.
- **Epistemic versus ontological biological naturalism** (Seth 2025 §4.5): consciousness can only be *understood* in light of life, versus consciousness *depends on* living substrate.

### 7. A terminological fault line the corpus inherits

Godfrey-Smith (2016) explicitly prefers the older three-way split — qualia, consciousness, intentionality — to the now-standard equation of qualia with phenomenal consciousness, attributing the merge to "writers who happen now to be at NYU (Nagel, Block, Chalmers)". On his usage, one can ask whether squid feel pain without asking whether squid are conscious. Every other paper in this batch uses the merged vocabulary. This means Godfrey-Smith's question is *broader* than the others', and comparisons of scope between him and, say, Aru et al. (who restrict themselves to phenomenal consciousness) must be made with that in mind.

### 9. What this batch does *not* contain

A reader of the synthesis should know the gaps.
- **No empirical test of the central claim.** None of the five papers reports data bearing directly on whether consciousness requires life. The empirical content is either supporting (anaesthesia and dendritic physiology in Aru et al.; micro-valence in Cleeremans & Tallon-Baudry) or proposed-but-not-run (closure measures in Seth 2025).
- **No engagement with the multiple-realizability literature on its own terms.** Bechtel & Mundale (1999) and Polger & Shapiro (2016) are cited in passing by Seth (2025) but not argued with; that debate belongs to batch A.
- **No treatment of what distinguishes conscious from non-conscious living systems.** Seth (2025) raises the question explicitly and defers it: "These, too, are questions for another time." Godfrey-Smith's animal-transitions section is the nearest thing to an answer in this batch, and it is the section that did *not* go into the *Journal of Philosophy* paper.
