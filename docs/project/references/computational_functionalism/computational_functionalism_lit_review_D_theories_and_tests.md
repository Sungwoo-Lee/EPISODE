# Computational Functionalism — Part D: Theories of Consciousness as Tests for Machines

**Topic folder:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/`
**Part of:** a multi-file, neutral review of how the idea of *computational functionalism* developed — roughly, the thesis that mental states are constituted by the computations a system performs, so that running the right computation in any substrate would suffice for having a mind.
**This part:** 7 papers (2015–2023), reviewed one at a time in the order listed in the batch assignment, 2026-09-21.
**Reviewer:** literature-reviewer
**Stance:** this file describes positions; it does not adjudicate between them.

## What this part is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question.** Suppose you want to know whether a machine is conscious. You cannot ask it and trust the answer — a system can be built to say "yes". So the obvious move is: find out what makes *humans* conscious, write that down as a theory, and then check whether the machine satisfies the theory. This part follows the seven papers that tried exactly that, and the papers that argued the move cannot work.

**Why it matters for the history of computational functionalism.** If consciousness were purely a matter of which *computation* is running, then the substrate — neurons, silicon, gears — would be irrelevant, and a functionally identical machine would be conscious by construction. Every paper here either supports that conclusion, restricts it, or denies it, and each does so by appealing to a theory of consciousness rather than to a philosophical thought experiment. The theories disagree sharply, and one of the most-cited results in this literature is an argument that *the disagreement cannot be settled by experiment* as long as the experiments use behavioural reports.

**The seven papers, in one line each.**
1. **Tononi & Koch (2015)** present Integrated Information Theory (IIT): consciousness is a system's *intrinsic cause–effect power* — its capacity to make a difference to itself, over and above its parts, quantified as a number they call Φ ("phi"). Because a standard digital computer's circuitry has that power only in tiny, disconnected pockets, they conclude a computer simulating a brain in perfect detail "would experience next to nothing" (p. 1).
2. **Dehaene, Lau & Kouider (2017)** split "consciousness" into two *information-processing* abilities — global availability of information to the whole system (they call it C1) and a system's monitoring of its own states (C2) — and argue current machines have neither, but that both are computable and could be built.
3. **Graziano (2017)** argues the brain builds an internal *model* of its own attention, and that saying "I am conscious" is that model reporting itself. On this view a machine that builds the same model would be conscious in the only sense there is.
4. **Doerig, Schurger, Hess & Herzog (2019)** give the *unfolding argument*: any theory that (like IIT) says two systems with identical input–output behaviour can differ in consciousness is, by a mathematical fact about recurrent and feedforward networks, either untestable by the standard experimental methods or already falsified.
5. **Kleiner & Hoel (2021)** formalise the same worry: measuring consciousness always requires an inference from a report, so a theory of consciousness and a theory of *how reports are generated* can only be tested as a pair — a "falsification problem".
6. **Butlin, Long et al. (2023)** propose a practical method: read several leading theories, extract from each a list of *indicator properties* a system would need, and score AI systems against the list. They find no current system is a strong candidate, and no obvious technical barrier either.
7. **Albantakis et al. (2023)** give the current full mathematical statement of IIT (version 4.0), including the formal definition of Φ that the earlier papers used informally.

**The single fault line.** Papers 1, 4, 5 and 7 concern whether a theory that looks *inside* a system (at its causal wiring) can be tested at all; papers 2, 3 and 6 assume you can proceed by looking at what *functions* a system performs. Whether that is a deep disagreement or a difference in what question is being asked is, in these seven papers, unresolved and explicitly discussed.

**Version caveats.** Five of the seven PDFs are the published journal versions. Two are preprints: Butlin et al. is arXiv:2308.08708v3 (a standalone report, never a journal article), and Kleiner & Hoel is arXiv:2004.03541v3, the preprint of the paper later published in *Neuroscience of Consciousness*. Details are given at the top of each entry.

## 1. Tononi & Koch (2015) — Consciousness: here, there and everywhere? [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Tononi and Koch 2015 - Consciousness here there and everywhere.pdf` — the published open-access (CC-BY) *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* review, 370: 20140167, article pages 1–16 plus references. Watermarked as downloaded through Sung Kyun Kwan University access. **PDF version: published.**

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** The paper begins from a practical problem, not a philosophical one. The science of consciousness works by finding *correlates* — behaviours and brain signals that go along with being conscious. Tononi and Koch argue that correlates run out exactly where we most need an answer. They cannot tell us why the cerebral cortex supports consciousness while the cerebellum does not, "though it has even more neurons and appears to be just as complicated" (p. 1) — indeed the cerebellum holds 69 of the brain's 86 billion neurons (p. 5) yet its loss does not diminish consciousness. And correlates cannot be extended to the cases we care about: a patient with a few surviving islands of cortex, a preterm infant, an octopus, or a machine (figure 2, p. 4).

Their proposed remedy is to invert the usual direction of explanation. Rather than start with the brain and ask how it could produce experience — which they suggest is why the "Hard problem" looks intractable (p. 5) — start with experience itself, write down its essential properties, and then ask what a physical system must be like to have those properties. That is Integrated Information Theory (IIT).

**Key findings and the main apparatus.**
- **Five axioms** about experience, taken as self-evident: it *exists intrinsically*; it is *structured* (composition); it is *specific* (information); it is *unified* (integration); it is *definite* (exclusion) (pp. 5–6).
- **Five matching postulates** about physical systems: a system must have cause–effect power *upon itself*; its subsets must also have such power; it must specify a particular cause–effect structure; that structure must be irreducible to independent parts; and it must be specified over one definite set of elements at one definite spatio-temporal grain (pp. 6–9).
- **A central identity:** "an experience is identical to a conceptual structure that is maximally irreducible intrinsically" (p. 9). The *quantity* of consciousness is the number Φ ("big phi", the structure's intrinsic irreducibility); the *quality* is the structure's shape in a high-dimensional "cause–effect space".
- **Empirical traction.** The theory's prediction that losing consciousness means losing integration has been tested with transcranial magnetic stimulation plus high-density EEG: a conscious cortex answers a magnetic pulse with a widespread, non-repetitive pattern; an unconscious one answers locally or stereotypically. The resulting *perturbational complexity index* (PCI) drops in deep sleep, in anaesthesia with several different drugs, and in vegetative patients (pp. 9, "Predictions").
- **The conclusion about machines.** Two results are derived, both against functionalism. First, a purely feedforward network has Φ = 0 and so is "a zombie — carrying out tasks unconsciously" (p. 13), yet any recurrent network can be re-expressed as a feedforward one with the same input–output behaviour over a bounded number of time steps; so a perfect behavioural duplicate of a human could be entirely unconscious. Second, a digital simulation of a brain is "virtual", and "just like a computer simulation of a giant star will not bend space–time around the machine, a simulation of our conscious brain will not have consciousness" (p. 15).

**Initial takeaway.** This is the paper in which the leading physically-grounded theory of consciousness states, in one sentence, that it is incompatible with computational functionalism: "in sharp contrast to widespread functionalist beliefs, IIT implies that digital computers, even if their behaviour were to be functionally equivalent to ours, and even if they were to run faithful simulations of the human brain, would experience next to nothing" (p. 1). It also supplies the positive alternative that later critics attack: what matters is not what a system computes but what *causal power its physical elements have over each other*.

### Phase 2: Argument reconstruction and technical detail

#### 2.1 The two anti-functionalist arguments, as premises

**Argument A — the feedforward zombie (pp. 13, 15).**

1. *Integration postulate.* A physical substrate of experience must specify a cause–effect structure that is irreducible across its minimum unidirectional partition; the degree of irreducibility is Φ, "a non-negative number" (p. 8).
2. *Feedforward systems have Φ = 0.* In a strictly layered network "the input layer is always determined entirely by external inputs and the output layer does not affect the rest of the system, hence neither layer can be part of a complex, and the same is true recursively for the next layers downstream and upstream" (p. 13). The reasoning is an induction over layers: peel off the input and output layer, and the argument reapplies to the remainder.
3. *Behavioural equivalence is achievable feedforward.* "Any neural network with feedback circuits can be mapped onto a purely feed-forward network in such a manner that the latter approximates its input–output relationships (for computations bounded by a maximal time step)" (p. 13, citing ref. [120]). Figure 5b gives a worked pair: a recurrent system with Φmax = 0.76 and 17 concepts, and a functionally equivalent feedforward system with Φmax = 0 and no concepts.
4. *Therefore:* a system behaviourally indistinguishable from a human, able to pass the Turing test, can have Φ = 0 — a "'perfect' zombie" (p. 13).
5. *Therefore:* input–output behaviour is not sufficient for consciousness; "it becomes essential to consider the internal circuitry — not just what the machine does, but how it does it" (pp. 13–15), and "there cannot be an ultimate Turing test for consciousness" (p. 15).

Note the scope restriction the authors themselves state twice: the unfolding into a feedforward network preserves behaviour only *for computations bounded by a maximal time step*, and the feedforward equivalent "will have many more nodes and connections" (p. 13). Both restrictions become load-bearing in Doerig et al. (2019), entry 4 below.

**Argument B — simulation is not duplication (p. 15).**

1. *IIT's identity claim.* Consciousness is a fundamental property of a physical system consisting in "real cause–effect power, specifically the power of shaping the space of possible past and future states in a way that is maximally irreducible intrinsically" (p. 15).
2. *Analogy premise.* Mass is likewise an intrinsic property with real causal power — bending space–time.
3. *Simulation premise.* "The brain is real, but a simulation of a brain is virtual." A simulation of a star does not bend space–time around the machine.
4. *Therefore:* a simulation of a conscious brain is not conscious.
5. *What is real is the hardware.* "The physical computer that is running the simulation is just as real as the brain", so the correct analysis identifies its actual elements — transistors — and computes Φ over them. The authors' expectation is that the machine "would likely not form a large complex of high Φmax, but break down into many mini-complexes of low Φmax", because digital circuitry has small fan-in and fan-out and its maximum cause–effect power sits at the clock's fast timescale (p. 15 and endnote 15).
6. The authors note the same conclusion was reached by Searle's Chinese Room and, 300 years earlier, by Leibniz's mill (endnote 14).

**The restriction that is easy to miss.** Argument B is *not* carbon chauvinism. Endnote 15 states: "there is no reason why hardware-level, neuromorphic models of the human brain that do not rely on software running on a digital computer, could not approximate, one day, our level of consciousness". IIT as presented here denies *computational* sufficiency while allowing a form of substrate independence — any physics with the right intrinsic cause–effect structure will do. The same endnote gives the Internet case: if each networked computer is black-boxed as a macro-element with real cause–effect power, "in principle, this kind of organization could be arranged so that it gives rise to a complex of high Φ, although this is certainly not the way the Internet works right now."

#### 2.2 The formal apparatus, as the paper states it

The paper is a review and gives definitions rather than derivations. Reproducing its own glossary (table 1, p. 6) and text:

- **Element.** A minimal component "having at least two states, inputs that can affect those states and outputs that depend on them" — a neuron, a logic gate.
- **Mechanism.** Any subset of elements, including the whole system, that has cause–effect power within the system.
- **Cause–effect repertoire.** "The probability distribution of potential past and future states of a system as informed by a mechanism in its current state." It is determined operationally "by perturbing the system in all possible ways" (p. 8) — note this is an interventionist, not an observational, quantity.
- **Integrated information φ ("small phi").** "Information that is specified by a mechanism above and beyond the information specified by its (minimal) parts"; it "measures the integration or irreducibility of the cause–effect repertoire specified by a mechanism."
- **MIP (minimum information partition).** "The partition that makes the least difference."
- **Concept.** A mechanism together with its maximally irreducible cause–effect repertoire, carrying the value φmax.
- **Conceptual structure / quale.** The set of all concepts with their φmax values, plotted as a constellation in cause–effect space (axes: every possible past and future state of the complex).
- **Integrated conceptual information Φ ("big phi").** "Conceptual information that is specified by a system above and beyond the conceptual information specified by its (minimal) parts … a non-negative number." Partitions are taken *unidirectionally*, "to ensure that cause–effect power is intrinsically irreducible … which implies that every part of the system must be able to both affect and be affected by the rest of the system" (p. 8).
- **Complex.** A set of elements specifying a *local maximum* of Φ. "Only a complex exists as an entity from its own intrinsic perspective."

The worked example (figure 4, p. 12) is three binary gates — an OR, an AND and an XOR — wired together, in state ABC = 100. The analysis yields a complex with Φmax = 1.92, whose largest concept has φmax = 0.5. Figure 5 reports Φmax = 10.56 for a well-connected simulated cortex, 3.22 after noise is raised to 0.3, and a breakdown at noise 0.48 into five small complexes of Φmax = 0.19 each, where the whole interacting system has only Φ = 0.03 — the formal expression of "aggregates are not conscious" (p. 13).

Two structural consequences the authors emphasise, both of which follow from **exclusion** rather than from integration:

- **Only maxima exist.** Two people talking form an integrated system with Φ greater than zero, but less than each brain's own Φmax; so there are two experiences and no group mind. "There is nothing-it-is-like-to-be two people, let alone the 300 plus million citizens making up the USA" (p. 13). This is IIT's answer to panpsychism's *combination problem* (James's twelve men with one word each, quoted p. 13).
- **A definite grain.** The substrate is specified over one spatio-temporal grain — the one at which Φ is maximal — so "a mechanism cannot have effects at a fine temporal grain, and additional effects at a coarser grain" (p. 9). Endnote 11 draws out that the stream of consciousness would then be "a discrete succession of 'snapshots'".

#### 2.3 What the theory is claimed to explain and predict

| Kind | Content | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Prediction (tested) | Loss and recovery of consciousness tracks breakdown and recovery of integration; PCI from TMS + high-density EEG falls in deep sleep, in anaesthesia under several agents, and in vegetative patients, and is high in every conscious subject tested | p. 9 |
| Prediction (counterintuitive) | Cortex may generate experience even when most pyramidal neurons are nearly silent, because inactive elements still specify a cause–effect repertoire ("the dog that did not bark", p. 10). Contrasts with the broadcast/ignition picture of global workspace | pp. 9–10 |
| Prediction (counterintuitive) | Progressively weakening the 200 million callosal fibres would produce an all-or-none split of one experience into two, at the point where whole-brain Φmax falls below each hemisphere's | p. 10 |
| Prediction (structural) | Whatever the neural correlate turns out to be, it must be a *local maximum* of Φ; there may be several in one brain; it has a definite spatial scale and timescale (∼100 ms rather than 1 ms or 10 s) | p. 10 |
| Explanation | Cortex supports consciousness (specialised elements interacting rapidly → high Φmax) while cerebellum does not (small, largely independent modules) | pp. 10, 13 |
| Explanation | Sensory and motor pathways and cortico-subcortical loops influence experience without being part of it, because they are not part of a local maximum | p. 13 |
| Extrapolation | Consciousness is graded and widespread; a photodiode-like circuit of a sensor and a memory element has "a modicum of experience" | pp. 11, 13 |
| Extrapolation | Feedforward networks, including deep-learning systems, have Φ = 0 | p. 13 |
| Extrapolation | Digital simulations of brains "experience next to nothing" | pp. 1, 15 |

The authors defend the extrapolations methodologically rather than empirically: "a theory is first tested and validated in situations that are close to ideal, and then extrapolated to more remote cases" (p. 15), with black holes as the precedent. They also concede the status of the axioms: "Whether the current set of five axioms are truly valid, complete and independent remains open" (p. 5), and "If it is not outright wrong, IIT most likely will have to be refined, expanded and adjusted" (endnote 4, referring to the then-current IIT 3.0).

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2015 · consciousness-science
- Question it asked: What kind of physical system can be conscious, and how can we decide for cases — patients, infants, animals, machines — where behavioural and neural correlates give out?
- Position in one line: Consciousness is identical to a maximally irreducible conceptual structure specified by a system's intrinsic cause–effect power, quantified as Φ; consciousness is therefore graded and widespread among biological systems but absent from feedforward networks, aggregates, and digital simulations of brains.
- Stance on computational functionalism: rejects — the paper states explicitly that behavioural or functional equivalence is insufficient, since a feedforward network with Φ = 0 can reproduce any bounded-time behaviour, and a simulation of a brain is "virtual" rather than causally real; it nonetheless allows neuromorphic hardware to be conscious (endnote 15), so its rejection targets computational sufficiency rather than non-biological substrates.
- Responds to / builds on: earlier IIT formulations (Tononi 2004, 2008; Oizumi, Albantakis & Tononi 2014); Dehaene & Changeux 2011 (global broadcast/ignition, contrasted); Chalmers on the Hard problem; Searle's Chinese Room and "veneer of jam"; Leibniz's mill; William James's combination problem; Strawson's panpsychism; Cohen & Dennett's "perfect experiment" (endnote 12); Massimini and colleagues on PCI; animat work by Albantakis, Edlund and colleagues.
- Key move: The identification of consciousness with Φ, plus two consequences it is remembered for — a behaviourally perfect feedforward "zombie", and the claim that a faithful digital simulation of a brain would experience next to nothing ("a computer simulation of a giant star will not bend space–time").
- Evidence type: review (theory exposition with cited empirical support, no new data)
- Testability: Yes, in three layers. Directly tested: the perturbational complexity index falls with loss of consciousness across sleep, anaesthetic agents and disorders of consciousness. Proposed and testable in principle: an all-or-none split of experience as callosal efficacy is reduced; the NCC being a local maximum of Φ at a definite spatial and temporal grain; consciousness persisting with near-silent (but not inactivated) pyramidal neurons; measurable multiple complexes in a single brain. Acknowledged as untestable at present: the extrapolations to photodiodes and simulated brains, which the authors defend by the "validate near, extrapolate far" methodology rather than by direct evidence.

## 2. Dehaene, Lau & Kouider (2017) — What is consciousness, and could machines have it? [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Dehaene et al. 2017 - What is consciousness and could machines have it.pdf` — the published *Science* review (358(6362), 486–492, 27 October 2017) in journal typesetting, deposited in UCLA's eScholarship repository (a cover page precedes the article; the article itself is paginated "1 of 7" … "7 of 7"). ### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** The paper opens with a domestic scene: you are driving and notice the fuel-tank light. "What makes you, a complex assembly of neurons, aware of the light? And what makes the car, a sophisticated piece of electronics and engineering, unaware of it?" (p. 486). The authors' strategy is to answer the machine question by first getting clear about "how consciousness arises in the only physical system that undoubtedly possesses it: the human brain" (p. 486).

Their central move is a distinction. The single word "consciousness", they argue, "conflates two different types of information-processing computations":
- **C1 — global availability.** Information is selected out of the flood, held, and broadcast so that the whole system can use it: recall it, act on it, speak about it. This is the transitive sense, as in "the driver is conscious of the light" (p. 486).
- **C2 — self-monitoring.** The system represents *itself*: whether it knows something, how confident it is, whether it just made an error. This is introspection, or what psychologists call metacognition (pp. 486–487).
- **C0 — unconscious processing.** Everything else. They stress this is where most of human intelligence lives, and that it is precisely what current AI reproduces.

They set aside the medical, intransitive sense ("the patient was no longer conscious", i.e. wakefulness) as having minimal computational content: "obviously, a machine must be properly turned on for its computations to unfold normally" (p. 486).

**Key findings (as reviewed).**
- **C0 goes deep.** Subliminal stimuli — words, digits, faces — are recognised invariantly across font, size and viewpoint, prime across vision and audition, elicit the N400 semantic-violation response, influence prefrontal task selection and motor inhibition, drive evidence accumulation, and support reinforcement learning when cue, reward or motivation is below threshold (pp. 487–488). "Bayesian inference and evidence accumulation, which are cornerstone computations for AI, are basic unconscious mechanisms for humans" (p. 487).
- **C1 solves a real engineering problem.** A modular brain cannot act on many parallel probabilistic interpretations at once; it must commit. The *global neuronal workspace* is the proposed answer: on top of specialised modules, a limited-capacity workspace selects one representation, holds it over time, and shares it (p. 488).
- **C1 has measurable signatures.** All-or-none perception in rivalry; the attentional blink and psychological refractory period showing a serial bottleneck; all-or-none "ignition" of a distributed frontoparietal network only on conscious trials; all-or-none firing of concept-selective neurons in prefrontal and temporal cortex; greater stability of conscious representations, where unconscious traces decay in roughly a second (pp. 489–490, table 1).
- **C2 also has measurable signatures.** Confidence as "a sense of the probability that a decision or computation is correct"; opt-out and persistence measures usable in animals and pre-verbal infants; the error-related negativity (ERN) and positivity upon error (Pe) arising before any feedback; meta-memory; reality monitoring, whose failure is linked to hallucination in schizophrenia. Prefrontal inactivation can impair metacognitive judgement "while sparing performance on the first-order task" (p. 490).
- **C1 and C2 doubly dissociate.** C2 without C1: typists slow after an error they never consciously notice, and an ERN can occur for undetected errors. C1 without C2: a memory pops into consciousness without accurate confidence, yielding false memories (pp. 490–491).
- **Machines currently do C0.** "The computations implemented by current deep-learning networks correspond mostly to nonconscious operations in the human brain" (p. 486). The car's low-fuel light is the paradigm: "when the light flashes, all other processors in the machine remain uninformed and unchanged" (p. 491).

**Initial takeaway.** This is the clearest statement in the corpus of the *functionalist* answer to the machine question. Consciousness is not a substance or an intrinsic property, but a pair of computations; a system that performs them is conscious in the only sense the authors think scientifically tractable. They close by naming the objection rather than answering it — "Are we leaving aside the experiential component ('what it is like' to be conscious)?" (p. 492) — and offering the empirical observation that in humans, losing C1 and C2 goes together with losing subjective experience.

### Phase 2: Argument reconstruction and technical detail

#### 2.1 The argument, as numbered premises

1. **Deflationary premise.** "Consciousness" conflates at least three things: wakefulness (set aside), global availability (C1), and self-monitoring (C2). Analysis should proceed on C1 and C2 separately (p. 486).
2. **Orthogonality premise.** C1 and C2 are "orthogonal dimensions of conscious computations" — overlapping in substrate (both depend on prefrontal cortex) but empirically dissociable in both directions (pp. 486–487, 490–491).
3. **Computational-role premise.** Each is characterised by what it *does*, not by what it is made of. C1 is the solution to an information-pooling problem "that, we contend, must be faced by any animal or autonomous AI system" (p. 488). C2 is a second-order system that estimates the reliability of first-order processing.
4. **Empirical premise.** In humans, C1 and C2 have identifiable neural implementations (ignition of a distributed workspace; prefrontal confidence circuits, ERN/Pe), and their presence or absence tracks what subjects report experiencing.
5. **Gap premise.** Current machines implement C0 only, with a few exceptions — Bayesian networks and probabilistic programs that carry uncertainty; a second network trained to predict the first's performance (Cleeremans's "self-redescription"); PathNet, which tracks which internal configurations succeed; curiosity-driven robots that monitor their own learning progress; adversarial training as a route to reality monitoring (pp. 491–492).
6. **No-barrier premise.** Nothing in the analysis makes C1 or C2 uncomputable; blackboard architectures of the 1960s already had the flavour of a global workspace (p. 491).
7. **Conclusion.** "We contend that a machine endowed with C1 and C2 would behave as though it were conscious; for instance, it would know that it is seeing something, would express confidence in it, would report it to others, could suffer hallucinations when its monitoring mechanisms break down, and may even experience the same perceptual illusions as humans" (p. 492).
8. **Anticipated reply, stated but not resolved.** "Still, such a purely functional definition of consciousness may leave some readers unsatisfied. Are we 'over-intellectualizing' consciousness …? Are we leaving aside the experiential component …? Does subjective experience escape a computational definition?" (p. 492). The authors declare these questions beyond scope and offer instead a covariation observation: in blindsight, a patient with primary-visual-cortex damage can localise stimuli in the blind field but "cannot report them (C1), nor can they effectively assess their likelihood of success (C2) — they believe that they are merely 'guessing'" (p. 492). "In this example, at least, subjective experience appears to cohere with possession of C1 and C2."
9. **Closing thesis.** "The empirical evidence is compatible with the possibility that consciousness arises from nothing more than specific computations" (p. 492).

#### 2.2 The explicit contrast with integrated information

The concluding remarks contain the paper's one direct engagement with IIT, without naming it in the body text: "Our stance … differs from other theories in being resolutely computational; we surmise that mere information-theoretic quantities do not suffice to define consciousness unless one also considers *the nature and depth of the information being processed*" (p. 492, emphasis added; reference 76 is Tononi, Boly, Massimini & Koch 2016). The disagreement is therefore about what kind of quantity a theory of consciousness should traffic in: a substrate-level scalar computed from causal structure (Tononi & Koch, entry 1), or a specification of which *computations* are being performed and on what content.

Note also the direct clash of verdicts on deep networks. Tononi and Koch say feedforward networks have Φ = 0 and are therefore zombies. Dehaene, Lau and Kouider agree that current feedforward networks are not conscious — but for an entirely different reason: they perform the wrong *computations* ("a juxtaposition of specialized processors or 'modules' that operate nonconsciously and, we argue, correspond tightly to the operation of current feedforward deep-learning networks", p. 487). On the first view no amount of added computation helps unless the architecture is recurrent in the right causal sense; on the second, adding a workspace and a monitor would suffice.

## 3. Graziano (2017) — The Attention Schema Theory: A Foundation for Engineering Artificial Consciousness [tier: short]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Graziano 2017 - The attention schema theory.pdf` — the published open-access (CC-BY) article, *Frontiers in Robotics and AI* 4:60, 9 pages, in the journal's "Hypothesis and Theory" category, part of a special issue on consciousness in humanoid robots. **PDF version: published.**

**Claim.** The attention schema theory (AST) is deliberately narrower than its rivals. Its stated purpose is "to explain how an information-processing device, the brain, arrives at the claim that it possesses a non-physical, subjective awareness and assigns a high degree of certainty to that extraordinary claim" (p. 1). It does not try to explain how a brain could *have* a non-physical essence: "It is not a theory that deals in the non-physical. It is about the computations that cause a machine to make a claim" (p. 1). Graziano is explicit that this is a reframing rather than an answer to the usual question: "the theory emphatically does not explain how we have a subjective experience. It explains how a machine claims to have a subjective experience, and how it is that the machine cannot tell the difference" (p. 5).

**Method or type.** A position and theory paper, written to introduce AST (developed from 2010 onward in psychology and neuroscience venues) to an engineering audience. It argues by analogy and by appeal to existing experiments rather than presenting new data.

**Key result or argument.** The analogy is the *body schema* — the brain's constantly updated internal model of limb shape, hinging and dynamics. A robot can answer "what is your physical relationship to the apple?" because it has a model of the apple and a model of its body. But ask "what is your *mental* relationship to the apple?" and, with those two models alone, it has nothing to consult; "it would be like asking a digital camera whether it is aware of the picture it just took" (p. 3).

AST's proposal is a third model: the brain builds a schema of its own *attention* — of the selective enhancement of some signals over others, in Desimone and Duncan's biased-competition sense. Like the body schema, this model is a useful simplification rather than an accurate physical description: it carries no information about neurons, synapses or competition dynamics. So when the machine is pressed on what its mental possession of the apple physically is, the only answer its model supports is that it "has no describable physical properties. It just is. It's a non-physical part of me" (p. 4). Hence the paper's compressed formula: "Consciousness is, in a sense, a cartoon sketch of attention" (p. 5). Crucially, this is not a canned utterance: "What is crucial here is the presence of a rich, descriptive model that is constructed beneath the level of cognition and language, and yet still is accessible to cognition" (p. 4), which is why the machine's answers can be flexible and self-consistent under questioning.

Two functions are claimed for the schema, each with its own evidence line. First, **control**: control engineering's internal-model principle (Conant & Ashby 1970) says a good controller contains a model of what it controls; so attention should be better controlled when the schema is intact. The prediction is that when people are unaware of a stimulus they can still attend to it, but attention becomes less stable, less able to error-correct and less able to adapt — results Graziano reads as confirmatory (McCormick 1997; Lin & Murray 2015; Webb et al. 2016a), while saying "more experiments are needed" (p. 6). One simulation study is cited in which modelled attention is better controlled with an internal model than without (van den Boogaard, Treur & Turpijn 2017). Second, **social cognition**: the same machinery, centred on the temporoparietal junction, attributes awareness to others; "arguably, all of social cognition depends on attributing awareness to other people" (p. 6).

The paper's engineering claim follows: "If AST is correct, then consciousness is buildable with current technology" (p. 7). It also contains direct criticism of the two rival theories reviewed above. Global workspace theory "is the same as the AST, if you took away the attention schema part, and had only the attention part … While in my view the theory is likely to be correct as far as it goes, it is incomplete. It does not explain why the globally broadcasted information would be associated with the property of subjective experience" (p. 7). Integrated information theory is, "to many scientists, including myself … non-explanatory and ultimately unfalsifiable", and as a construction recipe "has been disappointing, since even very complex technology that contains a lot of integrated information has not announced its consciousness yet" (p. 7).

**Place in the field's history.** AST sits between illusionism and the mainstream cognitive theories, and Graziano positions it against both. Against illusionism (Dennett, Frankish): "In AST, the brain does not experience an illusion. It does not subjectively experience anything. Instead, the machine has wrong, or simplified information that tells it that it is having an experience"; calling consciousness an illusion "is trying too hard to employ an everyday, intuitive concept that is not truly applicable" (p. 5). Against the "naïve theory" view (Gazzaniga; Nisbett & Wilson): in AST the construct is not socially learned but "wired into the system at a deep level and constructed automatically, like the body schema. It is inborn" (p. 5), plausibly ancient, given that vertebrate attention mechanisms are half a billion years old. This paper is the one usually cited when AST is listed among the theories from which machine-consciousness indicators are drawn (see entry 6).

**Relevance.** It supplies the corpus's clearest statement of the "explain the claim, not the experience" strategy, and a candid consequence: an artificial system with a *different* internal architecture would build a different self-model and so "would not necessarily lay claim to consciousness in the sense that we humans intuitively understand it" (p. 5). On this view what a machine says about its own experience is a function of its self-model's format, not a readout of an underlying fact — a point that cuts against report-based tests generally. The paper also ends with an ethical argument that machine consciousness is urgent rather than optional: without the capacity to attribute awareness to others, "our computers are sociopaths" (p. 7).

- Year · community: 2017 · consciousness-science
- Question it asked: What computations cause a brain — or a machine — to claim, with high confidence, that it has a non-physical subjective awareness?
- Position in one line: The brain builds a schematic, physically inaccurate internal model of its own attention (an "attention schema"), and the claim to be conscious is that model being read out by cognition; consciousness is "a cartoon sketch of attention".
- Stance on computational functionalism: supports — consciousness is identified with a specific information structure and its use, explicitly declared "buildable with current technology", with no role for substrate; the paper simultaneously narrows what is being claimed, since it addresses the claim of experience rather than experience itself.

## 4. Doerig, Schurger, Hess & Herzog (2019) — The unfolding argument: Why IIT and other causal structure theories cannot explain consciousness [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Doerig et al. 2019 - The unfolding argument.pdf` — the final published Elsevier open-access (CC-BY) article, *Consciousness and Cognition* 72 (2019) 49–59, redistributed through Chapman University Digital Commons; two repository cover pages precede the article. Citations use the journal pages. **PDF version: published.**

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** The paper targets a specific family of theories, which it names: *causal structure theories*. These say that whether a system is conscious depends on **how its parts interact** — on the wiring, not on what the system does. Two examples are given, and both are major theories. In Lamme's Recurrent Processing Theory (RPT), recurrent (top-down, re-entrant) processing is necessary and sufficient for consciousness; the initial feedforward sweep is unconscious. In Integrated Information Theory (IIT), consciousness is identified with Φ > 0, where Φ is computed from the causal structure, so that "φ is always greater than zero in recurrent systems (they are always conscious) and always equal to zero in feedforward systems (they are never conscious)" (p. 51).

The authors credit these theories: "IIT and RPT were amongst the first theories of consciousness to make precise predictions about which systems are conscious. As such, they contributed greatly to the advancement of the science of consciousness" (p. 51). Then they argue that the very precision creates an impasse.

**The core observation.** Both recurrent and feedforward neural networks are *universal function approximators* — each can approximate any input–output function to any accuracy with finitely many units (Hornik, Stinchcombe & White 1989; Schäfer & Zimmermann 2006). So for any recurrent network, there exists a feedforward network with the same input–output behaviour. The authors call the transformation **unfolding**. They stress that the facts are not in dispute: "These facts are uncontroversial and widely accepted (including by proponents of IIT: see Oizumi et al., 2014)" (p. 52).

**The consequence.** Every consciousness experiment — masking, binocular rivalry, figure–ground, even experiments that stimulate the brain directly with electrodes or TMS — is an input–output function; direct stimulation "still [involves] input-output functions. The only difference is that part of the input is provided by means of electrodes or TMS rather than through the sensory organs" (p. 51). And measurements of consciousness ultimately rest on subjective reports, because brain activity cannot be used as an a priori indicator of consciousness when explaining consciousness by brain activity is the goal (p. 50).

Put together: for any experimental result that supports a causal structure theory, there is a system with the *opposite* causal structure that produces the identical result. Φ is therefore "neither necessary (wakefulness can be implemented with φ = 0 …) nor sufficient (sleep can be implemented with high φ …)" (fig. 3, p. 52). The theory and the data are **doubly dissociated**.

**The dilemma.** Either a causal structure theory accepts that its unfolded twin is conscious — in which case the theory is false — or it insists the twin is unconscious despite being empirically indistinguishable — in which case "the only reason to believe that only the φ > 0 robot is conscious is to already believe in IIT, but this is circular" (p. 53), and the theory leaves empirical science.

**Initial takeaway.** This is the paper that turned a philosophical worry into a claim about a mathematical fact. The authors are careful to say what they are *not* doing: unlike the philosophical zombie argument, theirs "only targets causal structure theories of consciousness, and not physicalist or functionalist theories in general. In fact, the unfolding argument favours functionalist theories because (un)folding a network changes only its causal structure but not its function or physical nature" (p. 54). The closing sentence is the field-history line: "To remain within the realm of science, consciousness must be described in terms of what it does, and not how it does it" (p. 56).

### Phase 2: Argument reconstruction and technical detail

#### 2.1 The argument as the paper states it (p. 53, verbatim premises)

> **(P1):** In science we rely on physical measurements (based on subjective reports about consciousness).
> **(P2):** For any recurrent system with a given input-output function, there exist feedforward systems with the same input-output function (and vice-versa).
> **(P3):** Two systems that have identical input-output functions cannot be distinguished by any experiment that relies on a physical measurement (other than a measurement of brain activity itself or of other internal workings of the system).
> **(P4):** We cannot use measures of brain activity as a-priori indicators of consciousness, because the brain basis of consciousness is what we are trying to understand in the first place.
> **(C):** Therefore, EITHER causal structure theories are falsified (if they accept that unfolded, feedforward networks can be conscious), OR causal structure theories are outside the realm of scientific inquiry (if they maintain that unfolded feedforward networks are not conscious despite being empirically indistinguishable from functionally equivalent recurrent networks).

Each premise carries weight. P2 is the mathematical premise (universal approximation, extended by the authors to Turing machines, cellular automata and cyclic tag systems). P3 is what makes the dissociation empirical rather than merely notional. P4 is the anti-circularity premise, and is the one that distinguishes this argument from the everyday practice of using a brain measure as a consciousness meter.

#### 2.2 The constructive core: how to keep the function and change Φ at will

The paper's appendices are what make it more than a thought experiment. Appendix A gives an explicit recipe.

Start from a feedforward network implementing $x \mapsto F(x)$, which by IIT's own accounting has $\Phi = 0$. Decompose it at some layer $L_\alpha$ into two successive functions, so that

$$f_\beta \circ f_\alpha = F, \qquad \Phi = 0.$$

Now insert, after $L_\alpha$, a **Φ-increasing device** — a subsystem known to carry arbitrarily high Φ. The authors take these from the Aaronson–Tononi exchange: "Aaronson showed (Aaronson, 2014) and Tononi agreed (Tononi, 2014) that certain kinds of systems can have arbitrarily high φ. … Examples include expander graphs, XOR grids, Vandermonde matrices, large random networks" (p. 57). The device computes some function $g$. Because feedforward networks are universal approximators, there is a feedforward network computing $g^{-1}$, which is placed immediately after it, cancelling the functional effect. The result is

$$f_\beta \circ g^{-1} \circ g \circ f_\alpha = F, \qquad \Phi \text{ arbitrarily high.}$$

The Φ bookkeeping is explicitly borrowed from IIT itself: "the φ value of a composite system consisting of several feedforward networks and one recurrent network … is equal to the φ value of the recurrent network alone. Feedforward networks do not contribute to φ (Oizumi et al., 2014)" (footnote 4, p. 57). So the inserted device sets the whole system's Φ, while the inverse network restores the behaviour. The invertibility requirement is met by at least one of Aaronson's examples, since "Vandermonde matrices are invertible" (p. 57).

Appendix B gives a second, independent construction using **echo state networks / liquid state machines / reservoir computing**: inputs drive a recurrent reservoir, outputs are decoded from its dynamics, and both the input feed and the output decode can be feedforward and so contribute nothing to Φ. Echo state networks are universal approximators given a large enough reservoir with rich enough dynamics (Maass, Natschläger & Markram 2002), and richness rather than randomness is what matters (Rodan & Tiňo 2011). Hence "in principle, it is possible to train an echo state network to perform any function using a φ-increasing device as a reservoir" (p. 57).

Appendix C runs the same trick on the *content* of consciousness rather than its quantity. In IIT the maximal-Φ complex determines the quale. So insert, instead of a Φ-increasing device, a recurrent network whose connectivity and state IIT says corresponds to a particular quale — the authors' example is the quale of smelling ham — and cancel its functional effect with a feedforward inverse. Since the rest of the system is feedforward, the inserted network is the maximal-Φ complex. "Hence the network experiences the smell of ham consciously but reports performing a rivalry experiment (for example it reports seeing the cat image …)" (p. 57). Crucially, the cancelling network cannot abolish the quale, "because, being feedforward, it is not part of the maximal φ complex" (fig. A1 caption, p. 58).

#### 2.3 The examples, scaled from an implant to a whole brain (§2.2)

- **The auditory implant.** Replace the brain's recurrent sound-processing system with a feedforward implant taking the same spike trains in and putting the same spike trains out. "Even though the causal structure in the new implant is completely different, the rest of the brain does not notice any difference" (p. 53). All reports are unchanged: the person still says "I hear the drizzle of the rain". Either causal structure theories are wrong (if auditory consciousness survives) or unscientific (if it does not, undetectably). The authors flag the assumption — this "implies a certain level of functional isolation of the sound processing system, which may or may not be the case" — and note that the argument runs on the whole brain regardless (footnote 2, p. 53).
- **The feedforward brain.** Scaled up: a feedforward system behaving exactly like a human, "passing the Turing test seamlessly", identically describing what it is like to see red, responding identically to masking, rivalry and figure–ground, with the same wakefulness and sleep characteristics (pp. 53–54).
- **The real-world case.** Deep reinforcement learning reached super-human Atari performance with purely feedforward convolutional networks (Mnih et al. 2013), and Hausknecht & Stone (2015) replicated it with recurrent networks. "These systems are empirically identical (to a close approximation). One is conscious but the other is not, according to causal structure theories" (p. 53).

#### 2.4 What the paper concedes, and where the boundary is drawn

The paper is unusually explicit about the scope of its conclusion. Four limits are stated.

1. **Causal structure may still be an excellent *marker*.** §2.4 argues that brains are recurrent for engineering reasons — skull volume and energy budget — since "feedforward networks require many more neurons to implement a function than equivalent recurrent networks" (p. 54). So "causal structures may be good correlates for consciousness in humans not because they are identical with consciousness, but because they correlate well with neural information processing in general, which happens to covary with conscious state in humans as a contingent rule. This explains why causal structure theories may provide human consciousness-meters" (p. 54) — a defence, on functional grounds, of the clinical use of perturbational-complexity measures that entry 1 cites as IIT's confirmed prediction.
2. **The argument is not the zombie argument.** §2.5 gives two differences. Scope: the zombie argument targets all physicalist accounts including functionalist ones; unfolding "only targets causal structure theories … In fact, the unfolding argument favours functionalist theories because (un)folding a network changes only its causal structure but not its function or physical nature" (p. 54). Modality: "one can choose to dismiss the zombie argument by claiming that zombies are in fact not possible … In contrast, the existence of unfolded systems is a straightforward mathematical fact, and not a mere thought experiment" (p. 54). They add that never having observed an unfolded cortex "is not by itself a sufficient argument to call into question the unfolding argument".
3. **Functionalist theories are exempt.** §2.6 walks through global workspace theory, higher-order thought theory and predictive processing, and constructs a feedforward toy model of a global workspace (fig. 4, p. 55): two feedforward sensory networks project to a "workspace" that is itself a feedforward stack copying each layer to the next, and which can be queried at any layer by further feedforward readers — preserving broadcasting with a different causal structure. "What matters is the function, e.g., broadcasting, but not the (neural) implementation" (p. 55). A conditional is attached in footnote 3: "if GWT claims that recurrence per se is essential for consciousness, it becomes a causal structure theory and the argument applies."
4. **The correlation strategy is closed off too.** §2.7 takes Chalmers's proposal of "systematic bridging principles that underlie and explain the covariation between third-person data and first-person data" and argues that it cannot rescue a causal structure theory, "because there are infinitely many equivalent systems that produce exactly the same first person reports as humans, but with completely different causal structures" (p. 56).

#### 2.5 The disagreement about a shared premise

The concluding remarks make the structure of the dispute explicit, and this is the passage the synthesis will need: "Proponents of IIT have previously acknowledged that feedforward and recurrent networks can be functionally equivalent but have different consciousness, according to IIT (Oizumi et al., 2014). In other words, they share the same uncontroversial starting point as we do. However, conclusions differ strongly. Proponents of IIT suggest that this should prompt us to focus on the subjectivity of consciousness. In contrast, we conclude that adopting a causal structure theory precludes *any* experimental approach to consciousness" (p. 56). Entry 1 above is an instance: Tononi and Koch state the same unfolding fact (with a bounded-time-step qualification) and read it as showing that behavioural tests are inadequate, not that their theory is untestable.

One characterisation to carry forward with care. Doerig et al. summarise IIT as holding that "φ is always greater than zero in recurrent systems (they are always conscious)" (p. 51). IIT's own statements are more restrictive — a substrate must be a *maximum* of integrated information, not merely have Φ > 0 (entry 1, §"Aggregates are not conscious"; entry 7). The unfolding construction is built to respect the maximality condition (the inserted device is the maximal-Φ complex because everything else is feedforward), so the construction does not obviously turn on the looser gloss; but the gloss itself is a point where papers in this corpus describe IIT differently.

## 5. Kleiner & Hoel (2021) — Falsification and consciousness [tier: short]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Kleiner and Hoel 2021 - Falsification and consciousness.pdf` — **arXiv:2004.03541v3 [q-bio.NC], 28 April 2021**, 22 pages, carrying the date "April 29, 2021" on the title page. This is the **preprint** of the paper published in *Neuroscience of Consciousness* 2021(1): niab001; it has no journal pagination, so citations below give PDF pages. **PDF version: preprint.**

**Claim.** Testing any theory of consciousness requires two things at once: a *prediction* of what the system is experiencing, made from its internal observables (brain imaging, wiring, state), and an *inference* about what it is experiencing, made from its reports or behaviour. Falsification is a mismatch between them. Kleiner and Hoel prove that the relationship between the data feeding those two operations lands every contemporary theory in one of two bad places.

**Method or type.** A formal proof in an explicitly minimal framework. A class of physical configurations `P` maps, via a measurement correspondence `obs`, to a class of datasets `O`; a theory's prediction map sends datasets to a space of experiences, `pred : O ⇻ E`; the experimenter's inference map does the same from report data, `inf : O → E`. Each dataset splits into prediction data `o_i` ("internal") and inference data `o_r` ("report"). Falsification is then defined at a dataset as

$$\mathrm{inf}(o) \notin \mathrm{pred}(o).$$

The authors stress that they never need to specify `pred` for any particular theory: "It suffices that it exists, in some form or the other" (PDF p. 4). This is what makes the result theory-independent.

**Key result or argument.** A **substitution** is a transformation `S` of physical systems that leaves the report data fixed while making the prediction change incompatibly:

$$\mathrm{pred} \circ \mathrm{obs}(p) \;\cap\; \mathrm{pred} \circ \mathrm{obs}(S(p)) = \emptyset .$$

A **universal substitution** is one available for every possible report. Two theorems follow.

- **Theorem 3.10.** "If inference and prediction data are independent, either every single inference operation is wrong or the theory under consideration is already falsified" (PDF p. 11). Independence means one can vary the internal data while holding the report data fixed — which is exactly what ordinary experimental practice assumes. The proof needs only that the theory is *minimally informative* (it makes at least two different predictions).
- **Theorem 4.3.** "If a theory of consciousness implies that inference and prediction data are strictly dependent, then it is either already falsified or empirically unfalsifiable" (PDF p. 14). Strict dependence means the internal data is a function of the report data, `o_i = f(o_r)` — as when consciousness is identified with what is accessible to report. Then no experiment can distinguish what *does* happen from what *could* happen, so "experiments add no information to whether the theory is true or not, and such theories are empirically uninformative or tautological" (PDF p. 15).

Examples of independence are drawn from artificial neural networks (hidden units are not part of the output, so internal structure can be varied freely), universal computers (Turing machines, cellular automata), and universal intelligences (the AIXI model). For IIT the authors note the point is already conceded internally, citing Albantakis & Tononi (2019) on dynamically equivalent systems with different causal composition and Koch (2019) on a Turing machine with wildly different predicted contents for the same behaviour.

**Place in the field's history.** The paper positions itself explicitly against entry 4: the substitution argument "constitutes both a generalization and correction of the 'unfolding argument' against IIT presented in Doerig et al. (2019)" (PDF p. 2). The generalisation is in scope — the dilemma catches global workspace, higher-order thought and predictive processing as well as IIT, so "instead of putting the blame of this problem on individual theories of consciousness, we show that it is due to issues of falsification in the scientific study of consciousness, particularly the field's contemporary usage of report or behavior to infer conscious experiences" (PDF pp. 2–3). Notably, the theories that *escape* the unfolding argument by being functionalist are precisely the ones exposed to the second horn: global workspace, the attention schema and "fame in the brain" are listed as candidates for strict dependence (PDF p. 14), where `o_i ⊆ o_r`, which the authors connect to Block's distinction — condition (10) "holds true whenever a theory of consciousness is under investigation where access consciousness determines phenomenal consciousness" (PDF p. 14). The paper also records the existing replies to Doerig et al. (Kleiner 2020; Tsuchiya, Andrillon & Haun 2019).

The objections section is where the paper locates what is peculiar to consciousness. Asked why the argument does not wreck every science, the authors answer that in the temperature case one has two independent measurement routes, `P → O ← T`, whereas here the physical system determines *both* the internal data and the report, with no arrow from experience to data (PDF p. 16). Restricting tests to humans is rejected as implying that non-human reports carry no information about consciousness, and as probably insufficient anyway once brain interventions such as TMS are allowed. Phenomenology-first theories escape only if uniquely derivable from experience, which the authors say no theory currently is.

Two exits are offered rather than defended: **lenient dependency**, a relation between inference and prediction that is neither independent nor strict — "No current theory or testing paradigm that we know of satisfies this definition" (PDF p. 18) — and theories on which **physics is not causally closed**, so that experience itself makes a difference to physical dynamics and can be predicted about directly.

**Relevance.** This is the corpus's most general statement of the testability problem, and the one that refuses to let the functionalist side claim victory. It also shows that the debate is not about IIT's peculiarities: any theory that predicts experience from internal observables while inferring it from reports is caught, and any theory that closes the gap by defining experience in terms of reports loses its empirical content.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2021 · consciousness-science
- Question it asked: Under what conditions can any theory of consciousness be falsified, given that experience is never directly observed but only inferred from report or behaviour?
- Position in one line: If the data used to predict experience and the data used to infer it are independent, then every minimally informative theory is already falsified; if they are strictly dependent, the theory is empirically unfalsifiable — so the current testing scheme itself, not any particular theory, is the problem.
- Stance on computational functionalism: neutral-or-not-about-it — the argument is methodological and catches both sides: causal-structure theories fall to the independence horn, and theories that define consciousness by what is accessible to report or behaviour (behaviourism, and arguably global workspace, the attention schema and "fame in the brain") fall to the strict-dependence horn.
- Responds to / builds on: Doerig et al. 2019 (generalised and corrected); Oizumi, Albantakis & Tononi 2014 and Tononi 2004, 2008 (IIT); Baars 1997 and Dehaene & Changeux 2004 (global workspace); Rosenthal 2002 and Lau & Rosenthal 2011 (higher-order); Seth, Clark, Hohwy, Hobson (predictive processing); Hornik et al. 1989 and Schäfer & Zimmermann 2006 (universal approximation); Turing 1937, Wolfram 1984, Hutter 2004 and Solomonoff 1964 (universal computation and intelligence); Albantakis & Tononi 2019 and Koch 2019 (IIT's own acknowledgement of data independence); Kleiner 2020 and Tsuchiya, Andrillon & Haun 2019 (replies to the unfolding argument); Bayne 2018, Barrett & Mediano 2019, Mediano et al. 2019 (Φ underdetermination); Popper 1959 and Lakatos 1980 (falsification); Block 1996 (access vs phenomenal); Kim 1998 (causal closure).

## 6. Butlin, Long et al. (2023) — Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Butlin et al. 2023 - Consciousness in artificial intelligence - Insights from the science of consciousness.pdf` — **arXiv:2308.08708v3 [cs.AI], 22 August 2023**, an 88-page report by 19 authors (Patrick Butlin and Robert Long joint first authors). Not a journal article; citations below give the report's own page numbers. A footnote on the title page records a post-publication amendment: an earlier version's closing sentence read "…but also shows that there are no obvious barriers to building conscious AI systems", revised "to better reflect the messaging of the report: that satisfying these indicators may be feasible. But satisfying the indicators would not mean that such an AI system would definitely be conscious." **PDF version: preprint.**

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** This report asks a practical question and answers it with a procedure. The question: are current or near-term AI systems conscious? The procedure: survey the leading neuroscientific theories of consciousness, extract from each the *computational* conditions it says a system must meet, assemble those conditions into a checklist, and score actual AI systems against it.

The report's own statement of its contributions (p. 4) is threefold: showing that assessing consciousness in AI is scientifically tractable; proposing a rubric in the form of a list of **indicator properties**; and providing initial evidence that many of those properties can be implemented with current techniques, "although no current system appears to be a strong candidate for consciousness."

**The three assumptions**, stated up front (p. 11):
1. **Computational functionalism** — "Implementing computations of a certain kind is necessary and sufficient for consciousness, so it is possible in principle for non-organic artificial systems to be conscious."
2. **Scientific theories** — neuroscience has made progress in characterising the functions associated with consciousness.
3. **Theory-heavy approach** — assess systems by whether they meet functional or architectural conditions drawn from theories, "as opposed to looking for theory-neutral behavioural signatures."

They are explicit that the first is a working hypothesis, not a conviction: "Some researchers in this area reject computational functionalism (e.g. Searle 1980, Tononi & Koch 2015) but our view is that it is worth exploring its implications" (p. 12). And: "Although we have different levels of confidence in computational functionalism, we agree that it is plausible" (p. 14).

**The output.** Fourteen indicator properties drawn from five sources — recurrent processing theory, global workspace theory, computational higher-order theories, attention schema theory, predictive processing — plus two conditions (agency and embodiment) that are not tied to any one theory. The claim attached to the list is deliberately weaker than any of the theories it comes from: "Systems that have more of these features are better candidates for consciousness. Theories of consciousness make stronger claims than this, such as that some of these are necessary conditions for consciousness, and that combinations are jointly sufficient. We do not endorse these stronger claims" (p. 45).

**Key findings from the case studies.**
- **Transformer-based large language models:** "There is only a relatively weak case that Transformer-based large language models possess any of the GWT-derived indicator properties" (p. 59). The residual stream can be argued to be a workspace, but its dimensionality matches the input's, so the bottleneck is questionable, and more fundamentally Transformers are not recurrent: there are no modules that write to the workspace and receive from it.
- **Perceiver and Perceiver IO:** closer — arguably GWT-1 (modules), GWT-2 (bottleneck) and part of GWT-4 (state-dependent attention) — but the clearest missing element is global broadcast, since on a given trial an output query selects a single output module and input modules do not generally receive from the workspace (p. 60).
- **PaLM-E, a "virtual rodent", and DeepMind's AdA**, tested for agency and embodiment: PaLM-E's components are trained to *imitate* planning and visuomotor control rather than to learn from feedback about success or failure, and being untrained end-to-end it has not been exposed to the effects of its own outputs; the virtual rodent is trained end-to-end by reinforcement learning (sufficient for agency) but its tasks may have been solvable with stereotyped movements; AdA "may, therefore, be the most likely of the three systems we have considered to be embodied by our standards, despite not being physically embodied" (p. 63).

**Initial takeaway.** Two results matter for this corpus. First, the report makes computational functionalism *load-bearing and visible*: it states the thesis, adopts it as a hypothesis, names the cost of its being false, and shows exactly which conclusions would fall with it. Second, it excludes IIT from the survey for that reason — "We do not consider integrated information theory, because it is not compatible with computational functionalism" (p. 5) — which makes the entry-1 / entry-6 pair the sharpest fork in this part.

### Phase 2: Methods deep dive and argument structure

#### 2.1 How the report defines computational functionalism (§1.2.1, pp. 13–14)

The definition is careful and worth quoting, because several distinct theses are separated:

- Functionalism: "it is necessary and sufficient for a system to be conscious that it has a certain functional organisation: that is, that it can enter a certain range of states, which stand in certain causal relations to each other and to the environment."
- Computational functionalism adds that the relevant organisation is *computational*: "it is sufficient for a state to be conscious that it plays a role of the right kind in the implementation of the right kind of algorithm."
- Multiple realisability follows: "The material substrate of a system does not matter for consciousness except insofar as the substrate affects which algorithms the system can implement."

Three qualifications are then entered, all of which narrow the thesis:
1. **Not every substrate will do.** "Computational functionalism does not entail that any substrate can be used to construct a conscious system (Block 1996). As Michel and Lau (2021) put it, 'Swiss cheese cannot implement the relevant computations.'"
2. **Not input–output equivalence.** "Systems that compute the same mathematical function may do so by implementing different algorithms, so computational functionalism does not imply that systems that 'do the same thing' in the sense that they compute the same input-output function are necessarily alike in consciousness (Sprevak 2007)." In Marr's terms the relevant level is the *algorithmic and representational* one — neither the implementation level nor the abstract input–output level. This is a substantive choice: it places the report's functionalism between the target of the unfolding argument (entry 4, which works at the input–output level) and the causal-structure theories it exempts.
3. **Format may matter.** "It is consistent with computational functionalism that consciousness may depend on performing operations on states with specific representational formats, such as analogue representation (Block 2023)."

A footnote adds a metaphysical neutrality claim: the thesis is compatible both with there being "nothing more to a state's being conscious than its playing a certain role in implementing a computation" and with a state's consciousness being "a matter of its having sui generis phenomenal properties, for which its role in implementing a computation is sufficient" (p. 13, n. 3). Box 2 (pp. 16–17) makes the parallel point for materialism, property dualism, panpsychism and illusionism: "there is work for the science of consciousness to do on all four of these metaphysical positions."

The argument offered for the plausibility of the thesis is Chalmers's, cited in a footnote: "if a person's neurons were gradually replaced by functionally-equivalent artificial prostheses, their behaviour would stay the same, so it is implausible that they would undergo any radical change in conscious experience (if they did, they would act as though they hadn't noticed)" (p. 14, n. 4).

#### 2.2 The theory-heavy method, and the argument against behavioural tests (§1.2.3)

The inference rule is stated as a three-factor credence: "our confidence that a given system is conscious can be determined by (a) the similarity of its computational processes to those posited by a given scientific theory of consciousness, (b) our confidence in this theory, (c) and our confidence in computational functionalism" (p. 17).

The alternative — behavioural tests — is rejected for a specific reason: "we are sceptical about whether behavioural approaches to consciousness in AI can avoid the problem that AI systems may be trained to mimic human behaviour while working in very different ways, thus 'gaming' behavioural tests" (p. 18). Schneider's (2019) Artificial Consciousness Test, which looks for a ready grasp of consciousness-related concepts and "problem intuitions" such as the judgement that spectrum inversion is possible, is discussed and found to face a dilemma about training data: restricting a system's access to human writing on consciousness may or may not prevent gaming, and it is unclear whether one can give it enough data to engage with the test without giving it enough to game it (citing Udell & Schwitzgebel 2021).

The report also states the strongest objection to its own method, from Birch's (2022b) "theory-light" approach: evidence from humans "does not tell us how much their conditions can be relaxed while still being sufficient for consciousness" (p. 17). The reply is comparative rather than absolute — "Some AI systems will use processes that are much more similar to those identified by theories of consciousness than others, and this objection does not count against the claim that those using more similar processes are correspondingly better candidates" (p. 18) — and the report concedes the underlying interpretive problem: theories can be read restrictively (only this very specific human process suffices) or liberally (surprisingly simple systems qualify), and "it is not clear that these [moderate interpretations] have empirical support over the alternatives" (p. 17).

#### 2.3 Why IIT is excluded (§2.4 preamble, p. 33)

This is the passage that positions the report against entry 1, quoted in full because the synthesis will need it:

> "One theory that we do not discuss below is integrated information theory (IIT; Oizumi et al. 2014, Tononi & Koch 2015). The standard construal of IIT is incompatible with our working assumption of computational functionalism; Tononi & Koch (2015) hold that a system that implemented the same algorithm as the human brain would not be conscious if its components were of the wrong kind. Relatedly, proponents of IIT claim that the theory implies that digital computers are unlikely to be conscious, whatever programs they run (Albantakis & Tononi 2021). As a result, in contrast to other scientific theories, IIT does not imply that some AI systems built on conventional hardware would be better candidates for consciousness than others; this makes it less relevant to our project."

The report notes "weak IIT" (Michel & Lau 2020; Mediano et al. 2022), on which measures of integration correlate with global states such as wakefulness, sleep and coma, but finds its implications limited: it "does not (yet) tell us which measures to rely on or how to interpret their results when applied to artificial systems."

Three lessons are drawn from trying to apply the list (pp. 47–48): assessment "typically involves some interpretation of the description of the property", since precision beyond the theories' own would be less well supported by evidence; architecture, training and behaviour are *not* always sufficient to decide, so "interpretability methods, such as examining the information encoded in hidden layers … would be required"; and "if it is possible at all to build conscious AI systems without radically new hardware, it may well be possible now."

#### 2.5 Implications the report draws, and declines to draw

- **Both directions of error carry risk (§4.1).** Under-attribution: if systems capable of conscious suffering are built and unrecognised, harms follow at scale, with the farmed-animal case as the analogy and developer incentives as an aggravating factor. Over-attribution — "indeed, this already seems to be happening" — risks misallocating concern, discrediting better-evidenced claims, conflicting with safe-development practices, and displacing human relationships. The report separates being conscious from being capable of conscious suffering: "It is at least conceptually possible that there could be conscious systems that have no valenced or affective conscious experiences", and theories of valence are "less mature than theories of visual experience, suggesting an important priority for future work" (pp. 64–65).
- **Consciousness does not imply human-like motives (§4.2).** Conceptually, "there is no apparent conceptual incoherence in the idea of motivationally neutral conscious experiences which lack valence or affect"; empirically, the surveyed theories do not claim otherwise. The report notes that existential-risk arguments about AI "do not assume consciousness" (p. 68).
- **Capability incentives point toward the indicators.** Goyal and Bengio's global-workspace-inspired programme and LeCun's "configurator" are cited as cases where researchers are building consciousness-associated features to raise capability — "regardless of whether building in consciousness-associated features is the only possible route to greater capabilities, it is one that we are likely to take" (p. 67). A counterweight is offered: animal minds evolved under data, energy and ancestry constraints that do not bind AI design, "so we may well find ways to build high-performing AI systems which are not conscious."
- **Recommendations (§4.3)** are deliberately narrow: support research on consciousness science and its application to AI; use the theory-heavy method both prospectively and retrospectively (with mechanistic interpretability for the latter); extend theories to non-human animals; prioritise computational theories of valence; and keep working on behavioural tests, which "need not be 'theory-neutral'". The report lists but explicitly does not assess the standing policy proposals — Bryson's case that conscious AI should be avoided, Metzinger's moratorium call, Graziano's arguments in favour, and Schwitzgebel and Garza's "build only if confident either way".
- **Open questions (Box 4, pp. 69–70)** name the corpus's own fault line as a research target: "Determining whether consciousness is possible on conventional computer hardware is a difficult problem … sceptics of computational functionalism have noted that living organisms are not only self-maintaining homeostatic systems but are made up of cells that themselves engage in active self-maintenance (e.g. Seth 2021, Aru et al. 2023); further work could clarify why this might matter." It also asks whether unconventional (e.g. neuromorphic) silicon might differ from standard computers — the same exception entry 1 reserves.

## 7. Albantakis et al. (2023) — Integrated information theory (IIT) 4.0: Formulating the properties of phenomenal existence in physical terms [tier: short]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Albantakis et al. 2023 - Integrated information theory 4.0.pdf` — the published open-access (CC-BY) research article, *PLOS Computational Biology* 19(10): e1011465, 17 October 2023, 45 pages, 16 authors with Giulio Tononi as corresponding author. The article carries a competing-interests declaration: Tononi "holds an executive position and has a financial interest in Intrinsic Powers, Inc., a company whose purpose is to develop a device that can be used in the clinic to assess the presence and absence of consciousness in patients" (p. 2). **PDF version: published.**

**Claim.** This is the current canonical statement of the theory reviewed informally in entry 1. Its stated purpose is to express the postulates mathematically: "In principle, the postulates can be applied to any system of units in a state to determine whether it is conscious, to what degree, and in what way" (abstract, p. 1). Three changes since IIT 3.0 are named: a more accurate formulation of the axioms as postulates, a unique measure of *intrinsic information* consistent with the postulates, and an explicit treatment of causal *relations* (p. 3).

**Method or type.** A formal framework paper. No new empirical data ("There are no primary data in the paper"); the code is the PyPhi toolbox. The structure runs from five axioms of phenomenal existence through five matching postulates of physical existence to formulas, then to worked examples on small networks.

**Key result or argument.** The axioms are restated more tersely than in 2015 and now include a zeroth: existence ("Experience exists: there is something"), then **intrinsicality** (it exists for itself), **information** (it is this one), **integration** (it is a whole), **exclusion** (it is this whole), **composition** (it is structured by distinctions and the relations that bind them) (pp. 3–4). The paper explicitly lists candidate properties it *rejects* as non-essential — space, time, change, subject–object distinction, intentionality, sense of self, figure–ground, situatedness, will and affect — on the ground that experiences lacking each are conceivable and "also achievable, as revealed by altered states of consciousness reached through dreaming, meditative practices, or drugs" (p. 4).

The physical side rests on an operational criterion: "in physical terms, to be is to have cause–effect power" — to "take and make a difference" (p. 3). The formal chain is:

**Intrinsic information.** For a system `S` in current state `s` considering a possible effect state, the paper defines (eq. 5, p. 13)

$$ii_e(s,\bar{s}) = p_e(\bar{s}\mid s)\,\log\!\left(\frac{p_e(\bar{s}\mid s)}{p_e(\bar{s})}\right),$$

the product of *selectivity* (how concentrated the power is on that state) and *informativeness* (deviation from chance), with a symmetric definition on the cause side (eq. 7). Probabilities are interventional, and the unconstrained probability averages over all states with equal weight. The state maximising `ii` is the *maximal cause–effect state*.

**System integrated information** `φ_s` measures how much that maximal state's intrinsic information is reduced by the *minimum partition* — the directional partition that makes the least difference. **Exclusion** then selects the set of units maximising it, written `φ*_s`; that set is a **maximal substrate**, or **complex**. Crucially, many overlapping sets may have `φ_s` greater than zero; only maxima qualify.

**Composition** unfolds the complex into *distinctions* (a mechanism together with the cause and effect state it specifies, valued by `φ_d`) and *relations* (congruent overlaps among distinctions' states, valued by `φ_r`). The whole is a **Φ-structure**, and "the sum total of the φ values of the distinctions and relations that compose the Φ-structure measures its structure integrated information Φ … and corresponds to the quantity of consciousness" (p. 5); the structure itself is the quality.

**The explanatory identity:** "every property of an experience is accounted for in full by the physical properties of the Φ-structure unfolded from a maximal substrate (a complex) in its current state, with no further or 'ad hoc' ingredients" (pp. 5–6).

**Place in the field's history.** For this part of the corpus the decisive section is "Phenomenal versus functional equivalence" (pp. 36–39). The paper constructs three three-unit systems that realise the *same* input–output function (counting to eight) and even pass through the same sequence of global states, but differ in architecture. Their Φ-structures come out entirely different: Φ = 21.01 ibits for the first, Φ = 3.64 ibits for the second with the same system-level `φ_s` = 2 ibits, and `φ_s` = 0 for the third, which has only feedforward connections from one unit to the others and "condenses into three complexes with small Φ-structures" (p. 36). The conclusion drawn is the corpus's sharpest anti-functionalist sentence: "they are equivalent in what they do extrinsically, but not in what they are intrinsically" (p. 37), and "Each experience corresponds to a Φ-structure, not a set of functions, processes, or computations. Said otherwise, consciousness is about being, not doing" (p. 39).

The unfolding fact is accepted, not contested: "the behavior (input–output function) of any (discrete) recurrent system can also be implemented by a system with a feed-forward architecture", citing Krohn & Rhodes's prime decomposition theorem. The inference drawn from it is the opposite of Doerig et al.'s (entry 4): "This implies that any behavior performed by a conscious system supported by a recurrent architecture can also be performed by an unconscious system, no matter how complex the behavior is. More generally, digital computers implementing programs capable of artificial general intelligence may in principle be able to emulate any function performed by conscious humans and yet, because of the way they are physically organized, they would do so without experiencing anything" (p. 37). And in the discussion: "one consequence of IIT is that typical computer architectures are not suitable for supporting consciousness, no matter whether their behavior may resemble ours. By the same token, it can be inferred from IIT that animal species that may look and behave quite differently from us may be highly conscious, as long as their brains have a compatible architecture" (p. 40).

Two qualifications the paper itself enters are worth recording. First, tractability: "it is not possible in practice to exhaustively apply the postulates to unfold the cause–effect power of realistic systems", because of "multiple, nested combinatorial explosions", so "a full analysis of complexes and their cause–effect structure can only be performed on idealized systems of a few units" (p. 39); approximations such as "cut one" are offered. Second, openness: "some of the algorithmic choices remain open to further evaluation", including the treatment of background conditions and tie-breaking under symmetry (p. 40). It cites Barrett & Mediano's "the phi measure … is not well-defined for general physical systems" in its own reference list.

**Relevance.** This entry supplies the precise version of the claim that entry 4 attacks and entry 6 cites as its reason for excluding IIT. It also corrects a loose gloss: a recurrent system's having `φ_s` > 0 is *not* sufficient on IIT's own terms — the substrate must be a **maximum** of integrated information among overlapping candidates. Read together with entry 1, it shows what did and did not change in eight years: the axioms were re-listed and a zeroth added, relations and a unique intrinsic-information measure were introduced, and the verdict on digital computers was restated in the same terms.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2023 · consciousness-science
- Question it asked: Can the essential properties of experience be formulated as mathematically explicit requirements on a physical substrate, such that any system of units in a state can in principle be evaluated for whether, how much, and in what way it is conscious?
- Position in one line: An experience is identical to the Φ-structure — the fully unfolded set of causal distinctions and relations — specified by a maximal substrate of intrinsic cause–effect power, with the quantity of consciousness given by the summed integrated information Φ and the quality by the structure's form.

## Cross-paper notes for the synthesis

These are observations that only appear when the seven entries are read together. They are recorded, not adjudicated.

**1. The seven papers split three ways, not two.**
- *Theories that identify consciousness with causal structure*: Tononi & Koch 2015, Albantakis et al. 2023 (and, as a target of the same critique, Lamme's recurrent processing theory, which is not in this batch but is surveyed inside Butlin et al.).
- *Theories that identify consciousness with computational function*: Dehaene, Lau & Kouider 2017 (global availability plus self-monitoring), Graziano 2017 (a self-model of attention).
- *Meta-level papers about whether either kind can be tested*: Doerig et al. 2019 (only the first kind is in trouble), Kleiner & Hoel 2021 (both kinds are, in different ways). Butlin et al. 2023 sits outside this trio: it takes the functionalist side as a working hypothesis and builds a measurement procedure on it.

**2. Everyone agrees on the mathematical fact; the disagreement is about what follows.** Tononi & Koch state the recurrent-to-feedforward unfolding themselves in 2015 (p. 13, with a bounded-time-step qualification), Albantakis et al. restate it in 2023 citing Krohn & Rhodes's prime decomposition theorem (p. 37), and Doerig et al. call it "uncontroversial and widely accepted (including by proponents of IIT)" (p. 52). From the *same* premise, IIT concludes that behaviour is an unreliable guide to consciousness, while Doerig et al. conclude that a theory which says so has left empirical science. Doerig et al. name this convergence explicitly (p. 56). A synthesis should present this as a shared premise with divergent conclusions, not as a factual dispute.

**3. A characterisation of IIT that differs between papers.** Doerig et al. write that on IIT "φ is always greater than zero in recurrent systems (they are always conscious)" (p. 51). Albantakis et al. require more: "Many overlapping sets of units may have a positive value of integrated information … However, the exclusion postulate requires that the substrate of consciousness must be constituted of a definite set of units" — the one maximising `φ_s` (p. 10). Whether this weakens the unfolding argument is a live question in the replies Kleiner & Hoel cite (Kleiner 2020; Tsuchiya, Andrillon & Haun 2019), none of which is in this batch. Note that Doerig et al.'s own constructions are built to satisfy maximality (the inserted device is the maximal-Φ complex precisely because everything around it is feedforward), so the gloss and the construction come apart.

**4. Two papers explicitly refuse to answer the question the others are asking.** Graziano's AST "emphatically does not explain how we have a subjective experience. It explains how a machine claims to have a subjective experience" (p. 5). Dehaene, Lau & Kouider declare the experiential question "beyond the scope of the present paper" (p. 492) and offer a covariation observation about blindsight instead. Both nonetheless deliver a verdict on machines. A field history should mark this as a *change of explanandum*, since the theories they are compared against (IIT) take the experiential question as their starting point.

**5. The two "untestability" papers are not the same argument, and one catches the functionalists.** Doerig et al. exempt global workspace, higher-order thought and predictive processing by name and state that their argument "favours functionalist theories" (p. 54). Kleiner & Hoel generalise the setup and show a second horn: theories on which consciousness is determined by what is accessible to report — they name behaviourism, and "arguably" global workspace theory, the attention schema theory and "fame in the brain" — make prediction data a function of inference data, which yields empirical unfalsifiability (PDF p. 14). So on Kleiner & Hoel's analysis the two theories that entry 4 protects are exposed by a different mechanism. Kleiner & Hoel describe their paper as "both a generalization and correction" of the unfolding argument (PDF p. 2).

**6. Butlin et al. excludes IIT, and says why.** "We do not consider integrated information theory, because it is not compatible with computational functionalism" (p. 5), with the fuller statement on p. 33 citing Tononi & Koch 2015 and Albantakis & Tononi 2021. This is the cleanest evidence in the batch that computational functionalism now functions as a *methodological gate* in AI-consciousness work: the assumption determines which theories are even admitted to the assessment.

**7. Nobody in this batch is a carbon chauvinist, including the anti-functionalists.** Tononi & Koch's endnote 15 states that "there is no reason why hardware-level, neuromorphic models of the human brain that do not rely on software running on a digital computer, could not approximate, one day, our level of consciousness", and its Internet discussion allows a high-Φ complex in principle. Albantakis et al. say animals "that may look and behave quite differently from us may be highly conscious, as long as their brains have a compatible architecture" (p. 40). Butlin et al.'s open questions ask whether "unconventional (e.g. neuromorphic) silicon hardware" might differ from standard computers (p. 70). The dividing line drawn in this batch is *computation vs. physical causal organisation*, not *biology vs. machine*. The biological-naturalism challenge proper (Searle, Seth, Godfrey-Smith, Aru et al.) sits in batches B and C.

**8. Dehaene et al. and Tononi & Koch agree that today's deep networks are not conscious, for incompatible reasons.** For IIT, feedforward architecture gives Φ = 0 and no amount of added computation helps unless the causal organisation changes. For the global-workspace account, today's networks simply perform the wrong computations (C0), and adding a workspace and a self-monitor would suffice. Butlin et al.'s case studies operationalise the second view and reach a negative verdict on current systems (Transformers have "only a relatively weak case" for any global-workspace indicator, p. 59) — a negative verdict that a naive reading might mistake for agreement with IIT.

**9. Empirical anchors each theory claims.** Useful for a diagram: IIT points to the perturbational complexity index falling across sleep, anaesthesia and disorders of consciousness; global workspace points to all-or-none ignition, the attentional blink, the psychological refractory period and prefrontal decoding in no-report monkey paradigms; higher-order theories point to prefrontal inactivation impairing metacognition while sparing first-order performance; AST points to attention being less stable and less correctable without awareness. Doerig et al. accept the IIT-derived clinical measures as *markers* while denying the identity (p. 54).
