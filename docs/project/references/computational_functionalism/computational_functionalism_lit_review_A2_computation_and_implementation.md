# Computational Functionalism Field History — Part A2: Computation and Implementation

**Topic folder:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/`
**Part of:** a multi-file, neutral review of how ideas about *computational functionalism* developed — the thesis that mental states are constituted by the computations a system performs, so that running the right computation in any material would suffice for having a mind.

## What this part is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question.** Computational functionalism says a mind is whatever runs the right computation. That claim only means something if there is a fact of the matter about *which* computation a lump of matter is running. Otherwise anything could be said to run anything, and "the right computation" picks out nothing. This part collects the seven works that fought over exactly that: **what makes a physical system implement a computation**, and **what kind of computation, if any, the brain performs**.

**Why it matters for the field's history.** Two challenges nearly ended computational functionalism on technical grounds rather than on grounds about consciousness.

- **Triviality.** In 1988 the philosopher Hilary Putnam — who had himself invented the functionalist position two decades earlier — published what he presented as a proof that *every ordinary physical object realises every finite-state machine*. If that held, then a rock implements the computation your brain implements, and computational functionalism would entail that rocks have minds. John Searle argued, separately and less formally, that his wall could be read as running the WordStar word processor.
- **Individuation.** Even if a system implements *some* computation, which one? If the answer depends on what the states of the system *mean* — what they represent — then computation is not the fundamental level that computational functionalism assumed, and the theory inherits every problem that theories of mental content have.

**The story the seven works tell.**

- **Chalmers (1996, "Does a rock implement every finite-state automaton?")** answers the triviality challenge. His diagnosis: Putnam's construction reproduces only the *trace* of the machine — the particular sequence of states it happened to go through — and not its structure. Implementation requires transitions that would have held *had things gone otherwise*. He then concedes a weakened version of Putnam's result for the simplest machines, argues that those machines were never the right description of a mind anyway, and replaces them with **combinatorial state automata**, whose states are vectors of independently varying parts. Those are hard to implement by accident.
- **Shagrir (2006)** asks the descriptive question instead of the normative one: why do brain scientists *actually* call the brain a computer, and not the solar system or a washing machine? He grants the triviality point — being a computer is partly a matter of perspective — and answers that we take the computational perspective when the thing we want to explain is a regularity described in terms of *what the signals are about*, such as working out where a target is from where the eyes are pointing and where its image falls on the retina. Computational models explain such regularities, he argues, because the mathematical relations among the brain's signals mirror mathematical relations among the things those signals track. So for him computation and representation come as a package.
- **Piccinini (2010, "The mind as neural software")** pulls apart two claims that had been welded together for forty years: that the mind is the *functional organisation* of the brain, and that this organisation is *computational*. Neither entails the other, he argues, once "functional organisation" is explained in terms of **mechanisms** — components with functions, arranged in a particular way — rather than in terms of computational descriptions. He also gives "software" its narrow, computer-science meaning, on which a stored program is a physical part of the machine and not merely a description of it. The upshot is that whether the brain runs software is an empirical question for neuroscience, not one philosophy settles.
- **Sprevak (2010)** presses the individuation problem and defends what he calls the **received view** — that computation always involves representational content. His sharpest example is a single electrical circuit that outputs a high voltage only when both its inputs are high. Call high voltage "1" and it is an AND gate; call high voltage "0" and the very same circuit is an OR gate. Nothing physical decides between the two. So, he argues, computational identity cannot be fixed without reference to what the states represent.
- **Chalmers (1993/2011, "A computational foundation for the study of cognition")** is the positive programme the 1996 paper cleared ground for. It separates two theses: **computational sufficiency** (implementing the right computation suffices for a mind) and **computational explanation** (computation is the right framework for explaining cognition and behaviour), and defends both. The bridge is what he calls **causal topology** — the pattern of interaction among a system's parts, with the parts' material stripped away. A property that survives every change preserving that pattern is an *organizational invariant*; his central claim is that mental properties, including conscious experience, are organizational invariants. A footnote added in 2011 weakens this from a claim about what is metaphysically necessary to one about what holds as a matter of natural law.
- **Piccinini and Bahar (2013)** ask what the brain literally does, and treat it as a question for neuroscience rather than philosophy. They separate a broad, permissive notion of computation — processing signals whose physical make-up is irrelevant to the rules governing them, which they call **generic computation** — from its two familiar species, *digital* (strings of discrete symbols) and *analog* (continuous quantities). The brain, they argue from the neurophysiology of spikes, does the first but neither of the other two: neural computation is **sui generis**, and needs mathematics of its own.
- **Coelho Mollo (2018)** reframes the whole dispute by splitting one question into two. *Which* computation a system performs is settled functionally, by the pattern of transformations it imposes on classes of physical states — which is why a hydraulic machine and an electronic one can run the same computation. *How* a particular system manages to perform it is a separate, thoroughly material question about its mechanism, answered in terms of voltages or water levels. Structural detail is demanded by the second question and irrelevant to the first, which is why the two never had to conflict.

**A note on what this part does not cover.** Whether the *right* computation running in silicon would be conscious is argued in the other parts of this review; the papers here mostly bracket that and ask the prior question of what "running a computation" even amounts to.

## 1. Chalmers (1996) — Does a Rock Implement Every Finite-State Automaton? [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Chalmers 1996 - Does a rock implement every finite-state automaton.pdf` — the published *Synthese* article, 108: 309–333 (Kluwer, 1996), as a scanned and OCR'd copy. **PDF version: published.** OCR noise is visible in the mathematical notation (subscripts and superscripts are frequently mangled); all formal statements below were reconstructed from the surrounding prose and are marked where the OCR was ambiguous.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Cognitive science had grown used to saying that the brain "implements" some abstract machine, and artificial intelligence rested on a stronger claim Chalmers names **computational sufficiency**: "there is a class of automata such that any implementation of an automaton in that class will possess a mind" (p. 309). Hilary Putnam, in an appendix to *Representation and Reality* (1988, pp. 120–25), offered what he presented as a theorem that would wreck both: **every ordinary open physical system realizes every abstract finite automaton**. If a rock implements every automaton, then computational sufficiency makes the rock a mind. Putnam drew the conclusion that computational functionalism cannot found a theory of mind.

Chalmers' reply has three parts. First, Putnam's proof does not work, because it gets the *implementation* relation wrong. Second, a weakened version of Putnam's result can nevertheless be recovered — and what that shows is not that computationalism fails but that the *formalism* Putnam chose (the finite-state automaton, or FSA) was never a serious candidate description of a mind. Third, a better formalism is available: the **combinatorial state automaton** (CSA), whose internal states are vectors of independently varying components. Implementing a complex CSA demands fine-grained causal structure that arbitrary objects do not have.

**Key findings (as argued).**
- Implementation is not a matter of the physical system's actual sequence of states matching the automaton's. It requires **counterfactual-supporting** state-transition conditionals: *if* the system were in the physical correlate of formal state P, it *would* transit to a correlate of Q — "however it finds itself in the first state" (p. 316). Chalmers calls these **strong conditionals**.
- Putnam's construction gives only a **trace** of the automaton — the run it happened to take — not its structure. Transitions that never came up on the run correspond to nothing physical (p. 315).
- Add two cheap parts, a **clock** (a subsystem that reliably steps through a sequence of states) and a **dial** (a subsystem with many states that stays put once set), and Putnam's conclusion *is* recoverable for **inputless** FSAs. Chalmers proves this himself (p. 317). The lesson he draws is that inputless FSAs are trivial: their entire state space is "a single unbranching sequence of states ending in a cycle" (p. 318).
- For FSAs *with* input and output, Putnam's weaker claim — that such an automaton is realized by any system with the right input/output dependencies, whence "functionalism, if it were correct, would imply behaviorism!" (Putnam pp. 124–25, quoted at p. 320) — is likewise recoverable if the system has an **input memory** plus a dial (p. 322). Chalmers accepts the recovered result and calls it "quite counterintuitive, but it seems that we are stuck with it" (p. 322).
- The CSA changes the picture. Because its state is a vector whose components must be *independently variable* and must supervene on *distinct physical regions*, implementing a complex CSA requires "a complex causal interaction among a number of separate parts" (p. 325). Arbitrary systems have "no hope of passing" (p. 326).
- Searle's claim (1990) that his wall implements WordStar fails for the same reason: the wall does not satisfy the strong conditionals (p. 331). A weak observer-relativity survives — most systems implement *many* automata, so which description one uses depends on one's purposes — but whether a given system implements a given CSA is "an objective one" (p. 331).

**Initial takeaway.** This is the paper that made "implementation" a technical topic in its own right. Its lasting contribution is less the defence of computationalism than the criterion it introduced — *the causal structure of the system must mirror the formal structure of the automaton, counterfactuals included* — and the admission, made in the same paper, that this criterion is not yet quite sufficient (§7 leaves two problems open).

### Phase 2: Argument Reconstruction

#### 2.1 The thesis under attack, in Chalmers' own terms

> "The ambitions of artificial intelligence rest on a related claim of **computational sufficiency**, holding that there is a class of automata such that any implementation of an automaton in that class will possess a mind." (p. 309)

Implementation is the bridge: "the relation that connects the theory of computation with the theory of physical systems. (Putnam calls this relation 'realization'.)" (p. 310).

#### 2.2 Putnam's argument, as Chalmers reconstructs it

Putnam's premises:

- **P1 (Principle of Physical Continuity).** Accepted by Chalmers; plays only a minor role.
- **P2 (Principle of Noncyclical Behavior).** Every ordinary open system is in *different maximal states at different times*, because it is exposed to electromagnetic and gravitational radiation from a natural clock. (A *maximal state* specifies the system's physical makeup in perfect detail.) Chalmers accepts this too, noting that even a restricted version — every *noncyclic* system implements every FSA — "would still be a strong one" (p. 310).
- **P3 (implicit account of implementation).** Chalmers supplies what Putnam's discussion requires:

  > A physical system implements an inputless FSA in a given time-period if there is a mapping $f$ from physical states of the system to formal states of the FSA such that: if the system is in physical state $p$ during the time-period, this causes it to transit into a state $q$ such that formal state $f(p)$ transits to formal state $f(q)$ in the specification of the FSA. (p. 311)

The construction, for a rock $S$ over 12:00–12:07:

1. Take times $t_1,\dots,t_n$ (here $n=7$, one per minute) and $t_{n+1}$ for the end of the period.
2. For each $i$, define a **non-maximal interval state** $s_i$ = the region of phase space consisting of all maximal states $\mathrm{St}(S,t)$ with $t_i \le t < t_{i+1}$. Left endpoint included, right excluded, so the system is in exactly one $s_i$ at a time. **P2** guarantees the $s_i$ are disjoint.
3. Take any inputless FSA. Putnam takes one that alternates between two states $A$ and $B$, so the required trace over the period is $ABABABA$.
4. Define disjunctive physical states
   $$a \;=\; s_1 \vee s_3 \vee s_5 \vee s_7, \qquad b \;=\; s_2 \vee s_4 \vee s_6,$$
   and set $f(a)=A$, $f(b)=B$.
5. The rock then cycles through $ababa\,ba$ — Chalmers writes the sequence as $abababa$ — matching $ABABABA$.
6. **Generalisation.** For any inputless FSA and any noncyclic system, associate the initial physical state with the FSA's initial state, associate subsequent physical states with the FSA's subsequent states as fixed by its transition rules, and map the disjunction of associated physical states onto each formal state.

**Conclusion (Putnam).** Every noncyclic physical system implements every inputless FSA. Hence computational functionalism is empty.

#### 2.3 Two objections Chalmers does *not* press

- That the construction uses "**unnatural**" disjunctive states. Chalmers declines this line: "there does not seem to be an objective distinction between 'natural' and 'unnatural' states that can do the relevant work; and an objective distinction is needed, if having a mind is to be an objective matter" (p. 312).
- That the states are "**time-varying**". He calls this "a misreading of the argument. The states themselves are quite invariant over time. Time has simply been used to fix reference to these atemporal states" (p. 312).

Both refusals matter historically: they concede a great deal to Putnam before the attack begins.

#### 2.4 The attack: strong conditionals

The core premise of the reply:

> "The conditionals involved in the definition of implementation are not ordinary material conditionals... Rather, these conditionals have modal force, and in particular are required to support counterfactuals: *if the system were to be in state $p$, then it would transit into state $q$*." (p. 312)

Chalmers calls these **strong conditionals** and glosses them as a lawfulness requirement: "What makes the law of gravity a law is that under any circumstances, if two bodies have an appropriate mass, there will be such-and-such a force exerted. If it only happens between 12:00 and 12:07 today, due to unusual environmental conditions, it does not count" (p. 316).

He notes an important defensive point: these are *not* similarity-based counterfactuals in David Lewis's sense, so Putnam's reply to a Lewis-style requirement (that similarity metrics over possible worlds are vaguer than what is being explicated) does not touch them. "It requires that in all (or perhaps most) situations in which the system is in state $a$, it will transit into state $b$, no matter how similar or dissimilar that situation is from the actual one" (p. 316).

**Failure 1 — the transitions actually exhibited are not reliable.** Putnam's system is by definition *open* to environmental influence (he needs that openness for **P2**). So had conditions differed slightly, the rock might have been "buffeted into an entirely different state well before $t_{n+1}$" (p. 313).

Chalmers' close reading of Putnam's determination argument turns on a single clause. Putnam writes that a mathematically omniscient being can determine how $S$ evolves "given the boundary conditions at subsequent times and the other laws of nature" (Putnam p. 123, quoted at p. 314). Chalmers:

> "What is important here is the clause 'given boundary conditions at subsequent times'. This is information that the omniscient being in question may not use... It is illegitimate to appeal to the boundary conditions throughout the next interval." (p. 314)

The reason is that allowing it makes the claim trivial: "this is no more interesting than noting that if we fix the physical state during the next time interval, the physical state will be fixed" (p. 314).

**Failure 2 — unmanifested transitions are not mirrored.** An FSA specified by $A\to B$, $B\to A$, $C\to D$, $D\to E$, … requires that *if* the system had been in the correlate of $C$ it *would* have transited to the correlate of $D$. Putnam's construction leaves the physical correlates of unvisited formal states undefined. Read materially, those conditionals hold vacuously — which is exactly why the modal reading is needed (p. 315).

The compressed form of the objection:

> "We cannot infer that any system that goes through the sequence $abababa$ implements the FSA. In effect, Putnam has failed to ensure that his system reflects the structure of the FSA. All he has done is ensure that a particular **trace** of the FSA — that is, the states it goes through on a particular run — is reflected by the system's behavior." (p. 315; the trace observation is credited in n. 2 to Joseph O'Rourke)

**Revised definition (inputless FSAs).**

> A physical system implements an inputless FSA in a given time-period if there is a mapping $f$ from physical states of the system **onto** formal states of the FSA such that: for **every** formal state-transition $P \to Q$ in the specification of the FSA, if the physical system is in a state $p$ such that $f(p)=P$, this causes it to transit into a state $q$ such that $f(q)=Q$. (p. 315)

Two changes: the mapping must be *onto* (every formal state has a physical correlate), and a conditional must hold for *each* transition in the machine table.

#### 2.5 Chalmers' own repair of Putnam's result — the clock and the dial

Chalmers then argues *against himself*, showing the objections are cheap to meet.

- A system **contains a clock** if it has a subsystem that reliably transits through a sequence $s_1, s_2, \dots$ "irrespective of environmental conditions" (p. 316). Most clocks do this; possibly radiation-decay patterns do.
- A system **contains a dial** if it has a subsystem with an arbitrary number of states "such that when it is put into one of those states it stays in that state come what may" (p. 317) — marks on rocks will do.

> **Claim.** Every physical system containing a clock and a dial will implement every inputless FSA. (p. 317)

*Proof sketch as given.* Label physical states $[i,j]$ with $i$ the clock state and $j$ the dial state; from $[1,j]$ the system reliably steps to $[2,j], [3,j], \dots$ Assume the actual dial state is 1. Associate $[1,1]$ with the FSA's initial state and $[2,1],[3,1],\dots$ with its successors. For any FSA state $P$ not yet used, associate $[1,2]$ with it and $[2,2],[3,2],\dots$ with $P$'s successors; repeat until the FSA's states are exhausted. Map the disjunction of the associated $[i,j]$ onto each formal state. Every strong conditional in the strengthened definition is then satisfied (p. 317).

*A residual technicality Chalmers flags.* States $[n,i]$ at the final time-step have no established physical successor. He patches this by assuming the clock has an infinite sequence of states and mapping infinite disjunctions onto formal states — "not an especially unrealistic assumption; there are probably such infinite clocks in most continuous systems" (p. 317).

**What he concludes from his own repair.** Not that computationalism fails, but that "inputless FSAs are an inappropriate formalism" (p. 317): their state space is a single unbranching sequence ending in a cycle (or finitely many such), which is "a completely uninteresting kind of structure, as indeed is witnessed by the fact that it is satisfied by a simple combination of a dial and a clock" (p. 318). The cognitive gloss: "A wind-up toy or perhaps a videotape of my life could go through the same sequence of states, but it would not be a cognitive system. Cognition requires at least the possibility of functioning in more than one way" (p. 318).

**Anticipated reply (the blind, deaf paralytic).** If minds require an automaton *with* input and output, what of a person with effectively no sensory input or motor output? Chalmers answers that the inputs should be relocated inward — "stimulations of the optic nerve, or of the visual cortex or some other internal system, rather than as patterns of photons" — so the counterfactual sensitivity is still present: "*if* it were to be stimulated, their internal states would change in interesting ways" (p. 319). He later gives a second answer via CSAs (§6), which need no input or output at all.

#### 2.6 FSAs with input and output

Putnam concedes no universal realization here — a system must at least have the capacity for input and output — but offers a weaker result: **an FSA with input and output is realized by every physical system with the right input/output dependencies**. Chalmers stresses why that is still damaging: two FSAs can share input/output dependencies while differing enormously inside. His example is a machine that outputs zero on every input versus one that runs an exhaustive primality calculation and outputs zero either way (p. 319). And Putnam draws the corollary himself:

> "Thus we obtain that the assumption that something is a 'realization' of a given automaton description (possesses a specified 'functional organization') is equivalent to the statement that it behaves as if it had that description. In short, 'functionalism', if it were correct, would imply behaviorism!" (Putnam pp. 124–25, quoted at p. 320)

**Implementation conditions extended.** An FSA with input and output is a set of states, inputs, outputs, and transitions $(I,S) \to (S',O)$ for each input/state pair. Then:

> A physical system implements an FSA (with input and output) in a given time-period if there is a mapping $f$ from physical states onto formal states and from the system's inputs and outputs onto the FSA's inputs and outputs such that: for every formal state-transition $(I,S) \to (S',O)$, if the physical system is in a state $s$ and receiving input $i$ with $f(s)=S$ and $f(i)=I$, this causes it to transit into $s'$ with $f(s')=S'$ and to produce output $o$ with $f(o)=O$. (p. 321)

**Why Putnam's construction fails it.** Even with a clock and dial, nothing guarantees the system's behaviour *had the inputs been different*. Worse: "If the sequence of internal states $s_1, s_2, \dots$ is deterministic or reliable in itself... then the system will produce precisely the same transition behavior for any inputs. There is none of the sensitivity that an FSA with input and output is required to have" (p. 321).

**And why the repair is harder here.** The combinatorial nature of $(I,S)$ pairs blocks free disjunction: the same physical state can result from two distinct state/input pairs, so the iterative mapping is not well defined (pp. 321–22).

**The input-memory repair.** Say a system **contains an input memory** if it has a subsystem that goes into a distinct state for every possible sequence of inputs. Then:

> Every physical system with an input memory and a dial implements every FSA with the right input/output dependencies. (p. 322)

*Construction.* Label states $[j, i_1, \dots, i_n]$ ($j$ the dial, $i_1..i_n$ the recorded input sequence, $n \ge 0$). With the dial at 1, associate $[1]$ with an FSA state $S$; for each input sequence $i_1,\dots,i_n$, associate $[1, i_1, \dots, i_n]$ with the FSA state reached by feeding those inputs to the FSA from $S$. If states remain unreached, pick one, associate it with $[2]$, and repeat. Map disjunctions onto formal states.

**What Chalmers accepts as the cost.** "It follows for instance that any implementation of a simple zero-outputting FSA, supplemented with a dial and an input memory, will implement the complex primality-testing FSA. This result is quite counterintuitive, but it seems that we are stuck with it" (p. 322). And: "if AI is dependent on this FSA formalism, then functionalism still almost reduces to behaviorism", since two behaviourally equivalent people would be mentally equivalent "as long as they are carrying input memories and dials" (p. 323). He also raises the **giant lookup table** worry (citing Block 1981) — the construction may imply that an infinite lookup table "would be as conscious as you and me", which he calls troubling (p. 323).

**A second, independent route to the same reduction** (Chalmers' own parenthesis, not Putnam's). Suppose:

> **(++)** If a cognitive system implements two FSAs $F$ and $G$ both of which are sufficient to produce the system's behavioral function, and $G$ implements $F$, then the cognitive properties of the system arise in virtue of its implementing $F$ (as the added structure of $G$ is just irrelevant implementational detail). (p. 323)

By the FSA **minimization theorem** (Hopcroft & Ullman 1979, p. 67), any two FSAs with the same input/output dependencies implement a common simpler FSA with the same dependencies. So two behaviourally equivalent cognitive systems whose cognitive properties arise from implementing an FSA share cognitive properties. Therefore: "if functionalism is not to reduce to behaviorism, we must either reject (++) or reject FSAs as the basis for cognition" (p. 323).

#### 2.7 The positive proposal: combinatorial state automata

> "The trouble is that the internal states of these FSAs are **monadic**, lacking any internal structure, whereas the internal states of most computational and cognitive systems have all sorts of complex structure." (p. 324)

**Definition (as given).** A CSA is like an FSA except that its internal state is a vector
$$[\,S^1,\, S^2,\, \dots,\, S^n\,]$$
where each component takes one of finitely many values, called **substates**. Components are analogous to squares of a Turing machine tape or cells of a cellular automaton; substates to the symbols or cell values. Inputs and outputs may also be structured. Transition rules specify, *for each component of the state vector*, a function giving its new substate in terms of the old overall state vector and input vector — "it will frequently depend on only a few 'nearby' components of these vectors" — and likewise for each element of the output vector (p. 324). Vectors may be finite or infinite; Chalmers restricts to the finite case.

**Implementation conditions (as given).**

> A physical system implements a given CSA if there is a decomposition of its internal states into substates $[s^1, s^2, \dots, s^n]$, and a mapping $f$ from these substates onto corresponding substates $S^j$ of the CSA, along with similar mappings for inputs and outputs, such that: for every formal state transition $([\,I^1,\dots,I^k\,],[\,S^1,\dots,S^n\,]) \to ([\,S'^1,\dots,S'^n\,],[\,O^1,\dots,O^m\,])$ of the CSA, if the system is in internal state $[s^1,\dots,s^n]$ and receiving input $[i^1,\dots,i^k]$ such that the physical states and inputs map to the formal states and inputs, this causes it to enter an internal state and produce an output that map appropriately to the required formal state and output. (p. 325; superscript indices reconstructed — the OCR of this passage is degraded)

He adds a **locality** option: where the CSA's transitions depend only on a few neighbouring substates, the corresponding *restricted* conditional is what must hold (p. 325). And a stipulation that does the real work: "each element in such a decomposition corresponds to a distinct physical region in the system" (p. 325).

**Why this defeats Putnam-style constructions.**
- Finite CSAs are *no more computationally powerful* than FSAs — every CSA can be simulated by some FSA (p. 325). The gain is not in power but in **implementation conditions**: "An implementation of a CSA is required to consist in a complex causal interaction among a number of separate parts" (p. 325).
- "What does the work is precisely the combinatorial nature of the states, through the requirement that the parts of an internal state are independently variable. This imposes a constrained structure of dependencies on the system that arbitrary systems have no hope of passing" (pp. 325–26).
- It also handles the blind-deaf-paralytic case without input or output at all, since combinatorial structure alone blocks the objection (p. 326).
- Generality: "An FSA, a Turing machine, or a cellular automaton can be straightforwardly described as a CSA" (p. 326), and programs in LISP or C can be translated in principle.

#### 2.8 What the paper leaves open (§7) — stated by the author, not by a critic

**Open problem 1: the account still admits some false implementations.** Chalmers constructs the counterexample himself. The naive extension — sending $[a,b,c]$ with input $I$ to $[aI, bI, cI]$ — fails, because recombined states such as $[a,-,-]$ always transit to $[aI,-,-]$ regardless of the other components, which the CSA's transitions will generally forbid. The construction that *does* work makes every component sensitive to every previous component:
$$[a,b,c] \ \text{with input } I \ \longrightarrow\ [\,abcI,\; abcI,\; abcI\,]$$
(concatenation), so each resultant component state has a unique predecessor state-and-input, and recombination is safe (p. 327).

*The price.* The system triples in size per step. "If the strings that make up each component have length $L$ at one point, within 100 time-steps they will have length $3^{100}L$", which Chalmers gives as about $5 \times 10^{47}$ times larger — "In a very short time, the system will be larger than the known universe!" (p. 328). Generally, with $n$ components of $k$ values each at step $t$, each component must carry $k^{n}$ values at $t+1$ and $k^{n^{2}}$ at $t+2$, so size grows by a factor of $n$ per step.

*The upshot he draws.* "The threat of vacuity and of panpsychism have long since disappeared; the only physically possible systems that implement a non-trivial CSA will do so in virtue of having the right sort of fine-grained causal structure among their components" (p. 328). But the *logical* possibility remains troubling, and he judges that such systems "need to be ruled out as implementations, on grounds of lacking the right sort of causal structure" — which means "the account I have given of implementing a CSA is imperfect as it stands" (p. 329). Two candidate fixes are floated and neither is worked out: a **uniformity clause** ("underlying each formal state-transition of a CSA there needs to be a uniform causal mechanism, as opposed to the highly disjunctive causal mechanism found in the false implementations") or a **causal relevance clause** (p. 329). He notes that whether the Library-of-Babel response ("bite the bullet") is available turns on whether computational sufficiency holds with *logical* or merely *natural* necessity (p. 328).

**Open problem 2: the distinct-region requirement may be too strong.** Why not let CSA components be implemented by overlapping physical states? Because *some* independence condition is required, on pain of collapse: without it, any system implementing the corresponding collapsed FSA can be shown to implement the CSA, "and the structure of the CSA would be lost" (pp. 329–30). But real machines violate the strict version — "witness systems with virtual memory, where the value of a given CSA 'component' can be represented in various parts of the system depending on where there is free space" (p. 330). Two fixes are floated: a weaker independence condition, or a two-stage definition (the system implements a more complex CSA, which bears a purely mathematical relation to the target CSA). Left open.

#### 2.9 Application to Searle (§8)

- **Searle 1990 (the wall and WordStar).** Chalmers' verdict: "this argument fails... it is almost certain that the wall states will not satisfy the relevant strong state-transition conditionals, as the wall will not possess the requisite causal organization. Implementation is a determinate matter" (p. 331).
- **The concession.** A *weak* observer-relativity is granted: every system implements a trivial 1-state CSA, most a 2-state CSA, so an observer may choose among descriptions. "The vacuity problem would arise only if every system implemented every CSA, and that is not the case" (p. 331). He expects the brain to implement many automata depending on the part and level of detail considered.
- **Searle 1980 (the Chinese Room).** Chalmers' response is structural: two distinct CSAs are implemented in the room — one by the human's brain, one by "the causal organization between the marks on paper", which carry "counterfactual-supporting state-transition relations between the marks on paper, just as there are between silicon chips or neurons". Expect two minds (p. 332). This motivates ascribing mental properties to the ⟨system, CSA⟩ **pair** rather than to the system alone (p. 332).

#### 2.10 What the argument is meant to establish, and what it explicitly is not

> "This is only half the story, of course, and the easy half. The harder part is to take advantage of this bridge, showing that the physical properties that a computational description fixes are the properties in virtue of which minds arise. It is not implausible that minds arise in virtue of causal organization, but neither is it obvious." (pp. 332–33)

The harder half is deferred to Chalmers (1994b), "A Computational Foundation for the Study of Cognition" — entry 5 of this batch.

He also flags, in n. 3, a challenge he does not answer: **Maudlin (1989)** questions whether counterfactual-involving conditionals can be relevant to conscious experience — if you block a connection supporting a counterfactual in a currently static part of the system, could that plausibly remove the system's experience? "Maudlin suggests that it could not", and builds an argument that cognition is not based in computation. Chalmers: "This argument requires an in-depth treatment in its own right."

## 2. Shagrir (2006) — Why We View the Brain as a Computer [tier: short]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Shagrir 2006 - Why we view the brain as a computer.pdf` — the published *Synthese* article, 153: 393–416 (Springer, 2006). **PDF version: published.**

**Claim.** Shagrir advances "two radical claims" (p. 393). First, **being a computer is a matter of perspective**: everything can be conceived as a computer, so there is no fact of the matter, discoverable in the physics, that separates computers from non-computers. Second — and this is what stops the first claim from trivialising the science — **representational content plays an essential role in the individuation of states and processes into computational types**. Putting these together: we call the brain a computer because we want to explain a certain kind of regularity, and only computational theories can explain that kind.

**Method or type.** Conceptual analysis anchored in a single detailed case study from computational neuroscience, plus a seven-item objections-and-replies section. The case study is Shadmehr and Wise's *The Computational Neurobiology of Reaching and Pointing* (2005), Part II, on how the central nervous system computes the vector difference between the hand's location and a target's. Shagrir works through its three sub-problems — hand location from joint angles ("forward kinematics"), target location from eye orientation plus the target's position on the retina, and the difference vector $X_{dv} = X_t - X_{ee}$ — and through the Zipser–Andersen (1988) three-layer network that Shadmehr and Wise build on, itself constrained by the electrophysiology of Andersen, Essick and Siegel (1985).

**Key result or argument.**

- **§1, the negative half.** Every standard criterion for being a computer fails to separate a brain from a washing machine. *No computation without representation* (Fodor 1981; Pylyshyn 1984) fails whether representation is restricted to observer-independent content (chess machines have observer-dependent content; stomachs carry natural information about what one ate) or to combinatorial symbol systems (analog computers and connectionist models have neither). Fodor's *formality condition* fails on all three of its readings — mechanicalness, abstractness, algorithmicity — because "planetary movements, digestive processes and wash cycles" satisfy each (pp. 396–98). On algorithmicity he cites Putnam (1988) and Searle (1992), adding his own weaker but sufficient point: "even under the stricter understandings of implementation, very simple physical devices simultaneously implement very different automata" (p. 398).
- **The line he will not cross.** He endorses Churchland, Koch and Sejnowski's "interest-relative component" (1990, p. 48) but rejects Searle's conclusion that "computation is not discovered in the physics" and that computational cognitive science could never be a natural science (Searle 1992, pp. 208, 212, 225). His reply: "True, we do not discover that the brain is a computer, but decide to so describe it. But it does not follow, without additional assumptions, that there is nothing here to discover" (p. 399) — what the brain represents, and which operations it performs over those representations, remain empirical discoveries.
- **§2, the coinage: semantic task.** The problems computational neuroscience poses are input–output regularities "specified in terms of the representational content of the arguments (inputs) and values (outputs)" (p. 403). We take the computational approach to brains and not to planets because the tasks we want to explain in brains are content-specified and those in planets are not. Crucially, the content ascription is *prior* to the computational modelling: the electrophysiologists already called them "eye-position cells" before anyone built a network.
- **§3, the positive thesis.** Rejecting three rival explanations of computational explanatory force — multiple realizability (Fodor 1974; Putnam 1975), Fodor's truth-preservation, and mere mathematical abstraction — Shagrir locates the force in **correspondence**: a computational theory explains why content $G$ rather than something else comes out of content $F$ "by pointing to correspondence relations between mathematical or formal properties of the representing states — what we call computational structure — and mathematical or formal properties of the represented states" (p. 409). His clearest illustration is Marr and Hildreth's edge detector, the zero crossings of $(\nabla^2 G) * I(x,y)$: "Had the reflection laws been very different, the mathematical formula would still have described the same cellular activity, but the cells would no longer carry information about object boundaries" (pp. 409–10).
- **§4, the individuation argument.** His fictional **brown–cow cell** simultaneously implements an AND-gate and an OR-gate, depending on where the voltage threshold is drawn — "by means of the very same electrical activity, two different formal structures" (p. 411). Only content picks AND as *the* computational structure of a brown-cow detector, because AND corresponds to set intersection among the represented objects. He restricts the claim carefully: only **mathematical content** (Egan 1995) or **formal content** (Sher 1996) is doing the individuating, not specific content — a black-dog cell has the same computational identity (p. 412). And he accepts the cost Piccinini presses on him: naturalistic theories of content that appeal to computational properties "become circular", though a non-computational (causal or teleological) theory of content could still naturalise computation-in-the-brain (pp. 413–14).

**Place in the field's history.** This is the descriptive counterweight to the Putnam–Searle–Chalmers implementation debate. Chalmers had tried to answer the triviality challenge by tightening the *implementation* relation; Shagrir concedes the triviality point and relocates the answer in scientific practice and in content. The paper is one of the clearest statements of the **semantic view of computational individuation** — the thesis that what computation a system performs depends on what its states represent — and it explicitly positions itself against "the received view", which accepts no-computation-without-representation while insisting computation is nonetheless individuated non-semantically. That fault line is the subject of Sprevak (2010), entry 4 below, and the target of Coelho Mollo (2018), entry 7. Piccinini appears here as the named opponent on both fronts; his own positive account is entries 3 and 6.

**Relevance.** For the history of computational functionalism the paper matters in two opposite directions at once, and neither is adjudicated here. It *weakens* the doctrine's foundation by granting that being a computer is perspectival — there is no observer-independent fact that the brain, rather than the solar system, computes. But it *rescues* the empirical content of computational neuroscience, and in doing so makes computational individuation depend on representation, which is precisely the dependence a computational theory of mind hoping to explain content in non-semantic terms cannot afford. Shagrir says so outright: that philosophical outlook, in his opinion, "is flawed" (p. 413).

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2006 · philosophy-of-computation
- Question it asked: Why do brain scientists treat the brain, but not the solar system or a washing machine, as a computer — and what gives computational explanations their explanatory force?
- Position in one line: Being a computer is partly a matter of perspective, and we take that perspective when we want to explain a *semantic task* (an input–output regularity specified by the content of its inputs and outputs); computational explanations work because the mathematical structure of the representing states corresponds to mathematical relations among the represented states, which means computational individuation makes essential reference to (mathematical) content.
- Stance on computational functionalism: restricts — it accepts that computational description of the brain is legitimate and empirically contentful, but denies that computational identity is fixed independently of representation, which blocks the use of computation to explain content in non-semantic terms.
- Responds to / builds on: Putnam 1988; Searle 1992; Chalmers 1996 (cited as a refinement of implementation); Scheutz 2001; Fodor 1980, 1981, 1994; Pylyshyn 1984; Churchland, Koch & Sejnowski 1990; Sejnowski, Koch & Churchland 1988; Churchland & Sejnowski 1992; Marr 1982; Shadmehr & Wise 2005; Zipser & Andersen 1988; Andersen, Essick & Siegel 1985; Buneo et al. 2002; Burge 1986; Egan 1995; Sher 1996; Piccinini 2004 and "Computation without representation" (then forthcoming); Shagrir 1997, 1998, 2001.
- Key move: the notion of a **semantic task**, and the **brown–cow cell** — one cell, one pattern of electrical activity, simultaneously an AND-gate and an OR-gate, with content deciding which structure is *the* computational one.
- Evidence type: conceptual analysis built on a case study of published empirical work (no new data).
- Testability: the account is meta-scientific rather than predictive, but it does make a checkable claim about practice — that computational modelling in neuroscience is always applied to tasks whose inputs and outputs have already been given content by prior electrophysiology, and that models earn their explanatory force by exhibiting a correspondence to structure in the represented domain. Both are claims about the published literature that could be surveyed. It also implies a concrete counterfactual, illustrated with Marr's edge detector: change the physics of the represented domain and the same formula ceases to be an explanation.

## 3. Piccinini (2010) — The Mind as Neural Software? [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Piccinini 2010 - The mind as neural software.pdf` — the published article in *Philosophy and Phenomenological Research* LXXXI(2), September 2010, pp. 269–311 (body pp. 269–302, references to p. 311). **PDF version: published** (Wiley Online Library, institutional download). Printed page number = PDF page + 268.

Full title: *The Mind as Neural Software? Understanding Functionalism, Computationalism, and Computational Functionalism.*

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Piccinini opens by declaring what he is *not* doing: "My goal here is not to evaluate their plausibility. My goal is to formulate them and their relationship clearly enough that we can determine which type of evidence is relevant to them" (p. 269). The complaint is that two separate doctrines have been welded together for forty years. Quoting Fodor: there is a "widespread failure to distinguish the computational program in psychology from the functionalist program in metaphysics" (Fodor 2000, p. 104, quoted at p. 269). His running example of the damage is Paul Churchland's "Functionalism at Forty" (2005), which argues that functionalism is false because the brain is a connectionist rather than a classical computing system — an argument that only works if functionalism entails classical computationalism.

The three doctrines, as he will state them at the end (p. 301):

- **Functionalism:** the mind is the functional organization of the brain.
- **Computationalism:** the functional organization of the brain is computational.
- **Computational functionalism:** the mind is the computational organization of the brain — the conjunction of the other two, famously sloganised as *the mind is the software of the brain*.

**Key findings (as argued).**

- The slogan is **ambiguous between a strong and a weak reading**, and the ambiguity is "one source of confusion in this area" (p. 271). Strong: the brain literally stores and executes programs. Weak: the brain's organisation is computational in some broader sense.
- The weak reading, as it came down from Putnam, is nearly **trivial**, because it rests on **pancomputationalism** — "everything is a Probabilistic Automaton [i.e., a kind of Turing machine] under some Description" (Putnam 1967b, p. 31, quoted at p. 274). "If mental states are computational simply because at some level, everything is computational, then computational functionalism tells us nothing specific about the mind" (p. 274).
- The strong reading needs an account of **program execution**, and the received one — the **mapping view** of software implementation — cannot provide it, because it "turns everything into a computer" (p. 279). Cosmologists modelling galaxies with cellular automata would thereby make galaxies hardware running those automata; and since indefinitely many computational descriptions fit any system, the mind would be either indeterminate or "a collection of indefinitely many pieces of software" (p. 280).
- The **semantic view of computation** — that computational states are individuated partly by their content — cannot rescue the mapping view either, and Piccinini rejects it outright, naming Shagrir (2001, 2006) as a recent example. His decisive reason: "computability theorists and computer designers — i.e., those to whom we should defer in individuating computational states — individuate computational states without appealing to their semantic properties" (p. 282).
- The positive proposal is **mechanistic functionalism**: "Functionalism about a system S should be construed as the thesis that S is the functional organization of the mechanism that exhibits S's capacities" (pp. 288–89). Because mechanisms are explicated without any appeal to computation, this formulation does *not* beg the question of computationalism.
- Within mechanisms, computation is a special case: **computing mechanisms** manipulate strings of digits according to a general rule; **computers** are the subset that do so by storing and executing programs. So "explaining a capacity by program execution is not the same as explaining it computationally, which is not the same as explaining it mechanistically" (p. 291).
- The most striking consequence of taking the slogan literally: "the mind is a (stable state of) a component of the brain, in the same sense in which computer program tokens are (stable states of) components of computers" — so even an idle brain "might still have a mind, provided that its programs are still physically present", which he says matches the sense in which sleeping people have minds ("causally quiescent", Armstrong 1981) (p. 297).
- **Functionalism does not entail computationalism, and computationalism does not entail functionalism** (pp. 301–02). It is therefore "a fallacy to attack functionalism by impugning some computationalist hypothesis or another" (p. 301).
- And the final methodological upshot: whether the brain is a computing mechanism "cannot be done by examining intuitions about imaginary scenarios (Block 1978, Searle 1980, Maudlin 1989) — it can only be done by studying the functional organization of the brain empirically" (p. 302).

**Initial takeaway.** This is a clarification paper rather than a partisan one, and its historical effect was to make computational functionalism a *testable* doctrine rather than an a priori one. By separating "the mind is functionally organised" from "that organisation is computational", and by cashing out computation mechanistically rather than by mapping, it relocated the decisive evidence from philosophers' thought experiments to neuroscience. It is also the paper that puts the strongest pressure on Chalmers' notion of computational sufficiency — in a footnote (n. 8, p. 275) rather than in the main text.

### Phase 2: Argument Reconstruction

#### 2.1 The diagnostic premise: two doctrines, welded

- **P1.** Functionalism (the metaphysical claim, descending from Putnam 1960–67 and Fodor 1965–68; "also known as psychofunctionalism", Block 1980, p. 272) and computationalism (the scientific claim) are prima facie logically independent.
- **P2.** But on Putnam's own construal, functional organisations are *specified by computational descriptions* — the machine table of a Turing machine is his paradigm — so functionalism entails computationalism (Putnam 1967b; Block and Fodor 1972, cited p. 283).
- **P3.** And on the Fodor–Cummins tradition, psychological **functional analysis** was assimilated to computational description: "the paradigmatic psychological theory is a list of instructions for producing behavior" (Fodor 1968b, p. 630, quoted at p. 285, n. 19). Piccinini calls the result "the mongrel of functional analysis and computational description" (p. 285).
- **C1.** Hence attacks on computationalism read as attacks on functionalism (Block 1978; Churchland 2005), and Fodor himself came to admit the conflation (2000, p. 104).

#### 2.2 Which analogy is doing the work? (§2)

Piccinini separates three analogies between minds and computers, in increasing strength:

1. **Individuation analogy** (Putnam). Turing machine states are individuated by how they affect and are affected by other states, inputs, and outputs; mental states likewise by their relations to other mental states, stimuli, and behaviour (pp. 272–73). He notes that this analogy *by itself* does not entail computational functionalism — Putnam reached that conclusion later, on independent grounds.
2. **Combinatorial analogy.** Both minds and computing systems manipulate complex combinatorial structures according to recursive rules, and "prima facie, most things cannot manipulate combinatorial structures of arbitrary recursive complexity according to arbitrary recursive rules" (p. 276). Stronger, but still insufficient for the slogan, "because the analogy holds between minds and computing mechanisms in general, and many computing mechanisms (e.g., ordinary Turing machines) don't possess any software in the relevant sense" (p. 276).
3. **Versatility analogy.** Program-controlled computers "can acquire any number of new capacities simply by acquiring and switching between programs" (p. 277). This — and only this — is "the true source of the computational functionalist slogan" (p. 278).

**The history behind the weak reading.** Piccinini reconstructs the chain by which pancomputationalism entered the literature. McCulloch and Pitts (1943) gave a mathematical theory of neurons widely read as showing the brain is a Turing machine without tape; von Neumann then wrote that "anything that can be exhaustively and unambiguously described, anything that can be exhaustively and unambiguously put into words, is ipso facto realizable by a suitable finite neural network" of that type (von Neumann 1951, p. 23, quoted at p. 273). Piccinini's verdict: "It is now clear that von Neumann's statement isn't true, at least under its most natural interpretation... von Neumann's statement implicitly abuses the Church-Turing thesis" (p. 273). The Church–Turing thesis says what is informally computable is Turing-computable; it does not say that everything describable is computable, still less that everything *is* computational.

**The definition that the strong reading needs** (p. 277):

> An explanation by program execution of a capacity $C$ possessed by a system $S$ is a program $P$ for $C$ such that $S$ possesses $C$ because $S$ executes $P$.

#### 2.3 The attack on the received view of software implementation (§3)

**The received view, as stated** (p. 278):

> If there are two descriptions of a system, a physical description and a computational description, and if the computational description maps onto the physical description, then the system is a physical implementation of the computational description and the computational description is the system's software.

Documented with three quotations: Fodor (1968b, p. 638) on programming languages as mappings from physical states to English sentences; Churchland and Sejnowski (1992, p. 62) — "a physical system is a computational system just in case there is an appropriate (revealing) mapping between the system's physical states and the elements of the function computed"; Egan (1992, p. 446) on a realization function $f_g$ mapping equivalence classes of physical features to symbolic features.

**Objection 1 — universality.** Cosmologists model galaxies with cellular automata; the received view therefore makes galaxies "hardware running the relevant cellular automata programs" (p. 279). "If the mind is the software of the brain in the sense in which certain cellular automata are the software of galaxies, then the analogy between minds and computers becomes an analogy between minds and everything else" (p. 279). The strong reading collapses into the weak one.

**Objection 2 — multiplicity.** Indefinitely many cellular automata (different transition rules, time steps, cell sizes) and indefinitely many other formalisms (Turing machines, C++ programs) map onto the same dynamics. "Given the received view of software implementation, it follows that galaxies are running all these programs at once" (p. 280) — and brains likewise, so "we obtain either indeterminacy as to what the mind is, or that the mind is a collection of indefinitely many pieces of software. This is not a promising metaphysics of mind" (p. 280). Scheutz's (2004) amendment — requiring that *all* states and steps of the description map onto the system — improves matters but leaves indefinitely many descriptions standing (n. 14).

**An important separation from Putnam and Searle.** Piccinini is careful that his problem is *not* theirs. Putnam (1988) and Searle (1992) argue that any physical system implements a large number of, or perhaps all, computations by free mapping — his desk implements his web browser. Piccinini's reply, crediting Chrisley 1995, Copeland 1996, Chalmers 1996a, Bontly 1998 and Scheutz 2001, is that those descriptions are **anomalous**:

> "In the case of kosher computational descriptions — the kind normally used in scientific modeling — the work of generating successive descriptions of a system's behavior is done by a computer running an appropriate program (e.g., a weather forecasting program), not by the mapping relation. In the sort of descriptions employed in Putnam and Searle's argument, instead, the descriptive work is done by the mapping relation." (p. 281)

So Putnam's and Searle's problem is dismissed — "not very serious" — while Piccinini's own survives: "everything can be given an indefinite number of *bona fide* computational descriptions" (p. 281).

**Why the semantic view cannot patch it.** The semantic view (computational states individuated partly by content) looks protective: if only a few things have content, only a few things compute, and triviality is avoided. Piccinini gives three reasons it fails (p. 282):

1. Even conjoined with the received view it misses program execution — "Computers (and some automatic looms, for that matter) execute programs whether or not the digits they manipulate have content, and there are mechanisms that perform computations defined over interpreted strings of digits just like those manipulated by computers but do so without executing programs (e.g., many calculators)."
2. It begs the question against computationalists who deny content any individuative role (Stich 1983; Egan 1992, 2003).
3. "Most seriously, the semantic view of computational states is incorrect, because computability theorists and computer designers — i.e., those to whom we should defer in individuating computational states — individuate computational states without appealing to their semantic properties."

**A constraint of adequacy he sets for any replacement.** "The word 'software' was coined to characterize specific systems called 'computers'... We consider the invention of computers in the 1940s a major intellectual breakthrough — the discovery of something new... For all these reasons, a good account of software implementation must draw a principled distinction between computers and other systems" (p. 281). And the fact he presses against the mapping view: "modulo the compositional and functional relations between programs and their components at different levels of description, a computer runs one and only one program at any given time. An expert can actually retrieve the unique program run by a computer and write it down, instruction by instruction" (p. 283).

#### 2.4 The positive account, step 1: mechanistic functionalism (§4)

**Why not the purely causal notion of functional organisation?** It is not question-begging ("prima facie, causal properties are not ipso facto computational") but "too weak to underwrite a theory of mind" — under a liberal reading of input and output it applies to every physical system, since "every physical system may be said to take its state at time $t_0$ as input, go through a series of internal states between $t_0$ and $t_n$, and yield its state at $t_n$ as output" (p. 284).

**The mechanism template** (p. 285):

> A mechanism $M$ with capacities $C$ is a set of spatiotemporal components $A_1, \dots, A_n$, their functions $F$, and $F$'s relevant causal and spatiotemporal relations $R$, such that $M$ possesses $C$ because (i) $M$ contains $A_1, \dots, A_n$, (ii) $A_1, \dots, A_n$ have functions $F$ organized in way $R$, and (iii) $F$, when organized in way $R$, constitute $C$.

Unpacked with the heart: components (heart, arteries), input/output (blood), activity (the heart pumps), spatial relation (attachment), temporal relation (blood enters arteries after leaving the heart), active relation (the pumping causes the entry) (p. 286).

**Notions of function** (from Wimsatt 1972, pp. 4–5, at p. 286): *perspectival* (causal powers relevant on some view of what the system is doing), *evaluative* (contributing to proper functioning), *teleological* (contributing to the goals of the system or its users). Evaluative and teleological are special cases of perspectival.

**Four caveats, of which the third is load-bearing** (pp. 286–88):

1. Differences between authors' accounts of mechanism are irrelevant here; all fit the template under the broad perspectival notion.
2. Several legitimate notions of mechanism may coexist; the choice need not be settled here, but any notion "must be naturalistic".
3. **"Any further explication of the notion of function or mechanism cannot rely on the notion of computation in such a way as to turn all mechanisms into computing mechanisms, on pain of begging the question of computationalism again. Fortunately, computation plays no such role in current explications of these notions"** (p. 287). This is what makes mechanistic functionalism neutral.
4. Teleological function should not be conflated with the *etiological* (evolutionary) account of teleology — which does not apply to artifacts such as computers, and which would ground a theory of mind in evolutionary biology rather than in psychology and neuroscience (p. 288, n. 23).

**The reformulation** (pp. 288–89): "Functionalism about a system $S$ should be construed as the thesis that $S$ is the functional organization of the mechanism that exhibits $S$'s capacities." A consequence for individuation: states are individuated "not only by their relevant causal relations to other states, inputs, and outputs, but also by the component to which they belong and the function performed by that component when it is in that state" — hence, *pace* Putnam, Turing machine states are individuated partly as states of the **active device** rather than the tape (p. 289).

#### 2.5 The positive account, step 2: digits, strings, computing mechanisms, computers (§5)

The hierarchy is: mechanisms ⊃ computing mechanisms ⊃ program-controlled computers.

- **Digit** (p. 292): "a state of a particular that belongs to one and only one of a finite number of relevant types. The digits' types are unambiguously distinguishable (and hence individuated) by the effects they have on the mechanism that manipulates them." Formally: ceteris paribus, substituting one digit for another of the same type yields the same computation type and output string type; substituting one of a different type yields a different computation.
- **String** (p. 292): "a list of permutable digits individuated by the digits' types, their number, and their order within the string."
- **The contrast case, given at length.** Temperatures and bites of food "belong to indefinitely many relevant types", so they are not digits. And even if one could taxonomise bites of food into finitely many types (protein bites, fat bites), "sequences of bites of food would not constitute strings of digits, because digestion — unlike computation — is not precisely sensitive to the order in which an organism bites its food" (p. 293).
- **Computing mechanism** (p. 293): a mechanism that, under normal conditions, produces output strings from input strings "in accordance with a general rule, which applies to all relevant strings and depends on the inputs (and perhaps the internal states) for its application". A genuine random number generator is excluded: it emits strings but not by rule.
- **Computer** (p. 294): a computing mechanism with **processors** (capable of a finite number of primitive operations on fixed-length data strings, with which operation is selected by further strings called **instructions**) and memories. "A list of instructions constitutes a program. The execution of a program's instructions in the relevant order constitutes the execution of the program."

**The claim Piccinini says the literature missed** (p. 295):

> "When the behavior of ordinary computers is explained by program execution, the program is not just a description. The program is also a (stable state of a) physical component of the computer, whose function is to generate the relevant capacity of the computer. Programs are physically present within computers, where they have a function to perform. Somehow, this simple and straightforward point seems to have been almost entirely missed in the philosophical literature."

The named opponent is Cummins: "programs aren't causes but abstract objects or play-by-play accounts" (Cummins 1983, p. 34). Piccinini records in the same footnote that Cummins has since agreed that "stored programs are certainly causes" (personal correspondence), and argues that Cummins' weaker notion is what leads him and his followers "to the paradoxical conclusion that connectionist networks compute by executing algorithms or programs" (n. 32, p. 295).

#### 2.6 Computational functionalism, strong and generalised (§6)

**Strong / literal form** (p. 296): (i) the brain contributes to the production of behaviour by storing and executing programs, and (ii) the mind is constituted by those programs, plus perhaps the states and processes generated by executing them.

Four consequences he draws:

1. It licenses **explanation of mental capacities by program execution** — a mechanistic explanation postulating memory components storing programs and at least one processor manipulating and executing them (pp. 296–97).
2. It **entails multiple realizability** "in the sense in which different tokens of the same type of computer program can run on different kinds of hardware", so mental programs can be studied independently of neural implementation — this is psychology's task under the hypothesis (p. 297). He names Bechtel and Mundale 1999, Shapiro 2000, and Churchland 2005 as the "foes of multiple realizability" whose position this would overturn.
3. **"The biggest surprise"** (p. 297): the mind turns out to be a stable state of a component of the brain, so "even a brain that is not processing any data — analogously to an idle computer, or even a computer that is turned off — might still have a mind, provided that its programs are still physically present". He defends this as matching the sense in which sleeping or unconscious people still have minds — Armstrong's (1981) "causally quiescent" minds.
4. It is therefore **an empirical hypothesis of a third kind**. Philosophers had recognised two empirical questions — can a computer be programmed to exhibit mental capacities (AI), and can mental capacities be explained by program execution (cognitive psychology). Piccinini adds the prior and more fundamental one: is the brain the kind of mechanism that could run mental programs at all? "Assuming that the mind is the software of the brain presupposes that the brain has components of the relevant kinds, with the relevant functional and organizational properties... Whether brains are one kind of mechanism or another can only be determined by studying brains" (p. 298).

**Generalised form** (p. 299): "the mind is (some aspect of) the computational organization of a (computing) mechanism, regardless of whether that mechanism is a program-controlled computer, a connectionist computing mechanism, or any other kind of computing mechanism". This is offered as the formulation that captures authors who resist the stored-program picture — Putnam, Cummins, the Churchlands, Devitt and Sterelny, McCulloch and Pitts — without collapsing into triviality. The stated cost: "Abandoning the strong analogy between minds and computers... produces a loss of explanatory power. The generalized version... can no longer appeal to the flexibility that comes with the ability to acquire, store, modify, and execute different programs" (p. 299).

**What this does to the classic anti-computationalist thought experiments** (pp. 300–01). Block (1978), Searle (1980) and Maudlin (1989) are taken to show that intentionality or consciousness resists computational explanation, and that conclusion was then transferred to functionalism. On Piccinini's separation:

> "The only legitimate conclusion that may be drawn from these thought experiments is that computationalism is insufficient to explain intentionality or consciousness. This does not entail that computationalism explains no mental capacities, nor does it entail that intentionality and consciousness cannot be explained functionally by some process other than computation." (pp. 300–01)

He adds, neutrally, that "the intuitions behind the thought experiments are themselves in dispute. They remain an uncertain basis for reaching consensus on these matters" (p. 301).

#### 2.7 The engagement with Chalmers, in footnote 8

Because it is the only place where Piccinini confronts the doctrine of **computational sufficiency** directly, and because it targets entry 5 of this batch, it is reproduced in reconstruction (p. 275, n. 8).

Chalmers' position, as Piccinini renders it from the unpublished "A Computational Foundation for the Study of Cognition" and personal correspondence: *abstract causal organization* is "the patterns of interaction among the parts of the system, abstracted away from the make-up of individual parts and from the way the causal connections are implemented", yet at "a level fine enough to determine the causation of behavior". Unlike most non-mental properties, all there is to mental properties is abstract causal organisation, and abstract causal organisation is fully and explanatorily capturable computationally. Hence computation suffices for mentation while not sufficing for most other properties.

**Piccinini's dilemma:**

- **Horn 1.** If abstract causal organisation really is fine-grained enough to determine the causation of a system's behaviour, then — contrary to Chalmers' intent — it will capture the causal aspects of *any* property whatsoever, "including digestion, combustion, etc."
- **Horn 2.** If instead it excludes enough information to rule out properties such as digestion and combustion, then — again contrary to Chalmers' intent — "there is no reason to accept that abstract causal organization will capture every aspect of mental properties."
- **Conclusion.** "Either way, the specific connection between mentation and computation is not strengthened."

He does not claim to refute Chalmers, noting he lacks "space for a detailed discussion", and concedes the point is "fair" but says it "makes a difference only insofar as we have good evidence that computation is sufficient for mentation".

## 4. Sprevak (2010) — Computation, Individuation, and the Received View on Representation [tier: short]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Sprevak 2010 - Computation individuation and the received view on representation.pdf` — an **author-typeset postprint**, dated 8 August 2010, whose header reads "Published in *Studies in History and Philosophy of Science* (2010) 41: 260–270", doi `10.1016/j.shpsa.2010.07.008`. **PDF version: accepted manuscript.** It is paginated 1–29 in the author's own layout rather than 260–270, so citations below give the PDF page (`PDF p. n`). The OCR renders digits in an old-style figure encoding; all numerals quoted below were read from context.

**Claim.** Sprevak defends what he calls **the received view**, stated as a single italicised thesis (PDF p. 3):

> **R.** Computation essentially involves representational content

— "where no restriction is placed on the type of representational content involved, e.g. no ban on distal objects serving as content". He attributes the view to philosophers "with views as divergent as" Churchland (1986), Crane (2003), Cummins (1989), Dennett (1971), Fodor (1998), Pylyshyn (1984) and Searle (1992) (PDF p. 1, n. 1). The paper's target is the pair of attacks on R by Frances Egan (computation is purely mathematical) and Gualtiero Piccinini (computation is mechanistic — "Computation without representation", 2008). Sprevak engages Egan in detail and says a full treatment of "Piccinini's sophisticated position has to wait until another occasion" (PDF p. 2).

Two framing decisions matter for how the debate is read afterwards. First, he deliberately phrases everything in terms of *representational* rather than *intentional* content — "a representation need support no more than a basic notion of aboutness or reference" (PDF p. 4) — so as not to pre-judge whether computation requires cognitive agents, modes of presentation, or propositional structure. Second, he insists that "the basic units of physical computation are computational processes, and individuation of physical computational states is parasitic on the individuation of computational processes", and argues that "excessive focus on computational states has led to the unjustified assumption that physical computations could only essentially involve narrow content" (PDF p. 3).

**Method or type.** Conceptual analysis in two movements: three arguments against R rebutted (§2), then three positive arguments for R (§3). No new formalism and no empirical material; the illustrative cases are Marr's theory of vision, Turing's human clerk, and a two-input electrical circuit.

**Key result or argument.**

- **Dynamics is not individuation** (§2.1). The familiar argument — computational transitions are driven by syntactic "shape", not by content, so content is inessential — misidentifies the dependence at issue. "An advocate of R does not claim that the *causal dynamics* of computations depend on representational content. Her claim is that the *individuation* of computations depends on their representational content... The battleground for the received view are the facts about the individuation of computations, not the facts about their dynamics" (PDF p. 5). He adds that treating semantic properties as causally idle because syntax "does the causal work" commits one, via Kim-style exclusion reasoning, to a strong reductive materialism in which only bottom-level physics is causally relevant.
- **Egan on Marr, and the universal-realization problem** (§2.2). Egan (1995) reads Marr's computational level as purely function-theoretic — the retina computes $\nabla^2 G * I$ — and hence non-representational. Sprevak's reply proceeds in two steps. First, mathematical computation theory is pure mathematics: a Turing machine is a quintuple $M = (Q, \Sigma, \Gamma, \delta, q_0)$, its inputs and outputs are "not empirical ink-marks. They are mathematical entities" (PDF p. 8), and the theory "does not say anything about physical systems" — so Marr's computational level cannot simply *be* that theory; something must connect the abstract description to the retina. Second, Egan's candidate connector, the non-semantic **realization function** $f_R$ (quoted from Egan 1992, p. 446), is too cheap: "If the mere existence of a realization function is sufficient for a physical system to perform a computation, then almost every physical system performs every computation" (PDF p. 10) — citing Searle's brick wall (1992), Putnam's single particle (1988), and noting that "Chalmers (1996) argues that, with some qualifications, the transitions involved can also support counterfactuals" given a clock and a dial. He then closes off two ways of restricting which realization functions count: they cannot be excluded for being "too disjunctive" (the voltage-to-character mappings in a PC are disjunctive and unnatural yet perfectly genuine), nor for requiring too much epistemic work to construct (that would make computation anti-realist, "entirely a function of the epistemic powers and interests of cognitive agents", PDF p. 11). The remaining answer is representation: "Certain realization functions are special because they truly describe representation relations in the world" (PDF p. 11). Finally he notes that Egan's later position adds an **interpretation function** $f_I$ assigning mathematical content — and presses that this leaves $f_R$ idle: "All the work in establishing computational identity is done by the representational mapping, $f_I$, with nothing left over for $f_R$ to do" (PDF p. 12).
- **The narrow-content dilemma dissolved** (§2.3). Egan's second argument is that accepting R commits computational psychology to narrow content, which is either unavailable or a bad fit with a practice that ascribes broad, environment-specific contents. Sprevak rejects the premise that only narrow content is available. Against Egan's adaptation argument — that computational description must be environment-independent so we can ask whether a mechanism is well or ill adapted elsewhere (Egan 1994, p. 264, quoted at PDF p. 14) — he offers two strategies that can be used alternately: judge the *physical system* implementing the computation for adaptedness, or *stipulate* a shared representational content as part of the counterfactual supposition, since "representational conventions are easy to set up: they can be created by stipulation" (PDF p. 15). Burge's (1986) crack-detecting and shadow-detecting duplicates are used to show that both judgements — that the same computation is adaptive in both environments, and that each is adaptive only in its own — are compelling, and that the two strategies accommodate both. Against Fodor's **formality condition**, he distinguishes two versions (PDF p. 20):
  - **F1.** Any difference in representational content between computational states should be mirrored in a difference in their formal structure.
  - **F2.** For a computational process $P$, all differences in representational content *to which $P$ appears to be sensitive* should be mirrored in a formal difference between $P$'s states.

  F2 is enough to explain apparent semantic sensitivity and is compatible with broad content. Fodor endorses F1, Sprevak argues, only because he uses the wrong comparison class — all possible computational states in any environment, rather than the tokens that could occur in a state's own "owner" process. The consistency worry (what stops a water-processor being hijacked by a physically identical *twater* token?) is answered by noting that "a given computational process occurs in a particular environment, and throughout that process, the environment fixes unambiguous broad content for the tokens" (PDF p. 19). Upshot: "contra Fodor and Egan, computational psychology need not be individualistic."
- **Three positive arguments for R** (§3).
  1. *Paradigm cases involve representation.* Turing's mindless clerk maps ink-marks that represent numerals or numbers; an electronic computer's signals represent 0s and 1s, and also an e-mail's text or a picture. "It is also worth observing the marked lack of paradigmatic cases of computation without representational content" (PDF p. 22).
  2. *Input/output equivalence requires it.* Two computationally I/O-equivalent systems may share no physical or functional properties — one silicon taking electrical signals, one "tin cans and string" taking marbles; or a Roman-numeral adder and an Arabic-numeral adder. "The only thing that two physically diverse inputs and outputs have in common is that they represent the same thing" (PDF p. 23). The same appeal also recovers the intuition his opponents claim: two intrinsic duplicates, one calculating chess moves and one stock-market predictions, compute different functions *and* feel I/O equivalent — the second judgement arising because physical identity "prompts us to assign a common representational content in addition to whatever other content they may possess" (PDF p. 24).
  3. *AND versus OR.* A circuit that outputs 5 V if and only if both inputs are 5 V implements AND if 5 V represents 1 and 0 V represents 0, and OR under the reverse assignment. "No physical, structural, or functional property decides whether 5 V should be paired with 1... The situation is symmetrical with respect to both assignments" (PDF p. 24). Embedding the unit in a larger system does not settle it, since one could consistently adopt a mixed convention — AND on the left of the machine, OR on the right. "The difference between an implementation of an AND gate and an OR gate is a difference in representational content" (PDF p. 25).
- **What he grants is still missing** (§4). R is necessary, not sufficient: "not every process that consistently maps representations to representations is a computation. A computation should also, in a sense yet to be defined, be **mechanical**: there should be a counterfactually robust inter-linked series of steps in how it achieves its mapping of representational content" (PDF p. 27). And sameness of computation requires more than I/O equivalence — the mapping must be achieved "in the same way".

**Place in the field's history.** This is the counter-attack in the individuation debate, and it defines the positions cleanly enough that later work (including Coelho Mollo 2018, entry 7) takes its statement of the problem as the starting point. Sprevak, Shagrir and Piccinini form a triangle: Shagrir (entry 2) argues that computational individuation appeals to *mathematical* content; Piccinini (entries 3 and 6) argues it appeals to no content at all; Sprevak argues it appeals to content of **any** type, including distal and broad. His AND/OR example is the mirror image of Shagrir's brown–cow cell — both are single circuits with two candidate computational identities settled by content — and the two were written independently within four years of each other. Note also that Sprevak treats Chalmers (1996), entry 1 of this batch, not as the solution to universal realization but as a demonstration of how cheaply the counterfactual requirement can be met: "Chalmers (1996)'s argument requires that a physical system possess a 'clock' and a 'dial', but these conditions are easily satisfied" (PDF p. 10, n. 9). That is a reading of Chalmers' own §4 concession, not of his conclusion.

**Relevance.** For computational functionalism the stakes are direct. If Sprevak is right, the computations a system performs are fixed in part by what its states represent, and representational content is often broad — fixed by the environment the system is embedded in. Then two intrinsic physical duplicates in different environments do not perform the same computation, and "running the same computation" cannot be read off the system in isolation. Every claim in this literature of the form "any system running computation C has mind M" would need to specify which contents are in play. The paper does not itself draw that conclusion; it is stated here as the connection a historian of the field would want flagged.

- Testability: nothing empirical is proposed; the paper's claims are about individuation conditions. The nearest thing to a checkable prediction is an observation about practice — that there are no paradigm cases of computation without representational content, and that computational psychology as actually practised ascribes both narrow content (blobs, zero-crossings) and broad content (surfaces, shadows, cracks), which could be verified by survey of the literature.
- PDF version: accepted manuscript

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** The paper sets out to justify, rather than merely defend, two theses it says underwrite cognitive science and artificial intelligence (PDF p. 1):

- **Computational sufficiency:** "the right kind of computational structure suffices for the possession of a mind, and for the possession of a wide variety of mental properties."
- **Computational explanation:** "computation provides a general framework for the explanation of cognitive processes and of behavior."

Chalmers observes that the positive case for these has always been thin: "Advocates of computational cognitive science have done their best to repel these negative critiques, but the positive justification for the foundational theses remains murky at best. Why should computation, rather than some other technical notion, play this foundational role?" (PDF p. 2).

The answer comes in two moves. First, an account of **implementation**: a physical system implements a computation when its causal structure mirrors the formal structure of the computation. Second, the observation that this makes a computation "simply an abstract specification of causal organization" (PDF p. 5) — after which the argument turns on showing that mental properties depend only on causal organisation.

**Key findings (as argued).**

- Implementation is objective and non-trivial. Every system implements *some* computation (a one-state automaton, say), but no system implements every computation. The paper gives a quantitative estimate: for a combinatorial-state automaton with 1000-element state vectors and 10 values per element, there are at least $10^{1000}$ transition constraints each selecting one of at least $10^{1000}$ consequents, so "the chance that an arbitrary set of states will satisfy these constraints is something less than one in $(10^{1000})^{10^{1000}}$" (PDF p. 5).
- Implementation is **syntactic, not semantic**, by design: "computations are specified syntactically, not semantically" (PDF p. 6). Building representational content into implementation conditions would ground computation on a notion "so ill-understood that it desperately needs a foundation itself".
- The bridging notion is **causal topology** — "the pattern of interaction among parts of the system, abstracted away from the make-up of individual parts and from the way the causal connections are implemented" (PDF p. 7). A property is an **organizational invariant** if preserved by every change that preserves causal topology.
- Most mental properties are organizational invariants. Psychological properties follow from their causal-role definitions (Armstrong 1968; Lewis 1972). Phenomenal properties are argued for by the **Dancing Qualia** reductio: if experience were not organizationally invariant, a silicon back-up circuit with a switch would let experiences flip between red and blue while the system, by hypothesis unchanged in functional state, could never notice (PDF pp. 8–9).
- "It turns out that the CSA formalism provides a perfect formalization of the notion of causal topology" (PDF p. 10). With that, computational sufficiency follows — in Chalmers' words, it "establishes... the view that Searle has called 'strong artificial intelligence': that there exists some computation such that any implementation of the computation possesses mentality" (PDF p. 10).
- The version defended is deliberately weak: **minimal computationalism**, which does *not* claim that the brain is a Turing machine, a von Neumann machine, a computer, or a symbol manipulator, and is compatible with connectionism, dynamical systems, artificial life and even Gibsonian perception.
- **The 2011 footnote changes the modality of the central thesis.** To square it with *The Conscious Mind* (1996b), computational sufficiency and organizational invariance "must be understood in terms of nomological rather metaphysical necessity... These claims are compatible with the metaphysical possibility of systems with the same organization and no consciousness" (note 6).

**Initial takeaway.** This is the canonical positive statement of computational functionalism in its "abstract causal organization" form, and it is unusually explicit about what it is *not* claiming. Its enduring contributions to the field's vocabulary are three: the mirroring account of implementation, **causal topology** / **organizational invariance**, and the label **minimal computationalism**. Its enduring point of attack, correspondingly, is the claim in §3.2 that phenomenal properties are organizational invariants — which the paper supports by thought experiment and by reference to a companion paper, not by evidence.

### Phase 2: Argument Reconstruction

#### 2.1 The theory of implementation (§2)

**Short form** (PDF p. 2):

> A physical system implements a given computation when the causal structure of the physical system mirrors the formal structure of the computation.

**Expanded form** (PDF p. 2):

> A physical system implements a given computation when there exists a grouping of physical states of the system into state-types and a one-to-one mapping from formal states of the computation to physical state-types, such that formal states related by an abstract state-transition relation are mapped onto physical state-types related by a corresponding causal state-transition relation.

**For finite-state automata.** An FSA is given by input states $I_1,\dots,I_k$, internal states $S_1,\dots,S_m$, output states $O_1,\dots,O_n$, and transitions of the form $(S,I) \to (S',O')$ for each internal-state/input pair. Then (PDF p. 3):

> A physical system $P$ implements an FSA $M$ if there is a mapping $f$ that maps internal states of $P$ to internal states of $M$, inputs to $P$ to input states of $M$, and outputs of $P$ to output states of $M$, such that: for every state-transition relation $(S,I) \to (S',O')$ of $M$, the following conditional holds: if $P$ is in internal state $s$ and receiving input $i$ where $f(s)=S$ and $f(i)=I$, this **reliably causes** it to enter internal state $s'$ and produce output $o'$ such that $f(s')=S'$ and $f(o')=O'$.

The word doing the work is *reliably*: it is where the counterfactual requirement of the 1996 paper enters this formulation. The grouped state-types of the short form are recoverable as $\{s \mid f(s) = S_i\}$ for each $S_i \in M$.

**For combinatorial-state automata (§2.1).** An internal state is a vector $[S^1, S^2, S^3, \dots]$, each element taking one of finitely many values $S^i_j$ ("substates"); inputs $[I^1,\dots,I^k]$ and outputs may be structured likewise; transition rules specify, for each element of the state vector, a function giving its new value from the old overall state and input vectors. Internal state vectors may be finite or infinite — the infinite case needs "restrictions... so that these do not encode an infinite amount of information" (PDF p. 3). Implementation (PDF p. 4):

> A physical system $P$ implements a CSA $M$ if there is a vectorization of internal states of $P$ into components $[s^1, s^2, \dots]$, and a mapping $f$ from the substates $s^j$ into corresponding substates $S^j$ of $M$, along with similar vectorizations and mappings for inputs and outputs, such that for every state-transition rule $([I^1,\dots,I^k],[S^1,S^2,\dots]) \to ([S'^1,S'^2,\dots],[O^1,\dots,O^l])$ of $M$: if $P$ is in internal state $[s^1,s^2,\dots]$ and receiving input $[i^1,\dots,i^n]$ which map to formal state and input $[S^1,S^2,\dots]$ and $[I^1,\dots,I^k]$ respectively, this reliably causes it to enter an internal state and produce an output that map to $[S'^1,S'^2,\dots]$ and $[O^1,\dots,O^l]$ respectively.

The vectorization carries a stipulation, stated slightly more softly here than in the 1996 paper: "A natural requirement for such a 'vectorization' is that each element correspond to a distinct physical region within the system, although there may be other alternatives" (PDF pp. 3–4).

**Four reasons CSAs are preferred to FSAs** (PDF p. 4), given that finite CSAs are no more computationally powerful: (i) implementation conditions are far more constrained, capturing causal organisation "to a much finer grain"; (ii) structured states have explanatory utility; (iii) CSAs directly reflect the organisation of Turing machines and cellular automata; (iv) a unified account covers finite and infinite machines. A worked translation for Turing machines is given: the overall state is a vector of the head's internal state plus, for each tape square, an ordered pair of symbol and an occupancy flag.

#### 2.2 Seven questions answered (§2.2)

1. **Does every system implement some computation?** Yes — every physical system implements the one-state FSA, most the two-state cyclic FSA. "This is no problem, and certainly does not render the account vacuous. That would only be the case if every system implemented every computation, and that is not the case" (PDF p. 5).
2. **Does every system implement any given computation?** No, and this is where the combinatorial argument against Searle's wall is made. "What is required is not just a mapping from states of the system onto states of the CSA, as Searle (1990) effectively suggests. The added requirement that the mapped states must satisfy reliable state-transition rules is what does all the work" (PDF p. 5). He concedes the limit of the argument: "while we lack knowledge of the fundamental constituents of matter, it is impossible to prove that arbitrary objects do not implement every computation (perhaps every proton has an infinitely rich internal structure), but anybody who denies this conclusion will need to come up with a remarkably strong argument."
3. **Can one system implement more than one computation?** Yes. "We might say that within every physical system, there are numerous computational systems. To this very limited extent, the notion of implementation is 'interest-relative'... [but] the question of whether a given system implements a given computation is still entirely objective" (PDF p. 5).
4. **If even digestion is a computation, isn't this vacuous?** No, and the answer turns on the phrase *in virtue of*. Any instance of digestion implements some computation, "but the system's implementing this computation is in general irrelevant to its being an instance of digestion" — the same computation could run on a SPARC without digesting anything. "With cognition, by contrast, the claim is that it is *in virtue of* implementing some computation that a system is cognitive" (PDF pp. 5–6). Chalmers is explicit that this is what has to be argued, and that "if this relation or something like it did not hold, the computational status of cognition would be analogous to that of digestion".
5. **What about Putnam's argument?** The compressed version of entry 1: Putnam requires only weak material conditionals, whereas implementation requires "a reliable, counterfactual-supporting connection between the states", satisfied "for every transition in the machine table, not just for those whose antecedent states happen to come up in a given time period" (PDF p. 6). Two parenthetical notes: Putnam's own reply addresses a *different* class of counterfactuals ("if the system had not been in state A, it would not have transited to state B"); and "it turns out that Putnam's argument for the widespread realization of inputless FSAs can be patched up in a certain way; this just goes to show that inputless FSAs are an inappropriate formalism for cognitive science, due to their complete lack of combinatorial structure", while the input/output version "cannot be patched up in an analogous way".
6. **What about semantics?** The deliberate exclusion, quoted in full because it is the point Shagrir and Sprevak contest: "nothing in my account of computation and implementation invokes any semantic considerations, such as the representational content of internal states. This is precisely as it should be: computations are specified syntactically, not semantically. Although it may very well be the case that any implementations of a given computation share some kind of semantic content, this should be a consequence of an account of computation and implementation, rather than built into the definition. If we build semantic considerations into the conditions for implementation, any role that computation can play in providing a foundation for AI and cognitive science will be endangered, as the notion of semantic content is so ill-understood that it desperately needs a foundation itself" (PDF p. 6). Supporting points: Turing (1936) built in no semantic constraints; some Turing machines "defined by random sets of state-transition quintuples" support no non-trivial interpretation at all, and we still need an account of implementing those; computer designers secure implementation by getting the causal organisation right. He endorses Haugeland's slogan, "if you take care of the syntax, the semantics will take care of itself" (Haugeland 1985), and proposes that computation and content should both rest on the common notion of **causation** (citing Dretske 1981, Fodor 1987), so that "the two notions will not sway independently, while at the same time ensuring that neither is dependent on the other for its analysis" (PDF p. 7).
7. **What about computers?** Computation, not computers, is fundamental. A computer must be *programmable*; universality is optional. On the brain: "Arguably... In any case, the question is quite unimportant for our purposes. What counts is that the brain implements various complex computations, not that it is a computer" (PDF p. 7).

#### 2.3 Causal topology and organizational invariance (§§3.1–3.2)

**Why not the Turing test?** Chalmers rejects the usual behavioural route to computational sufficiency before starting: behaviour may be produced by systems lacking mentality (the giant lookup tables of Block 1981), and "the demise of logical behaviorism has made it very implausible that it suffices for *specific* mental properties: two mentally distinct systems can have the same behavioral dispositions" (PDF p. 7).

**Causal topology** (PDF p. 7): "the pattern of interaction among parts of the system, abstracted away from the make-up of individual parts and from the way the causal connections are implemented... a dynamic topology analogous to the static topology of a graph or a network." Systems have causal topology at many levels; the relevant level for cognition is "fine enough to determine the causation of behavior", for the brain "probably the neural level or higher". He flags that the notion "is necessarily informal for now".

**Organizational invariant:** a property $P$ invariant under any change preserving causal topology. The five admitted change types (PDF pp. 7–8): (a) moving the system in space; (b) stretching, distorting, expanding, contracting; (c) replacing sufficiently small parts with parts performing the same local function — "e.g. replacing a neuron with a silicon chip with the same I/O properties"; (d) replacing causal links with others preserving the same dependencies — "we might replace a mechanical link in a telephone exchange with an electrical link"; (e) any other change not altering the pattern of causal interaction.

**The contrast cases** are chosen to be exactly the ones opponents use. Flying is not an organizational invariant (move the plane to the ground). Digestion is not (replace the parts with metal preserving causal patterns: "no food groups will be broken down, no energy will be extracted"). Being a tube of toothpaste is not (deform the tube, or swap toothpaste for peanut butter).

**The central claim** (§3.2): most mental properties *are* organizational invariants, with an explicit exception for properties partly supervenient on the environment — knowledge, and belief on some construals. For those, "causal topology will contribute as much as the brain contributes. It is just that the environment will also play a role" (PDF p. 8).

The claim is then split:

- **Psychological properties** (belief, learning, perception — "characterized by their causal role"): these follow from Lewis's (1972) analysis, since same causal topology implies same patterns of causal interaction among states (PDF p. 9).
- **Phenomenal properties** ("characterized by what it is like to have them", Nagel 1974): these are harder, and Chalmers concedes "it seems unlikely that these can be defined by their causal roles... Phenomenal properties are still quite mysterious and ill-understood."

**The Dancing Qualia reductio, step by step** (PDF pp. 9, as given):

1. *Assume* conscious experience is not organizationally invariant.
2. Then there exist systems with the same causal topology but different conscious experiences — say because one is neural and one silicon.
3. Since they share causal topology, one can in principle be transformed into the other by gradual changes, replacing neurons one at a time with I/O-equivalent silicon chips.
4. Along that spectrum there must be two systems, $N$ and $S$, differing in less than ten percent of the system, whose conscious experiences differ.
5. **The key step:** take the relevant neural circuit in $N$ and "install alongside it a causally isomorphic silicon back-up circuit, with a switch between the two circuits."
6. Flipping the switch changes the experience by hypothesis — "from a bright red experience to a bright blue experience".
7. But the causal topology is constant, so all functional states and behavioural dispositions are fixed. "If noticing is defined functionally (as it should be), then there is no room for any noticing to take place... There is certainly no room for a thought 'Hmm! Something strange just happened!', unless it is floating free in some Cartesian realm." Even such a thought would be "utterly impotent".
8. Flip the switch repeatedly and "red and blue experiences 'dance' before the system's inner eye; it will never notice."
9. *Reductio*: "if one's experiences change, one can potentially notice in a way that makes some causal difference." So the assumption is false.

He adds "This needs to be worked out in more detail, of course", deferring to Chalmers (1995) for the full Dancing Qualia and Fading Qualia arguments. Note 3 engages Searle (1991), who suggests a silicon-replaced subject might find himself thinking "I can't see anything. I'm going totally blind" while his voice says "I see a red object in front of me" — Chalmers replies that it is "very unclear where there is room for such 'wanting' to take place, if it is not in some Cartesian realm", and that Searle's alternative suggestions (reduction to total paralysis) "require a change in causal topology and are therefore not relevant".

#### 2.4 Justifying the two theses (§3.3)

The step from organizational invariance to computation is short and is given in one paragraph (PDF p. 10). An organizationally invariant property depends only on a pattern of causal interaction between parts; that pattern can be abstracted into a CSA description, "the parts of the system will correspond to elements of the CSA state-vector, and the patterns of interaction will be expressed in the state-transition rules". The requirement is that "each part has only a finite number of states that are relevant to the causal dependencies between parts, which is likely to be the case in any biological system whose functions cannot realistically depend on infinite precision." Then:

> "In fact, it turns out that the CSA formalism provides a perfect formalization of the notion of causal topology. A CSA description specifies a division of a system into parts, a space of states for each part, and a pattern of interaction between these states. This is precisely what is constitutive of causal topology."

Therefore computational sufficiency holds, and with it Searle's "strong artificial intelligence". Computational explanation follows by the same route, with an important qualification: "the thesis of computational explanation is not an exclusive thesis" — neural and environmental properties may be invoked, but "insofar as neural properties are explanatorily relevant, it is in virtue of the role they play in determining a system's causal organization" (PDF p. 10).

#### 2.5 The seven objections and the replies (§3.4)

Chalmers sorts objections into two families: those saying computation cannot reproduce cognitive *capacities*, and those conceding the capacities but denying that they suffice for mentality. He takes four of the second kind, then three of the first.

| Objection | Source given | Reply |
|---|---|---|
| "A computational model is just a simulation" — we don't expect a model hurricane to be a hurricane | Searle 1980; Harnad 1989 | Turns entirely on organizational invariance. "A computational simulation is not a mere formal abstraction, but has rich internal dynamics of its own." Hurricanes, digestion, temperature are not organizational invariants — specific physical elements are definitional; cognition arguably is. Hence "a model that is computationally equivalent to a mind will itself be a mind" (PDF p. 11). |
| Syntax is not sufficient for semantics; programs are syntactic; minds have semantics | Searle 1984 | The argument "equivocates between programs and implementations". "It is the program that is syntactic; it is the implementation that has semantic content." Implementations are "real physical systems with complex causal organization, with real physical causation going on inside... circuits and voltages push each other around in a manner analogous to that in which neurons and activations push each other around". Conclusion: "Syntax may not be sufficient for semantics, but the right kind of causation is" (PDF p. 11). |
| The Chinese Room | Searle 1980 | Supports the **Systems Reply**. Gradually replace neurons with demons doing the same causal work, then merge neighbouring demons until one demon maintains the whole organisation — possibly inside its own head by memorisation. The organisation is "implemented in a baroque way, but we should not let the baroqueness blind us". The demon is "simply a kind of causal facilitator"; we should not expect it to have the experiences "any more than it will share the experiences of an ant that crawls inside its skull: both are cases of two computational systems being implemented within a single physical space" (PDF pp. 11–12). |
| What about the environment? | — | Computational organisation gives only the internal contribution — "the so-called 'narrow content' of a belief, if this exists; see Fodor 1987". "The full panoply of mental properties might only be determined by computation-plus-environment, just as it is determined by brain-plus-environment", which is no worse a position than neuroscience's (PDF p. 12). |
| Is cognition computable? | Dreyfus 1974; Penrose 1989 | "To some extent an empirical issue, but the relevant evidence is solidly on the side of computability." If the low-level laws of physics are computable, neurophysiological processes can be simulated, and so can the whole brain. Penrose's quantum-gravity speculation "offers no arguments to back up this speculation" (PDF p. 12; cf. Pour-El & Richards 1989). |
| Gödel's theorem | Lucas 1963; Penrose 1989 | Not answered in detail, since "the answer to it is not a direct application of the current framework". The note given: seeing the truth of arbitrary Gödel sentences "requires that we have the ability to determine the consistency or inconsistency of any given formal system, and there is no reason to believe that we have this ability in general" (PDF p. 12). |
| Discreteness and continuity — cognition may depend on analog values or chaotic dependencies | — | Three responses. (i) Extend CSAs to real-valued substates with restricted transitions (Blum, Shub & Smale 1989). (ii) Replace discrete transitions with differential equations for a fully continuous framework (MacLennan 1990); whether that "truly qualifies as computational is largely a terminological matter". (iii) Most importantly, a discrete abstraction approximates a continuous process to any accuracy, and "it is implausible that the correct functioning of mental processes depends on the precise value of the tenth decimal place of analog quantities", since background noise would wash it out. Indeed noise implies continuous computations "can never be reliably implemented in practice, but only approximately implemented" (PDF pp. 12–13). |

#### 2.6 What computationalism is *not* committed to (§4)

This section is where the paper does its clearing, and it is unusually generous to opponents.

- **Not a literal Turing machine.** Edelman (1989, p. 30) is quoted at length, arguing that individual variation in brain structure makes it "highly unlikely that they could be Turing machines" and that the world cannot "function as a tape". Chalmers: the fields "are not committed to the claim that the brain is literally a Turing machine with a moving head and a tape, and even less to the claim that that tape is the environment" (PDF p. 14).
- **Not a von Neumann architecture** — "far ahead of any empirical evidence and is most likely false."
- **Not rule-following** — "It is entirely possible that the only 'rules' found in a computational description of thought will be at a very low level."
- **Not "the brain is a computer"** — "it is not computers that are central but computations".
- **Not symbolic computationalism** (Newell & Simon 1976; Fodor & Pylyshyn 1988), which he glosses as the claim "that the computational primitives in a computational description of cognition are also representational primitives". Connectionist systems, by contrast, perform **subsymbolic computation** (Smolensky 1988): "the basic bearers of semantic content are distributed representations, patterns of activity over many units, whereas the computational primitives are simple units that may themselves lack semantic content... the level of computation falls below the level of representation" (PDF p. 14). He stresses that the symbolic/subsymbolic distinction "divides the class of computations within each of these formalisms" rather than separating Turing machines from neural networks.
- **A disagreement with Fodor about Turing.** Fodor (1992) makes Turing's main contribution the idea that syntactic transitions can respect semantic content. "This strikes me as false. Turing was concerned very little with the semantic content of internal states, and the concentration on symbolic computation came later. Rather, Turing's key contribution was the formalization of the notion of mechanism, along with the associated universality of the formalization" (PDF p. 15). And the methodological point: a semantic constraint on computation "sacrifices the universality that is at the heart of Turing's contribution", since universality applies to syntactically defined classes of automata.
- **His own verdict on the strong forms:** "I suspect that they are all false, but in any case their truth and falsity is not the issue here" (PDF p. 15).

#### 2.7 Minimal computationalism, and how it could be wrong (§5)

> "The view that I have advocated can be called minimal computationalism. It is defined by the twin theses of computational sufficiency and computational explanation, where computation is taken in the broad sense that dates back to Turing." (PDF p. 15)

Three ways it could fail, stated by the author (PDF p. 15):

1. The fundamental laws of physics turn out noncomputable *and* that noncomputability shows up in cognitive functioning.
2. Cognitive capacities turn out to depend essentially on infinite precision in analog quantities.
3. Cognition turns out to be mediated by a non-physical substance whose workings are not computable.

Compatibility is claimed with "connectionism, logicism, and approaches focusing on dynamic systems, evolution, and artificial life", and even with Gibsonian perception: "If perception turns out to work as the Gibsonians imagine, it will still be mediated by causal mechanisms, and the mechanisms will be expressible in an appropriate computational form" (PDF p. 16).

#### 2.8 The 2011 footnote — what the author revised

Listed as note 6, this is the only change to the 1993 text and it weakens the central thesis in a specific way:

> "In order to make them compatible with the views of consciousness in Chalmers 1996b, the thesis of computational sufficiency and the claim that mental properties are organizational invariants must be understood in terms of nomological rather metaphysical necessity: the right kind of computation suffices with nomological necessity for possession of a mind, mental properties supervene nomologically on causal topology. These claims are compatible with the metaphysical possibility of systems with the same organization and no consciousness."

That is: philosophical zombies remain metaphysically possible on the revised reading; computational sufficiency holds as a matter of natural law in this world. The same note qualifies computational explanation: if cognitive processes are taken to include intentional or representational states, "I think these cannot be explained wholly in terms of computation, as I think that phenomenal properties and environmental properties play a role here." The version he says is "most directly supported by the argument in the text" is that "computation provides a general framework for the **mechanistic** explanation of cognitive processes and behavior. That is, insofar as cognitive processes and behavior are explainable mechanistically, they are explainable computationally."

Note 4 also records a scepticism that sits oddly beside the paper's confidence: "I am skeptical about whether phenomenal properties can be explained in wholly physical terms. As I argue in Chalmers 1996b, given any account of the physical or computational processes underlying mentality, the question of why these processes should give rise to conscious experience does not seem to be explainable within physical or computational theory alone. Nevertheless, it remains the case that phenomenal properties depend on physical properties, and if what I have said earlier is correct, the physical properties that they depend on are organizational properties."

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 1993 written, 2011 web version / 2012 journal publication · philosophy-of-mind (with philosophy-of-computation)
- Question it asked: What justifies computation's foundational role in cognitive science — specifically, why should implementing the right computation suffice for having a mind, and why should computation provide a general framework for explaining cognition?
- Position in one line: A physical system implements a computation when its causal structure mirrors the computation's formal structure; a computation is therefore an abstract specification of causal organisation ("causal topology"); mental properties, including phenomenal ones, are organisational invariants; and since any causal topology can be specified as a combinatorial-state automaton, implementing the right computation suffices for a mind and computation provides a general explanatory framework — a position deliberately kept minimal, committed to neither symbols, rules, von Neumann architecture, nor the brain being a computer.
- Stance on computational functionalism: supports — this is the canonical positive statement of the doctrine in its abstract-causal-organisation form, though the 2011 footnote downgrades computational sufficiency from metaphysical to nomological necessity.
- Responds to / builds on: Putnam 1960, 1967, 1988; Searle 1980, 1984, 1990, 1991; Turing 1936; Armstrong 1968; Lewis 1972; Nagel 1974; Block 1981; Dretske 1981; Fodor 1975, 1987, 1992; Fodor & Pylyshyn 1988; Newell & Simon; Pylyshyn 1984; Smolensky 1988; Haugeland 1985; Harnad 1989; Dreyfus 1972/1974; Edelman 1989; Gibson 1979; Lucas 1963; Penrose 1989, 1990; Pour-El & Richards 1989; Bowie 1982; Blum, Shub & Smale 1989; MacLennan 1990; Dietrich 1990; and the author's own Chalmers 1995 (Dancing/Fading Qualia), 1996a (the rock paper), 1996b (*The Conscious Mind*).

## 6. Piccinini & Bahar (2013) — Neural Computation and the Computational Theory of Cognition [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Piccinini and Bahar 2013 - Neural computation and the computational theory of cognition.pdf` — the published article, DOI `10.1111/cogs.12012`; received 29 August 2011, revised 20 March 2012, accepted 24 May 2012. **PDF version: published** (Wiley Online Library, institutional download). The running footer prints "Cognitive Science 34 (2013) 453–488"; page citations below follow that printed pagination (printed page = PDF page + 452). Authors: Gualtiero Piccinini (Department of Philosophy) and Sonya Bahar (Department of Physics and Astronomy), both at the Center for Neurodynamics, University of Missouri–St. Louis — a philosopher-and-physicist collaboration, which is why the paper's evidence base is neurophysiological rather than purely argumentative.

The paper carries two epigraphs that frame the whole argument (p. 453): Lashley's "The brain is an analogical machine, not digital" (1958, p. 539), and Fodor's "[A]ny [psychological] theory that is incompatible with known facts of neurology must be, ipso facto, unacceptable" (1965, p. 176). The paper's conclusion is that Lashley was wrong too, and that Fodor's methodological principle has teeth.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** The computational theory of cognition began with McCulloch and Pitts (1943), who argued that neural activity is *digital* computation; Lashley and others replied that it is *analog*. Piccinini and Bahar argue that both camps were wrong, and that the question was never settled because it was never asked empirically. Their definition, adopted for the paper: "we define computationalism as the thesis that neural computation (simpliciter) explains cognition" (p. 454).

Their answer has two parts, one affirmative and one negative:

- **Affirmative.** A *weak* version of computationalism is true: neural processes are computations **in the generic sense** — the processing of medium-independent vehicles according to rules sensitive to differences between portions of those vehicles.
- **Negative.** The *specific* versions are both false on current evidence. Neural computation is neither digital nor analog: "typical neural signals, such as spike trains, are graded like continuous signals but are constituted by discrete functional elements (spikes). Therefore, typical neural signals are neither continuous signals nor strings of digits; neural computation is **sui generis**" (p. 476).

**Key findings (as argued).**

- Five theses about the nervous system are separated and ordered — input–output system; functionally organized; feedback control; information-processing; computing — with the entailments spelled out. Computing entails having inputs/outputs and being functionally organized, but not the reverse; and computing entails neither feedback control nor information processing (p. 457).
- **Generic computation** is defined without any appeal to representation: "the processing of vehicles (defined as entities or variables that can change state) in accordance with rules that are sensitive to certain vehicle properties and, specifically, to differences between different portions (i.e., spatiotemporal parts) of the vehicles" (p. 458). The load-bearing notion is **medium independence** — cooking, cleaning and exploding are excluded because they are "defined in terms of specific physical alterations of specific substances".
- Two arguments establish generic computationalism: one from the medium independence of spike trains, one from the medium independence of information. The second yields "processing information entails computing, but the reverse does not hold".
- The traditional objections do not refute computationalism. **Insufficiency objections** (Gödel-style, consciousness, understanding, abduction, embodiment, embeddedness) are "at best partial: Even if it is granted that computation is insufficient for X, it does not follow that neural processes are not computations... All that follows is that explaining X requires something else *in addition to* computation" (p. 464). **Objections from neural realization** (chemical signals, temporal constraints, spontaneous activity, analog-not-digital) each fail for a specific reason.
- Neural processes are **not analog computations** in the strict sense (Pour-El 1974), despite genuine similarities — continuous dynamics, graded dendritic potentials, neural integrators in oculomotor control. The decisive disanalogies: what is functionally significant about a spike "is not the absolute value of the voltage or the exact shape of the voltage curve but simply whether a spike is present or absent"; firing rates and spike timing have no counterpart in analog computers; and neural integrators operate on spike trains rather than continuous variables (pp. 466–67).
- Neural processes are **not digital computations** either, and this is the paper's longest argument: spikes cannot be typed as digits, and even if they could, spike sets could not be assembled into strings.
- The consequences are prescriptive. Understanding neural computation "requires a specially designed mathematical theory (or theories) rather than the mathematical theories of analog or digital computation" (p. 453); several inferences commonly drawn from computationalism do not follow; and "anyone seriously interested in explaining cognition should strive to show how the computations he or she postulates may be carried out by neural processes... The better an explanation of cognition is grounded in neural computation, the better the explanation" (p. 479).

**Initial takeaway.** This is the paper that converted "what kind of computer is the brain?" from a slogan into a question with an evidential answer, and answered *neither of the two on offer*. For the history of computational functionalism its significance is double-edged and the paper says so: it rescues a weak computationalism that almost nothing threatens, while removing the specific analogy — brain-as-digital-computer — that made the doctrine interesting and that most arguments for substrate independence had quietly assumed.

### Phase 2: Argument Reconstruction, with the Evidence Base

#### 2.1 Five theses, and what each adds (§1)

| Thesis | Statement (p.) | What it adds | Status the authors assign |
|---|---|---|---|
| (1) | "The nervous system is an input–output system" (454) | Internal processes mediate stimulus and response — more than behaviourism, but if input and output are liberally construed "any physical system belongs in the class" | "obviously true but says nothing" |
| (2) | "...a **functionally organized** input–output system" (455) | Neural activity has the *function* of generating specific responses. Shares the class with engines, refrigerators, stomachs | "stronger... but remains weak. Again, there is no reason to doubt it" |
| (3) | "...a **feedback control**, functionally organized, input–output system" (455) | Names the specific function: controlling behaviour in response to bodily and environmental stimuli including the system's own effects. Shares the class with autopilots | "not trivial and took serious scientific work to establish rigorously (Cannon, 1932). Nevertheless, it is by now uncontroversial" |
| (4) | "...an **information-processing**, feedback control..." (455) | Names the means. Four senses of information are distinguished | uncontroversial in at least one important sense |
| (5) | "The nervous system is a **computing system**" (457) | Presupposed by any generic computationalism; requires an account of computation independent of information processing | The paper's subject |

**The four senses of "information"** (pp. 455–57), which matter because the paper is careful not to let information smuggle in computation:

- *Thermodynamic* — related to entropy; "everything carries information and every physical process is an instance of information processing in this sense". Set aside as too general.
- *Mutual information* (Shannon & Weaver 1949) — a measure of statistical dependency between source and receiver; "experimentally well established that neural signals such as firing rates are statistically dependent on other variables" (Dayan & Abbott 2001, ch. 4; Baddeley, Hancock & Földiák 2000).
- *Natural semantic information* (Grice's 1957 "natural meaning"; Dretske 1981) — a signal carries it about a source iff it causally or reliably correlates with the source. Crucially, it **cannot misrepresent**: "A variable that does not correlate with a source does not misrepresent; it simply fails to carry natural information about the source" (p. 456).
- *Non-natural semantic information* (Grice's "non-natural meaning"; Scarantino & Piccinini 2010) — representation proper, which can be wrong. "A signal may represent objects that do not even exist."

The authors flag a terminological divergence they then commit to: many cognitive scientists call anything carrying natural information a "representation"; most philosophers reserve the word for states that can misrepresent. "From now on, we will follow this usage" (p. 456).

**The entailment map** (p. 457): (5) entails (1) and (2), but not conversely — "there are plenty of systems — including functionally organized systems such as the animals' locomotive, circulatory, and respiratory systems — that do not perform computations in any relevant sense". And (5) entails neither (3) nor (4), the counterexample being "a computer programmed to manipulate meaningless digits according to some arbitrary rule".

#### 2.2 Three kinds of computation (§2)

**Generic computation** (§2.1, p. 458), quoted in full because everything downstream rests on it:

> Computation in the generic sense is the processing of vehicles (defined as entities or variables that can change state) in accordance with rules that are sensitive to certain vehicle properties and, specifically, to differences between different portions (i.e., spatiotemporal parts) of the vehicles.

With three riders: a rule is "just a map from inputs to outputs; it need not be represented within the computing system"; processing is done by a functionally organized mechanism, so that "if the mechanism malfunctions, a miscomputation occurs"; and the vehicles are **medium independent** —

> vehicles are medium independent if and only if the rule (i.e., the input–output map) that defines a computation is sensitive only to differences between portions of the vehicles along specific dimensions of variation — it is insensitive to any more concrete physical properties of the vehicles. (p. 458)

The exclusion clause is explicit: "many processes, such as cooking, cleaning, and exploding, are defined in terms of specific physical alterations of specific substances. They are not medium independent; hence, they are not computations." And the **Watt governor** is given as a system that exerts feedback control "without ever transducing the signals into an internal medium solely devoted to computing — that is, without any medium-independent processing" (pp. 458–59), which is why thesis (3) does not entail (5).

**Digital computation** (§2.2, pp. 459–60). A **digit** is "a macroscopic state (of a component of the system) whose type can be reliably and unambiguously distinguished by the system from other macroscopic types", with many microscopic states per digit type — "a huge number of distinct arrangements of electrons (microscopic states) correspond to the same charge stored in a capacitor". Digits are concatenated into **strings**; a digital computation processes strings by a rule. Two points are singled out as important later (p. 460): "(a) whether a particular microscopic state belongs to a digit type is unambiguous relative to the behavior of the system; and (b) the output of a computation depends (either deterministically or probabilistically) only on the internal state of the system and on the number of input digits, their types, and the way they are concatenated within the string during a given time interval."

Figure 1 sets out nested classes within digital computation: **classical computation** (digital computation over language-like vehicles, in Fodor and Pylyshyn's 1988 sense), **algorithmic computation** (digital computation following an algorithm, language-like or not), and **computation of Turing-computable functions** (which need not proceed algorithmically — "the internal process may be irreducibly continuous"). McCulloch–Pitts nets and perceptrons are digital throughout; Hopfield nets and PDP networks "are digital in their inputs and outputs but not in their internal dynamics".

**Analog computation** (§2.3, p. 461). Following Pour-El (1974), also Rubel (1993) and Mills (2008): systems "whose function is to manipulate continuous variables — variables that can vary continuously over time and take any real values within certain intervals — specified by differential equations". The decisive contrast with digital: "While a digital computing system can always unambiguously distinguish digits from one another, a physically implemented analog computing system cannot do the same with the exact values of continuous variables, because these variables can only be measured within a margin of error." Quantum computation is noted as a third species (qudits, qubits). Figure 2: generic computation properly contains digital and analog "and more".

#### 2.3 The two arguments for generic computationalism (§3)

**Argument from the functional organization of the nervous system** (p. 462, as numbered by the authors):

1. The neural processes that explain cognition are manipulations of medium-independent vehicles.
2. Manipulations of medium-independent vehicles are computations in the generic sense.
3. Therefore, the neural processes that explain cognition are computations in the generic sense.

*Evidence offered for premise 1.* The vehicles are "voltage changes in the dendrites, neuronal spikes (action potentials), neurotransmitters, and hormones", with spike trains the focus since they are "the primary means by which neurons send long-distance signals". Nervous systems "transduce different physical stimuli into a common internal medium" (Adrian 1928; Adrian & Zotterman 1926); spikes obey the all-or-none principle; the functionally relevant aspects are **rate coding** and **temporal coding**, the latter mattering "when stimuli must be processed more quickly than would be possible if the brain relied on spike rates" (VanRullen, Guyonneau & Thorpe 2005), with examples from vision (Bair & Koch 1996; Gollisch 2009; Meister & Berry 1999) and somatosensation (Johansson & Birznieks 2004). The authors are careful to record that "the relative importance and prevalence of temporal versus rate coding remains an issue of intense debate (London et al. 2010)". The medium-independence conclusion follows because "the functionally relevant aspects of spike trains, such as spike rates and spike timing, are similar throughout the nervous system regardless of the physical properties of the stimuli... and may be implemented either by neural tissue or by some other physical medium, such as a silicon-based circuit".

**Argument from semantic information processing** (p. 463):

1. Some neural processes that explain cognition process semantic information.
2. Anything that processes semantic information is a computation in the generic sense.
3. Therefore, some neural processes that explain cognition are computations in the generic sense.

The key step is premise 2, and the reason given is medium independence again: "Processing information means processing vehicles on the basis of the information they carry rather than their specific physical properties. And the information carried by a signal is defined independently of the physical medium that implements the signal." The converse is explicitly denied — "medium-independent vehicles need not carry any information".

#### 2.4 Why not analog (§5)

The authors take "analog" in three senses and rebut each route to analog computationalism.

| Sense of "analog" | Who uses it | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Broad: dynamical evolution of real variables in real time | Churchland & Sejnowski 1992; van Gelder 1998 | True of the brain, but "so are all or most physical processes according to current physical theory" — the weather, planetary motion, digestion. "Even more important, given a sufficiently fine-grained description, ordinary digital computers are analog in this sense, too!" (p. 466) |
| Representations bearing a positive analogy to what they represent | Grush 2004; Waskan 2003; receptive fields (Hubel & Wiesel 1962; Schreiner & Winer 2007); rodent barrel cortex (Inan & Crair 2007) | Evidence accepted that nervous systems contain analog models — "But analog models need not be represented and manipulated by analog computers" |
| Strict: analog computers properly so called | Pour-El 1974; Rubel 1993; Mills 2008 | "Claiming that neural systems are analog computers is a strong empirical hypothesis", committed to the functionally significant signals being irreducibly continuous variables |

*Similarities the authors concede* (p. 466): both brains and analog computers have essentially continuous dynamics (Dayan & Abbott 2001; Ermentrout & Terman 2010); neurotransmitters and hormones are best modelled as continuous variables with continuously operating receptors; dendrites and some axons transmit graded potentials; and "as Rubel (1985) points out, neural integrators do play an important role in at least some processes, such as oculomotor control" — integrators being "the most important components of classical, general-purpose analog computers".

*The three disanalogies that decide it* (pp. 466–67): the mathematics of analog computers cannot be applied directly, so "new mathematical theory — specific to neural processes — had to be invented"; spikes are all-or-none and "what is functionally significant is not the absolute value of the voltage or the exact shape of the voltage curve but simply whether a spike is present or absent", so the significant variables are firing rates and spike timing, for which "there is nothing similar... in analog computers"; and neural integrators "operate on spike trains (as opposed to continuous variables), which means they operate on a different type of vehicle than the integrators of analog computers".

#### 2.5 Why not digital (§§6–7)

**Step 1: what McCulloch and Pitts assumed** (§6). Their empirical justification was the similarity between digits and spikes — spikes "appear to be discrete or digital, that is, to a first approximation, their occurrence is an unambiguous event relative to the functioning of the system" (p. 467). But two further assumptions were needed, and the authors document that both were known to be false or unsupported:

- **A fixed number of neural stimuli are always necessary and sufficient to generate a pulse.** "They knew this is false: They explicitly mentioned that the 'excitability' of a neuron does vary over time (McCulloch & Pitts, 1943, pp. 20–21)." Added since: absolute and relative refractory periods; short- and long-term potentiation and depression; stochastic transmembrane ion flow (Hille 2001; White et al. 1998) and stochastic neurotransmitter release (Bennett & Kearns 2000); and residual variability even in pacemaker-like firing (Le Bon-Jego & Yuste 2007; Yassin et al. 2010).
- **All neurons are synchronized on temporal intervals of fixed uniform length equal to synaptic delay**, with each interval affecting only the next. "As McCulloch and Pitts pointed out, these assumptions are not based on empirical evidence. The reason for these assumptions was, presumably, that they allowed the mathematics of digital computation to describe, at least to a first approximation, what they presumed to be most functionally relevant" (p. 468).

The authors then note what theoretical neuroscience actually used instead, "in order of decreasing biological detail and increasing computational tractability": conductance-based models (Hodgkin & Huxley 1952), integrate-and-fire networks (Caianiello 1961; Knight 1972; Lapicque; Stein 1965), and firing-rate models (Wilson & Cowan 1972). "From a mathematical point of view, the models employed by theoretical neuroscientists after McCulloch and Pitts resemble those of Rashevsky's original mathematical biophysics, in that they do not employ concepts and techniques from computability theory" (p. 469).

**Step 2: the argument** (§7, p. 469):

1. Digital computation requires the manipulation of strings of digits.
2. Neural spikes are not digits, and even if they were digits, spike sets would not be strings.
3. Therefore, the manipulation of spike sets is not digital computation.

Two caveats are entered before the evidence (pp. 469–70):

- *Against the retreat to levels.* A classicist might say classicism is committed to digital computation at the "cognitive" level, not the implementation level. Two replies: "biological cognitive systems — that is, nervous systems — cannot be analyzed into two levels, one of which is cognitive and one of which is implementational (cf. Lycan, 1990). Nervous systems contain many mechanistic levels (Craver, 2007)... Thus, the levels of neurons and networks are as cognitive as any higher level." And the emergence of higher-level digital computation from lower-level non-digital computation "requires both an explicit account of how this occurs and some empirical support, both of which are lacking to date."
- *Independence from representation.* "The present argument is not formulated in terms of the notion of representation. It rests on the notion of digits as discrete entities that can be concatenated into strings... In fact, our argument is consistent both with representationalism and its denial." It is likewise indifferent to digital-vs-analog representation (Maley 2011), localized-vs-distributed, and symbolic-vs-subsymbolic (Rumelhart & McClelland 1986; Smolensky 1989).

**Step 3: spikes cannot be typed as digits** (§7.1). The chain of reasons, in the authors' order:

1. *The relevant time interval cannot be instantaneous* — "otherwise, there would be uncountably many digits in any finite amount of time" — and spikes themselves take about 1 ms.
2. *McCulloch and Pitts' synaptic-delay interval fails on three counts*: synaptic delay is variable (Lin & Faber 2002); there is "no empirical evidence of any such master time interval" of which inter-spike intervals are exact multiples; and neurons are not perfectly synchronized.
3. *No fixed interval of physiological significance is available.* Spikes may occur within 1 ms of stimuli in vitro (Mainen & Sejnowski 1995), but in vivo firing "often occurs with high variability, due to a large number of factors in the fluctuating cellular environment, which includes thousands of neuronal connections and dozens of different cell types" (p. 471).
4. *Probabilism alone is not the problem* — digital computations may be probabilistic, with a **finite** sample space. **The problem is the sample space.** "Whereas digital computational events are temporally discrete, spike times are values of continuous variables... there appear to be no functionally significant time intervals during which spikes are to be counted, and as a result, spikes must be counted over real time. Because of this, the probability that a spike occurs at any instant of real time is zero. In other words, the sample space for spiking events during a time interval is uncountably infinite. Spike probabilities need to be given by probability densities" (p. 471; Dayan & Abbott 2001, p. 24). Conclusion: "as there can only be finitely many digit types but there are an uncountable number of possible spiking events, spiking events cannot be digits."
5. *A deeper reason: individual spikes rarely carry unambiguous functional significance.* Two strands of evidence. **Spontaneous activity** — suggested to account for "at least 75% of the brain's energy consumption" (Raichle & Mintun 2006), variously pacemaker-regular (Le Bon-Jego & Yuste 2007; suprachiasmatic nuclei firing without input), default-network-related (Raichle et al. 2001; Zhang & Raichle 2010, "although still controversial", Morcom & Fletcher 2007), or functionally noisy via stochastic resonance (Douglass et al. 1993; Levin & Miller 1996; Moss et al. 2004). The authors note that spontaneous activity per se is no objection — digital computations depend on internal states too — but that neural spontaneous activity has all the features that disqualify spikes as digits. **Rate coding** — the crayfish photoreceptor "fires at a rate of about 5 Hz in the dark and speeds up to 30 Hz when exposed to visible light" (Bruno & Kennedy 1962); in such cases "individual spikes have no functional significance on their own... many individual spikes might in principle be added to or removed from a spike train without appreciably altering its functional significance".

Two alternative candidates for the role of digit are then rejected (pp. 472–73):

- **Precisely timed spike patterns** (triplets or quadruplets within ~1 ms): "these attempts have failed: Rigorous statistical analysis indicates that both the number and types of precisely timed spike patterns are those that should be expected by chance (Oram, Wiener, Lestienne, & Richmond, 1999)." And "according to some estimates, the minimal signaling units in the human brain are of the order of 50–100 neurons (Shadlen & Newsome, 1998)."
- **Spike rates as digits** — von Neumann's (1958) proposal. Three problems: no non-arbitrary way to parse continuously varying rates into time intervals; no clear way to concatenate rates across neurons into strings; and, "perhaps most important, spike rates cannot be digits in the standard sense, because they cannot be typed into finitely many types of unambiguous physiological significance", since precision is limited. The authors close the point with an unusually direct historical remark: "Von Neumann pointed out that he did not know how to do logic or arithmetic over such entities as spike rates, and hence, he did not know how the brain could perform logic or arithmetic using such a system. As far as we know, no one after von Neumann has addressed this problem. And without a solution to this problem, von Neumann's proposal remains an empty shell."

**Step 4: even if spikes were digits, spike sets would not be strings** (§7.2).

- *Sets of synchronous spikes* (§7.2.1). A first difficulty — "the looseness of the boundaries between neural structures", such that cortical columns cannot be defined "precisely enough that one can determine, neuron-by-neuron, whether each neuron belongs to one structure or another", with neurons recruited and reorganised (Elbert & Rockstroh 2004; Recanzone, Merzenich & Schreiner 1992) — is judged "may not be fatal". **The fatal problem is that "synchrony between spikes is a matter of degree."** The community's working definition is *stochastic phase synchronization*, "which measures the degree to which the phase difference between neurons remains constant; thus, neurons can be synchronized even with a (relatively) constant phase lag" (Pikovsky, Rosenblum & Kurths 2003). "The interesting question about neural synchrony is not *whether*, in an absolute sense, there is synchrony in a neural process, but rather *how much* synchrony there is" (p. 474). Synchrony's genuine functional roles (attention: Fries et al. 2001, 2008; Roy et al. 2007; Steinmetz et al. 2000; pathology: epilepsy, Uhlhaas & Singer 2006; Parkinson's, Tass et al. 2003) are acknowledged — the point is that degree-valued synchrony cannot deliver string membership.
- *Spike trains from single neurons* (§7.2.2). Temporal order gives a natural concatenation relation, but a string needs an unambiguous first and last member. Two problems. First, "the question of which spikes belong to a string appears to be ill defined", because of stochasticity and spontaneous background activity — illustrated with the "typically broad power spectrum of spiking frequencies from the unstimulated crayfish mechanoreceptor–photoreceptor system", where under periodic stimulation the stimulus frequency appears encoded in the power spectrum superimposed on that background (Bahar 2003; Bahar & Moss 2003), with no way "to parse which spikes are the ones encoding the stimulus". Second, "individual spike trains are not useful units of functional significance. What has functional significance is not a specific train of spikes... but something held in common by many different spike trains that are generated by the same neuron in response to the same sensory stimulus. The only known way to find useful units of functional significance is to average spike trains from single neurons over many trials" (pp. 474–75).

**Step 5: the neural code is not a computational code** (§7.3). Neural encoding is "the functional dependence of some property of a neural response on some property of an environmental stimulus", decoding the inverse inference. Computational coding is "one-to-one (sometimes, many-to-one) mappings between classes of strings of digits" (Rogers 1967, pp. 27–29), exact and usually exactly reversible. "Whereas neural encoding is only approximate and approximately reversible, (digital) computational coding is (by definition) exact... The existence of a neural code does not entail that neural processes are digital computations" (p. 475).

**Step 6: where the burden lies now** (§7.4). "The discrete nature of spikes was the main empirical motivation for digital computationalism. Once the original assumptions about spikes that supported digital computationalism are rejected, the burden is on supporters of digital computationalism to find evidence that some neural variables are suited to being treated as strings of digits, and that this has something to do with explaining cognitive phenomena" (p. 476).

#### 2.6 The inferential step, stated plainly

The move from evidence to conclusion is a disjunctive elimination with a definitional premise:

1. By §2.2, digital computation requires vehicles that can be typed into **finitely many unambiguously distinguishable** types and **concatenated** into strings with determinate membership.
2. By §2.3, analog computation requires **irreducibly continuous** functionally significant variables.
3. By the neurophysiology in §§5 and 7, the functionally significant properties of neural signals — firing rate and spike timing — satisfy neither: spike events live in an uncountable sample space over real time and carry no individually determinate functional significance (so not digits), while what is functionally significant is presence-or-absence rather than voltage magnitude and there is no analog-computer counterpart to rate and timing (so not continuous variables in the strict analog sense).
4. By §3, they are nevertheless medium-independent, hence generic computations.
5. Therefore neural computation is a generic computation of neither established species — **sui generis** — and "understanding neural computation requires a specially designed mathematical theory (or theories)".

The authors are explicit that step 3 is an *empirical* claim about "current neuroscientific evidence" and could in principle be overturned; the burden-shifting in §7.4 is what that acknowledgement amounts to in practice.

#### 2.7 Consequences (§8)

**Neural computation is sui generis** (§8.1). The supporting observation about practice is stated sharply: "in realistic mathematical models of neural processes, which can be tested against experiments, the explanatory role of (digital) computability theory and (digital) computer design is nil" (p. 476). And the template it fails to match: "When a process is explained by digital computation, at a minimum the explanation includes a stream of digital inputs, internal states, and outputs, a set of rules for manipulating the digits, and a system of digital circuits that perform the manipulations... But nothing of this sort has been found to explain neural processes. If the present argument is right, none is forthcoming" (p. 477).

**Two fallacies** (§8.2).

- *Equating computation with information processing.* Named holders: Churchland, Koch & Sejnowski 1990; Fodor 2008; O'Brien & Opie 2006; **Shagrir 2010** — which places entry 2 of this batch on the other side of this dispute. "Equating computation with information processing obscures the structural (non-semantic) differences between different types of computing... To understand the nature of neural computation, it is essential to define computation independently of information processing (and, a fortiori, representation) so as to highlight its structural aspects."
- *Running computationalism together with mechanistic explanation.* "Marr (1982) dubbed the mathematical analysis of the functions of neural mechanisms 'computational theory'... But thus understood, computationalism ceases to be an empirical hypothesis about the specific functional nature of neural processes, to become a trivial consequence of the definition of mechanistic explanation."

**Four inferences that do not follow from generic computationalism** (p. 477):

- (a) that we can capture what is functionally relevant to neural processes in a formalism from digital computability theory;
- (b) that computer programs can be functionally equivalent to neural processes in the way programs are computationally equivalent to each other;
- (c) that the study of neural or mental computation is independent of the study of neural implementation;
- (d) that the Church–Turing thesis applies to neural activity as it applies to digital computers.

On (b) the distinction is drawn carefully: when one program simulates another, "the computations of the simulating program *encode* (in the sense of computer science) the computations of the simulated program"; when a program implements a model of a neural process, "the computations of the simulating program are a means for generating approximate representations of the states that the mathematical model attributes to the neural process. This kind of model attempts to capture something essential to a neural process... by employing suitable abstractions and idealizations. There is no sense in which such modeling work can proceed independently of the empirical study of neural processes" (p. 478). On (d): the argument from the Church–Turing thesis to computationalism (e.g. Churchland 2007) "commits the fallacy of conflating mechanism and a form of computationalism restricted to the computations that fall under the Church-Turing thesis (Copeland, 2000; Piccinini, 2007c)". The hypercomputation option is noted as a live alternative (Copeland 2000; Siegelmann 2003), and then set aside: "If, as we have argued here, neural activity is not digital computation, then the question of whether neural computation is computable by Turing machines does not even arise." On humans: "Human beings can certainly perform digital computations, as anyone who has learned basic arithmetic can attest... However, there is no evidence that human beings are computationally more powerful than Turing machines."

**The prescription for psychology** (§8.3). If neural computation is sui generis, what becomes of mental models (Johnson-Laird 1983, 2010), production systems (Newell 1990), and "theories" (Murphy 2002)? Two tempting responses are rejected. *Autonomy* fails because "functional analysis turns out to be an elliptical or partial mechanistic explanation (Piccinini & Craver, 2011). Thus, psychological explanation is a type of mechanistic explanation after all... directly constrained by neural structures." *Elimination* is "premature" — "Computational theories in psychology are often our current best way of capturing and explaining psychological phenomena." The remaining option is reinterpretation: "take psychological constructs that rely on digital computation and gradually reinterpret or replace them with theoretical constructs that can be realized by known neural processes, such as the spike trains of neuronal ensembles" (p. 479). The rule of thumb offered: "The better an explanation of cognition is grounded in neural computation, the better the explanation." A door is left open for digital computationalists: "We cannot rule out that some cognitive phenomena are explained by digital computation. If so, then digital neural computation must somehow emerge from non-digital neural computation. The next frontier for serious digital computationalists is to develop an explicit account of how this occurs and find neuroscientific evidence to back it up" (p. 480).

**The anti-reductionism reply.** Asked whether grounding cognition in neural computation leads to grounding everything in quantum mechanics, the answer is that mechanistic grounding runs both ways: "In an ideally complete mechanistic explanation, a specific subset of the capacities of entities at one level must be shown to contribute something essential to the capacities of entities at a higher level... an ideally complete mechanistic explanation of a phenomenon integrates all and only the information that is needed to explain the phenomenon at each level of a mechanistic hierarchy" (p. 480).

## 7. Coelho Mollo (2018) — Functional Individuation, Mechanistic Implementation [tier: short]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Coelho Mollo 2018 - Functional individuation mechanistic implementation.pdf` — full title: *Functional individuation, mechanistic implementation: the proper way of seeing the mechanistic view of concrete computation*. DOI `10.1007/s11229-017-1380-5`; received 27 November 2016, accepted 15 March 2017; © The Author(s) 2017, **open access**. **PDF version: published** (Springer typeset open-access article), but deposited through the King's Research Portal, whose cover sheet labels it "Peer reviewed version" and cites it as "SYNTHESE, 1-21. Advance online publication" — so it carries no final volume or page numbers. Citations below give the PDF page (`PDF p. n`); the article body begins at PDF p. 2. Author: Dimitri Coelho Mollo, Department of Philosophy, King's College London, and Institut für Philosophie, Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin. The manifest and this review's file name date it 2018 (the year of the print issue); the article itself is 2017.

**Claim.** The mechanistic view of concrete computation — the attempt by Piccinini (2007, 2015), Miłkowski (2013) and Fresco (2014) to naturalise computation using the tools of New Mechanism — is under threat from a tension noted by Haimovici (2013): mechanistic explanation is supposed to deliver structural detail, while computational explanation is medium-independent and delivers almost none. Coelho Mollo argues that the standard reply fails, that computational **individuation** is indeed purely functional, and that mechanism's real job lies elsewhere — in **implementation**. Since a theory of concrete computation needs both, mechanism keeps a central role, "though for reasons other than the ones normally adduced by computational mechanists" (PDF p. 3).

**Method or type.** Conceptual analysis, structured as a rescue operation on an existing position. Eight sections: New Mechanism (adopting Illari and Williamson's 2012 deliberately inclusive definition — "A mechanism for a phenomenon consists of entities and activities organised in such a way that they are responsible for the phenomenon"); the mechanistic view of computation; Haimovici's dilemma; why the standard reply fails; the amended view; and an application to the multiplicity-of-computations problem.

**Key result or argument.**

- **The dilemma** (§4, PDF p. 8). Two claims that cannot both hold alongside the thesis that computational explanation is adequate mechanistic explanation: "1. Good mechanistic explanation tends toward full structural detail at all levels of the mechanism. 2. Computational explanation is necessarily abstract, insofar as it ignores most structural detail, caring only about degrees of freedom."
- **Piccinini's reply, quoted** (2015, pp. 124–125, at PDF p. 8): "I reject the view that mechanistic explanation requires the specification of structural and functional properties at all levels of organization. Instead, mechanistic explanation requires the specification of all relevant structural and functional properties at the relevant level(s) of mechanistic organization." On this reading, the structural properties computational explanation does specify are *degrees of freedom*, which are the ones relevant at the pertinent level.
- **Why that reply fails** (§5). The constraint left standing — that the system have adequate degrees of freedom — "amounts to the trivial requirement that the physical system have structural properties able to realise the functional characterisation" (PDF p. 10), and that is precisely the kind of weak structural constraint functional explanation already imposes. His illustration is his own laptop: a functional decomposition into "processor" and "memory register" already constrains structure (whatever plays the processor role must be physically arranged to do what a processor does), while a structural decomposition into "2 Ghz Intel Core chips" and a "500 GB solid-state Flash hard-disk" already constrains function. He then blocks the natural rejoinder — that computational explanation *stops* at the right level of abstraction while functional explanation merely declines to go further — by pointing out that functional explanation has exactly the same reasons for stopping: "It is not true that functional explanation improves with more structural detail—one of its hallmarks, multiple realisability, is lost in the process" (PDF p. 11). Conclusion: "If that is all that is needed to count as mechanistic, New Mechanism ends up being a rather trivial position" (PDF p. 11), and the honest label would be the one Piccinini himself used earlier — "the functional view".
- **The amendment** (§6, PDF p. 12). Computation in physical systems consists in:
  1. Manipulation of medium-independent vehicles according to a rule sensitive only to their degrees of freedom.
  2. The medium-independent vehicles are functional components of a teleofunctional mechanism.
  3. The manipulations that vehicles undergo are activities internal to a teleofunctional mechanism.
  4. It is one of the teleological functions of the teleofunctional mechanism to carry out (1).

  The dissolving move is a distinction between two questions that both deserve the name "computational explanation": **computational individuation** ("how does system $S$ compute function $f$?" — answered functionally, medium-independently, preserving multiple realisability) and **computational implementation** ("how does system $S$ implement the functional profile for the computation of function $f$?" — answered in terms of voltages, gates, chips and disks, and "not medium-independent, for the question cannot be answered in a medium-independent way", PDF p. 13). "Haimovici's dilemma does not grab a hold because the computational explanations that are purely functional are different in kind to the computational explanations that require structural detail" (PDF p. 14).
- **Where this sits relative to the rest of the batch** (PDF pp. 14–15, and this is the sentence the synthesis will want): "The appeal to mechanism in the mechanistic view plays an analogous role to mapping relations in the simple mapping account put forward by Putnam (1988), and to causal topology in Chalmers' (2011) causal account: it provides the bridge between functional computational individuation, and implementation in physical systems. But it provides further constraints than its competitors: it includes mapping as well as causal considerations, into a richer, more constrained, picture." Physical computational systems must be more than mappable and more than causal — "they must be mechanisms—organised systems with relatively clear boundaries, decomposable into physical parts that play a role in bringing about the overall behaviour of the system; and they must be teleofunctional mechanisms with the teleological function of performing computations." Two payoffs claimed: pancomputationalism is avoided, and teleological function "introduces a normative dimension useful in accounting for miscomputation".
- **The multiplicity of computations** (§7). This is where the paper answers entries 2 and 4 of this batch directly. Sprevak's (2010) device $D$ takes two voltage inputs and produces one output; taking 0–5 V for False and 5–10 V for True gives the truth table of conjunction, and switching the assignment gives disjunction — so $D$ "seems to be simultaneously computing both logical functions" unless semantic content settles it. Coelho Mollo rejects Piccinini's (2008) reply — appeal to wide systemic function and immediate context — on two grounds: it "concedes too much", since it grants that $D$ implements more than one computation, and it fails against **fully dual computational systems**, in which every gate and the system as a whole admit two consistent interpretations (a case he credits to Oron Shagrir and Nir Fresco). He endorses instead Dewhurst's (2016) split between *computational* and *logical* individuation — the indeterminacy is real but lies at the logical level, "at least one step above computational individuation" — while rejecting Dewhurst's claim that computational individuation happens at the **physical** level. His objection is that the physical level is too fine-grained to permit computational **equivalence**, and hence destroys multiple realisability. Two illustrations: devices $D_1$ and $D_2$ identical but for their engineering "cushion" intervals (4–5 V versus 5–6 V, producing a null value inside), and devices identical but for noise bands (4.5–5.5 V versus 4.9–5.1 V) would count as computationally non-equivalent "despite their striking similarity" (PDF p. 18). His fix is to individuate at the **functional** level, by **equivalence classes** of physical states defined by "input values that lead to uniform behaviour of the whole device": $D_1$ and $D_2$ then share one input–output table over classes EC1 and EC2, "the labels are fully arbitrary", and a hydraulic device $D_3$ whose equivalence classes are ranges of water levels in tanks shares the same profile. "Thereby, computational equivalence is possible even between systems that work by means of completely different physical principles—and the multiple realisability of computation is preserved" (PDF p. 20). Logical indeterminacy survives, and is declared "a welcome result".
- **The residual worry the author states himself** (PDF p. 21). Whether there are principled, non-observer-relative ways of carving a device's functional structure. His gesture at an answer: teleological functions "bestow, as it were, privileged, objective, capacities on teleofunctional mechanisms and their components. Hence privileged observer-independent functional and structural decompositions would be available for all teleofunctional mechanisms, computational ones included. Whether this strategy will bear any fruit depends, though, on whether there are objective teleological functions in the world—a question that lies beyond the scope of this paper." He also records (n. 2) that he remains neutral on which theory of teleological function is correct, and that the view inherits any attack on the notion itself (citing Craver 2013).

**Place in the field's history.** This is the batch's closing move in the individuation debate, and it is a reframing rather than a new side. Against Piccinini (entries 3 and 6), it concedes the semanticists' structural point — computational *individuation* is functional, not mechanistic — while denying them the conclusion, because mechanism is relocated to implementation. Against Shagrir (entry 2) and Sprevak (entry 4), it grants that no non-semantic theory can tell an AND-gate from an OR-gate, and then argues that this was never a question about computational individuation. And it explicitly positions itself in the lineage of entries 1 and 5: mechanism is offered as the successor to Putnam's simple mapping and to Chalmers' causal topology, doing the same bridging job under strictly tighter constraints.

**Relevance.** For a history of computational functionalism the paper matters because it separates two things the doctrine has always run together. "Which computation is this system running?" is answered functionally and medium-independently — which is what substrate independence needs. "How does this system manage to run it?" is answered only in the idiom of the specific material — silicon, neurons, valves, beer tins. On this picture substrate independence is not a discovery about minds but a consequence of where computational identity is fixed; and the price of that is a class of systems restricted to teleofunctional mechanisms, which in turn rests on there being objective teleological functions in the world. The paper states that dependency and leaves it open.

## Cross-paper notes for the synthesis

These are observations a historian of the field would want carried forward. They record what the papers say about each other; they do not adjudicate.

### 1. Two separate challenges, often merged

The batch contains two distinct threats to computational functionalism that are easy to run together, and the papers themselves distinguish them:

- **Triviality / universal realization** — does every system implement every computation? Putnam (1988) and Searle (1990) say yes. Chalmers (1996, 2011) says no, once the implementation conditionals are read modally and the formalism upgraded to combinatorial state automata. Shagrir (2006) concedes the point and relocates the answer to scientific practice. Piccinini (2010) explicitly declines to fight this battle, calling Putnam's and Searle's problem "not very serious" because their descriptions are anomalous — and then raises a **different**, surviving problem: everything can be given indefinitely many *bona fide* computational descriptions (p. 281).
- **Individuation** — given that a system computes, which computation is it running? Shagrir, Sprevak and Coelho Mollo take this as the central question; Chalmers addresses it only through the observation that "within every physical system, there are numerous computational systems" and that choosing among descriptions is "interest-relative" to a very limited extent (2011, PDF p. 5).

A synthesis that treats these as one debate will misattribute positions. Piccinini in particular is often listed as an anti-triviality theorist; his own text places his objection elsewhere.

### 2. The semantics fault line, with named sides

Every paper in this batch takes a position on whether computational identity depends on representational content, and several name each other.

| Author | Position | Where stated |
|---|---|---|
| Chalmers (1993/2011) | **No** — "computations are specified syntactically, not semantically"; building content into implementation conditions would ground computation on something that "desperately needs a foundation itself" (PDF p. 6) | §2.2 |
| Shagrir (2006) | **Yes, but only mathematical content** — content picks "the" computational structure out of the many a mechanism implements; specific content is irrelevant (pp. 411–12) | §4, objections 2–3 |
| Piccinini (2010) | **No**, and the semantic view "is incorrect" because computability theorists and computer designers individuate computational states without semantic properties (p. 282). Names Shagrir 2001, 2006 | §3 |
| Sprevak (2010) | **Yes, content of any type** — distal, broad, narrow, mathematical. Non-semantic realization functions yield universal realization; nothing physical distinguishes AND from OR (PDF pp. 10–11, 24–25) | §§2.2, 3.3 |
| Piccinini & Bahar (2013) | **No**, and lists equating computation with information processing as a **fallacy**, naming **Shagrir 2010** among its holders (p. 477) | §8.2 |
| Coelho Mollo (2018) | **No** for computational individuation; **maybe** for *logical* individuation, which is a level above (PDF pp. 17, 20) | §7 |

The sharpest disagreement about a third party is over **Marr**. Shagrir reads the Zipser–Andersen and Marr–Hildreth models as explanatory only because of correspondence to structure in the represented domain; Sprevak reports Egan's contrary reading of Marr's computational level as purely function-theoretic and argues Egan's own machinery collapses into a representational one; Piccinini and Bahar cite Marr (1982) as an example of a *fallacy* — conflating computationalism with mechanistic explanation by naming the mathematical analysis of neural functions "computational theory" (p. 477). Three papers, three uses of the same book.

### 3. What each paper takes "implementation" to be — the succession

There is a clean lineage here, and the last paper states it explicitly. Coelho Mollo (2018, PDF pp. 14–15): mechanism "plays an analogous role to mapping relations in the simple mapping account put forward by Putnam (1988), and to causal topology in Chalmers' (2011) causal account", but with tighter constraints.

| Account | Bridge from abstract computation to physical system | Constraint strength |
|---|---|---|
| Simple mapping (Putnam 1988, as reported) | A mapping from physical states to formal states | Weakest — yields universal realization |
| Causal / CSA (Chalmers 1996, 2011) | Causal structure mirroring formal structure, with counterfactual-supporting transitions and vector states over distinct physical regions | Strong; Chalmers' own §7 (1996) admits two unresolved gaps |
| Mechanistic (Piccinini 2010; Piccinini & Bahar 2013) | Components with functions, organised; digits, strings, rules; medium independence | Strong, and non-trivially restrictive about *which systems compute* |
| Semantic (Shagrir 2006; Sprevak 2010) | Representation relations that "truly describe" the world's structure | Selects among mappings rather than restricting systems |
| Amended mechanistic (Coelho Mollo 2018) | Functional individuation **plus** mechanistic implementation, over teleofunctional mechanisms | Tightest as stated, but rests on objective teleological functions existing |

### 4. Direct engagements a synthesis can cite as such

- **Piccinini against Chalmers' computational sufficiency**, in a footnote rather than the main text (2010, n. 8, p. 275). The dilemma: if abstract causal organisation is fine-grained enough to determine the causation of behaviour, it captures the causal aspects of *any* property including digestion and combustion; if it is coarse enough to exclude those, there is no reason it captures every aspect of mental properties. Piccinini notes he lacks space for a full discussion and concedes the point is "fair".
- **Chalmers against Searle**, twice over: the wall/WordStar claim fails the strong conditionals (1996, p. 331; 2011, PDF p. 5), and the Chinese Room contains two distinct computational systems, so expect two minds (1996, p. 332; 2011, PDF pp. 11–12, with the ant-in-the-demon's-skull analogy).
- **Sprevak on Chalmers' clock and dial** (2010, PDF p. 10, n. 9): cited *not* as the solution to universal realization but as showing how cheaply the counterfactual requirement can be satisfied — "these conditions are easily satisfied". This is a reading of Chalmers' own §4 concession, and a synthesis should present it as such rather than as a disagreement about Chalmers' conclusion.
- **Coelho Mollo against Piccinini's reply to Haimovici** (2018, §5): granting Piccinini's rejection of the full-structural-detail requirement collapses the mechanistic view into the functional view, since the surviving constraint — adequate degrees of freedom — is the same one functional explanation already imposes.
- **Coelho Mollo against Piccinini on multiplicity** (2018, §7): Piccinini's (2008) appeal to wide systemic function "concedes too much" by granting that a dual gate implements more than one computation, and fails outright for fully dual systems.
- **Chalmers against Fodor on Turing** (2011, PDF p. 15): Fodor (1992) makes Turing's contribution the idea that syntactic transitions respect semantic content; Chalmers says "This strikes me as false", and that Turing's key contribution was "the formalization of the notion of mechanism, along with the associated universality of the formalization".

### 5. Unanswered challenges the authors flag themselves

These are the loose ends the papers leave, stated by their own authors. They are useful for a history because they mark where the field's work was left undone rather than where it was defeated.

- **Chalmers (1996), §7.** The CSA account still admits logically possible false implementations (the exponentially exploding construction), which he judges *should* be excluded; a "uniformity clause" or a causal-relevance clause is floated but not worked out. And the distinct-physical-region requirement may be too strong (virtual memory), yet dropping independence collapses CSA implementation into FSA implementation.
- **Chalmers (1996), n. 3.** **Maudlin (1989)** questions whether counterfactual-supporting conditionals can be relevant to conscious experience at all — if a connection supporting an unexercised counterfactual is blocked, can that plausibly change experience? Chalmers: "This argument requires an in-depth treatment in its own right." It is not treated in either Chalmers paper here, and Piccinini (2010) lists Maudlin alongside Block and Searle.
- **Chalmers (2011), note 6, added in 2011.** Computational sufficiency and organizational invariance are downgraded from metaphysical to **nomological** necessity, so systems with the same organisation and no consciousness remain metaphysically possible. Any synthesis quoting "computational sufficiency" from this paper must quote the footnote with it.
- **Shagrir (2006), reply to objection 7.** He cannot rule out that the relevant semantic relations reduce to causal relations involving distal stimuli and responses, "giving rise to a broader, non-semantic, account of computation. I doubt such an account will be forthcoming, but my reasons for this pessimism will have to wait for another occasion" (p. 414).
- **Sprevak (2010), §4.** The representation condition is necessary but not sufficient: computation must also be "mechanical" in a sense "yet to be defined", and sameness of computation requires achieving the mapping "in the same way" — neither is worked out.
- **Piccinini & Bahar (2013), §8.3.** "We cannot rule out that some cognitive phenomena are explained by digital computation. If so, then digital neural computation must somehow emerge from non-digital neural computation. The next frontier for serious digital computationalists is to develop an explicit account of how this occurs and find neuroscientific evidence to back it up" (p. 480). Also: von Neumann's problem of how to do logic or arithmetic over spike rates is reported as still unsolved (p. 473).
- **Coelho Mollo (2018), §7.** Whether principled, observer-independent carvings of functional structure are available turns on "whether there are objective teleological functions in the world—a question that lies beyond the scope of this paper" (PDF p. 21).

### 6. Where the empirical content actually is

Only one paper in this batch turns on data: **Piccinini and Bahar (2013)**. Everything else is conceptual analysis, thought experiment, or (in Shagrir's case) a case study of other people's empirical work. If the synthesis wants to say the field ever submitted computational functionalism to an empirical test, this is the paper to cite, alongside Piccinini (2010)'s methodological argument that computationalism "can only be done by studying the functional organization of the brain empirically" (p. 302). The verdict those two deliver is narrower than it first looks: they do not test whether the right computation would suffice for a mind; they test what kind of computation the brain performs, and answer **neither digital nor analog**.

### 7. Reading order suggestion

Chronology is not the clearest order here. For a reader coming to the debate fresh: Chalmers 1993/2011 (the positive programme) → Chalmers 1996 (the defence of its implementation relation) → Shagrir 2006 (the descriptive counterweight) → Piccinini 2010 (the disentangling) → Sprevak 2010 (the individuation counter-attack) → Piccinini & Bahar 2013 (what the brain actually does) → Coelho Mollo 2018 (the reframing that assigns each of the preceding a job).
