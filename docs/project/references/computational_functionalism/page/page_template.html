Running the Right Program

Sixty years of argument about whether a machine carrying out the right computation would have a mind: how the idea was stated, what has been thrown at it, and which questions are still open in 2026.

PURPOSE

Since the 1960s a family of views has held that what makes something a mind is not what it is made of but what it does — specifically, the computation it carries out. If that is right, the same mind could run on neurons, on silicon, on water pipes, or on a billion people passing radio messages. Philosophers call this computational functionalism, and its best-known consequence is that a machine running the right program would have a mind, and might feel things.

For fifty years the argument was conducted with imaginary machines. Then systems arrived that people talk to as though they were minds, and the question acquired a bill: whether to assess machines for consciousness, whether to build them, what would be owed to them. This page puts six research communities on one timeline, names the debates that cut across them, and records what their arguments can and cannot show.

It is a review of a field, not an argument for a position. It is built from {{N_REFS}} works, {{N_READ}} of them read from the PDF, and it states each position at its strongest. The headline is that none of the {{N_DEBATES}} debates traced here is settled — and that the loudest disagreement may not be the load-bearing one. Almost nobody in this collection says machines could never have minds; Searle, the most-engaged opponent here, writes that "only a machine could think"{{CITE:searle1980}}. What stays open is narrower: whether organisation by itself is enough, and at what level of detail a copy would have to be made.

01 The field at a glance

The earliest work here is Putnam's 1967 statement of functionalism, known only by name; the latest are 2025 and 2026 papers about systems you can type at. In between, the argument changes hands: philosophers of mind hold it alone for two decades, philosophers of computation take over the middle, and from 2014 consciousness scientists, neuroscientists, AI researchers and ethicists arrive together. The density on the right of figure 1 is the point — over half the collection is from the last twelve years.

One lane needs a warning before you read it. Biology and neuroscience has exactly one work here{{CITE:aru2023}}, because the biological challenge in this collection is mostly written by philosophers and consciousness scientists. That lane is not a field; it is a paper.

02 Three claims that are not the same claim

Most confusion in this literature comes from running together three claims of increasing strength. The collection's own ladder, which should not be collapsed:

  • Multiple realizability. The same psychological state can be realised by many different physical states. This is satisfied even in an all-carbon world, because two brains differ. It is assumed by every reviewed work here and contested by none — but only because the standard challenge to it is one of the works this collection does not hold.
  • Substrate independence. Mental states "can supervene on any of a broad class of physical substrates"{{CITE:bostrom2003}}. This adds the class, and the clearest statement of it in the collection is also the one that declines to defend it.
  • Organizational invariance. The property is preserved under every change that preserves causal topology — the pattern of interaction among a system's parts, abstracted from what the parts are made of{{CITE:chalmers1994cfc}}. This names what the class has in common, and it is the strongest rung.

Two further distinctions do a lot of work. Computational sufficiency — "there is a class of automata such that any implementation of an automaton in that class will possess a mind"{{CITE:chalmers1996rock}} — is a metaphysical claim, and is not the same as computational explanation, the scientific programme of explaining cognition computationally. Separating them is one work's central contribution: functionalism is metaphysics, computationalism is science, and neither entails the other once function is cashed out mechanistically{{CITE:piccinini2010}}.

One caveat travels with every quotation of computational sufficiency from the canonical source. The version held here is the author's web text of 1993 with a footnote added in 2011, and that footnote restricts sufficiency and organizational invariance to nomological rather than metaphysical necessity — leaving philosophical zombies metaphysically possible{{CITE:chalmers1994cfc}}. A citation that drops it claims something the paper no longer says.

03 Where the collection stands — and why that is not a vote

Figure 3 counts how each work stands on the thesis. Read it as a description of this collection. The collection was assembled to trace one thesis, so works that engage it are over-represented by construction, and a work that never mentions computational functionalism is absent whatever its influence. Two further cautions. "Restricts" is not "rejects": it marks a work that narrows the thesis or declines its stronger form in its own words, which is where the entire biological literature here sits. And two of the fourteen supports are narrow — one work supports only indirectly, by arguing that psychology does not reduce to physics without ever saying the autonomous level is computational{{CITE:fodor1974}}, and one only defensively, by arguing that the felt quality the whole debate is about is not a well-defined target{{CITE:dennett1988}}.

04 Five eras

Boundaries fall where the question being asked changed, and are approximate. They are the synthesis's, not the field's.

1967–1978 · 4 works Functionalism stated, and attacked from inside

Mental states as states in a machine table. The attack comes immediately, and from the architects: the machine-table level is simultaneously too fine — "if you and I differ only in the respect that your most probable response to the pain of stubbing your toe is to say 'damn' and mine is to say 'darn'", our pains are different types — and too coarse{{CITE:blockfodor1972}}. The same pair raise the possibility that two systems could share functional organisation while one feels nothing, call it deeper than their other arguments, and set it aside. Six years later it is cashed: the population of China, wired by radio, would have your organisation for an hour, and every version of functionalism falls into either liberalism (attributing minds too freely) or chauvinism (denying them too freely){{CITE:block1978}}. The author is careful about what this is: "a burden-of-proof argument", not a refutation.

1979–1995 · 8 works Is running the right program sufficient? The thought-experiment era

The medium is the imagined case. The Chinese Room is built to establish exactly one of its author's five numbered propositions — that instantiating a program is never by itself sufficient for intentionality{{CITE:searle1980}}. The reply that becomes standard is gradual replacement: swap neurons one at a time for parts that do the same job, and anyone who says the endpoint is unconscious must name the neuron at which the lights went out{{CITE:cuda1985}}. Fading and dancing qualia sharpen it into a dilemma about whether a subject could be systematically wrong about his own experience{{CITE:chalmers1995qualia}}. From an unexpected angle, Olympia attacks the defence rather than the thesis: whether a system runs a program depends on what it would do, while what you feel seems to depend on what is actually happening, and a machine can support every counterfactual with apparatus that does nothing during the run{{CITE:maudlin1989}}. And one paper denies that the thing being argued over is well defined at all{{CITE:dennett1988}}.

1996–2013 · 8 works What is it to run a computation at all?

The question moves down a level. If a rock can be described as running your brain's program, saying a mind is a program says nothing — so implementation is re-stated to require transitions that would have held had things gone otherwise, and the formalism is replaced when the author's own counterexample shows a clock and a dial recovering a weakened version of the problem{{CITE:chalmers1996rock}}. Others concede the point and relocate the answer: we take the computational stance towards brains because the regularities we care about are specified by what the signals are about{{CITE:shagrir2006}}, or because nothing physical decides whether a circuit is an AND gate or an OR gate{{CITE:sprevak2010}}. The era ends with the collection's one data-driven verdict: neural processes are computations in a generic sense but neither digital nor analog{{CITE:piccinini2013}} — removing, on empirical grounds and by its own defenders, the brain-as-digital-computer analogy that many arguments had quietly assumed.

2014–2021 · 12 works Theories of consciousness become machine tests, and the testability crisis opens

A leading theory states in one sentence that it contradicts functionalism: digital computers, even if functionally equivalent to us, "would experience next to nothing"{{CITE:tononi2015}}. Another answers from the same starting point that consciousness is two computations and nothing more{{CITE:dehaene2017}}. A third changes the explanandum, explaining why a machine claims to have experience and stating that it "emphatically does not explain" how we have one{{CITE:graziano2017}}. Then the floor moves: because recurrent and feedforward networks are both universal function approximators, any experiment supporting a causal-structure theory can be reproduced by a system with the opposite structure{{CITE:doerig2019}}, and a generalisation catches both camps in a dilemma between already-falsified and unfalsifiable{{CITE:kleiner2021}}. Biology returns as an immanent correction — metabolism is part of a human's functional profile{{CITE:godfreysmith2016}}, and perception exists to keep a body viable{{CITE:seth2018beast}} — and ethics arrives without committing to any theory{{CITE:schwitzgebel2015}}.

2022–2026 · 10 works Systems that talk back: assessment, precaution, and the biological alternative

All six communities contribute, and several works are jointly authored across them. The thesis becomes load-bearing and visible: one report states it, adopts it as a working hypothesis, and uses it to decide which theories are admitted — excluding integrated information theory "because it is not compatible with computational functionalism" — then extracts fourteen indicator properties and finds no current system a strong candidate{{CITE:butlin2023}}. Another converts every objection into an engineering challenge with explicit, self-described "extremely rough" credences{{CITE:chalmers2023llm}}. Neuroscience answers that the obstacles are architectural, while disclaiming the stronger reading three times{{CITE:aru2023}}. A grounding argument asks whether these systems' states mean anything before asking whether they feel{{CITE:mollo2023vector}}, and a companion names the Redescription Fallacy that dismissals keep committing{{CITE:milliere2024}}. And the question acquires institutions: a governance programme{{CITE:long2024welfare}}, a sentience-candidature framework with a citizens' panel{{CITE:birch2024}}, and the systematic statement of the biological alternative{{CITE:seth2025}}.

05 The dial nobody sets

Every argument on this page needs an answer to one question: copied at what level of detail? The computations a brain performs, single neurons, individual synapses, sub-neuronal receptors? Each participant needs a setting, hardly anyone argues for theirs, and no reviewed work argues that another's setting is wrong. They simply assume different ones.

The settings that are stated run from the input–output level — "what it does, not how"{{CITE:doerig2019}} — through information-processing computations{{CITE:dehaene2017}} and Marr's algorithmic level{{CITE:butlin2023}}, down through each neuron's functional role{{CITE:cuda1985}}, individual synapses{{CITE:bostrom2003}}, and sub-neuronal detail such as dual-compartment pyramidal neurons and metabotropic receptors{{CITE:aru2023}}. One work maps the axis explicitly as a four-rung ladder and concedes the first three rungs, denying only a connection that is merely counterfactual{{CITE:maudlin1989}} — which is why reading Olympia as an argument against substrate independence gets it backwards.

The figure's real content is the lower half. Nine works deny that this is a scale at all: their claim is about the parameter rather than a setting of it. Two more deny there is any bottom, because metabolism does not stop anywhere{{CITE:godfreysmith2016}}{{CITE:seth2025}}. Two have a grain fall out of their theory's own postulate rather than choosing one{{CITE:tononi2015}}. One commits without argument{{CITE:schwitzgebel2015}}. Offered as a reading, not as anyone's stated claim: this is plausibly the debate whose openness explains the others', since sufficiency, biology and the language-model question each turn on a setting of it. The nearest support is the three works that say only that the parameter cannot be read off the human case{{CITE:shevlin2021}}{{CITE:birch2024}}{{CITE:long2024welfare}}. One empirical route is proposed — causal and informational closure measures{{CITE:seth2025}} — and it has not been run.

07 Who reads whom

Unlike many neighbouring literatures, this field is well connected across its communities: neuroscientists read philosophers and philosophers read neuroscientists, several works are co-authored across communities, and the object of study is shared and concrete. Two debates are nevertheless carried by one community each — implementation and individuation are almost entirely philosophy of computation, and testability is entirely consciousness science — and one strand is carried by a single paper from 1988 with nothing after it{{CITE:dennett1988}}.

The caution that matters most

Some of what looks like agreement is co-authorship. The governance report{{CITE:long2024welfare}} is not independent of the two works it cites for its central claims: both of their authors contributed to it, and it quotes one of their credences verbatim as evidence. The same two people disagree sharply about language models. Separately, the indicator report{{CITE:butlin2023}} and that governance report share authors in both directions. Any figure showing them as two sources for the same conclusion is showing one author group twice, and this page therefore never calls their convergence independent.

08 One verdict, four routes, no shared premise

Four reviewed works conclude that a detailed computer simulation of a brain would not be conscious. It would be easy, and wrong, to draw that as a consensus. The reasons have nothing in common: one says a simulation is virtual while consciousness is real intrinsic cause-effect power{{CITE:tononi2015}}; one that intentionality is a biological phenomenon{{CITE:searle1980}}; one that a virtual machine is not a process that actually has an architecture — reached while defending computationalism{{CITE:klein_maudlin}}; and one that simulating a mechanism is neither implementing nor realising it{{CITE:seth2025}}. The only kinship anyone claims is asserted in an endnote rather than argued.

09 What these works are said to show, against what they say

Each reviewer was asked, for every work in their batch, to check claims routinely attributed to it against the text. Forty-one such claims were checked, and twenty-five do not hold as stated. The pattern is not sloppiness in the literature so much as compression: a paper that kills one narrow thesis gets cited for a broad one.

10 What this collection does not contain

A collection assembled around one thesis has holes, and several of them change how a debate reads. Three are worth naming before the table. The standard challenge to multiple realizability is not held, which is why that premise looks unanimous above. The developed replies to the Chinese Room are held nowhere and the 27 published peer commentaries on it were not reviewed, so the most-engaged work here reads as less answered than it was. And the whole reply literature on Olympia is absent, so this page contains one argument and one retrospective on it with 27 years and an entire exchange in between, visible only as a bibliography.

11 Where things stand in 2026

  • Nothing is settled. Of the {{N_DEBATES}} debates traced here, none is closed. Three lean, and each lean is narrower than it sounds: towards Putnam's mapping construction failing, towards architectural rather than behavioural evidence, and towards no current system being a strong candidate.
  • Two things the collection does agree on. That any recurrent system's bounded input–output behaviour can be reproduced by a feedforward system — stated by both camps{{CITE:tononi2015}}{{CITE:doerig2019}}{{CITE:albantakis2023}}, with the entire disagreement about what follows. And multiple realizability, which is unanimous by absence.
  • The unresolved axis is a dial, and nine of the thirty-one works that address it say the dial is the wrong picture. Nothing here measures it; one empirical route is proposed and has not been run.
  • A fourth position is easy to miss. Alongside "the duplicate feels something", "it doesn't" and "we can't tell", one work argues the question has no determinate answer{{CITE:dennett1988}}. Every work from 2015 on presupposes there is a determinate fact about whether a given system is conscious, and none of them engages the denial — a thirty-seven-year silence in this record, not a settled question.
  • The field is almost entirely argument, not data. One reviewed work turns on measurements, and it answers a different question{{CITE:piccinini2013}}.

12 Why it matters for this project

Interpretive, not a finding

Nothing in these works is about this project's agents, and nothing here shows that an artificial agent has experiences. The project's agents receive an internal damage signal called nociception; pain is the thing being explained, never the label for the signal. Turning any point below into an experiment is a question for the project's pain-science reviewer and its research triage, not for this page.

The project trains reinforcement-learning agents — agents that learn by trial and error — with a nociception channel, and studies how their behaviour changes when that channel is active. That is a behavioural description, and "pain-like" only in that descriptive sense. Three points from this history bear on how such work should be described.

  • The collection supplies the vocabulary for saying what is not being claimed. The three-way split between simulating a mechanism, implementing it and realising it{{CITE:seth2025}} is the cleanest way to state that a model of a damage signal is a model, not an instance. "Nothing gets wet in a weather forecasting computer."
  • The behaviour-to-architecture shift is a caution about behavioural evidence generally. Reviewed works converge on the view that a trained system's behaviour is weak evidence about its internal states. The argument is about consciousness; the structure of the worry transfers to any behavioural signature read off a trained agent.
  • The collection offers no ladder these agents could be climbing. The grain debate asks how finely a brain must be copied before the copy inherits its mental properties. These agents are not copies of any nervous system, so adding biological detail to a nociception model does not move an agent along any axis this literature recognises — not because the threshold is far away, but because no threshold here is defined for a system of this kind.

13 Glossary

computational functionalism
The view that what makes a state mental is the computational role it plays, so running the right computation in any material would suffice.
computational sufficiency
The specific claim that any implementation of a certain class of automata would possess a mind. Distinct from computational explanation, which is the scientific programme of explaining cognition computationally.
multiple realizability
The same psychological state type can be realised by many different physical state types.
substrate independence
Mental states can supervene on any of a broad class of physical substrates. Stronger than multiple realizability.
organizational invariance
A property preserved under every change that preserves causal topology — the pattern of interaction among a system's parts, abstracted from what the parts are made of. The strongest of the three rungs.
implementation
The relation connecting an abstract computation to a physical system: on the account used here, the system's causal structure mirrors the computation's formal structure.
individuation
Which computation a system is running, as opposed to whether it is running one at all.
triviality / universal realization
The claim that every ordinary open physical system realizes every finite automaton, which would make the thesis empty.
grain of functional equivalence
How finely a system must be copied before the copy counts as functionally equivalent. The dial of §5.
liberalism / chauvinism
Falsely attributing mental properties to systems, and falsely denying them.
absent, inverted, fading and dancing qualia
Functionally identical systems that feel nothing, or feel something systematically different; a subject whose experience dims during neuron-by-neuron replacement while he reports it unchanged; and a switch that changes experience without changing organisation.
integrated information
A quantity a system specifies above and beyond its parts, on the theory that identifies consciousness with intrinsic cause-effect power rather than with computation.
unfolding argument
Since a recurrent network's behaviour can be reproduced by a feedforward one, theories that identify consciousness with causal structure are either falsified or untestable.
indicator properties
Computational conditions extracted from several theories at once and used to score a system as a better or worse candidate — not as necessary or sufficient conditions.
sentience candidate
A system whose evidence base implies a realistic possibility of sentience that it would be irresponsible to ignore, and is rich enough to identify welfare risks.
gaming problem
A system trained on human-generated data is well placed to satisfy any criterion we publish for sentience, which is what makes published criteria fragile.
nociception
The nervous system's encoding of damage signals, which is not pain. In this project, the agents' injury-reporting channel.
named only
On this page: a work whose PDF is not held, so it was not reviewed and carries no claim here.
