# Computational Functionalism — Part G: The Deflationary Reply

**Topic folder:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/`
**Part of:** a multi-file, neutral review of how the idea of *computational functionalism* developed — roughly, the thesis that mental states are constituted by the computations a system performs, so that running the right computation in any substrate would suffice for having a mind.
**This part:** 1 paper, reviewed 2026-09-21. This batch was added late, after a domain reviewer found that the corpus's coverage of the qualia debate was one-sided in a way that distorts the historical record.
**Reviewer:** literature-reviewer
**Stance:** this file describes positions; it does not adjudicate between them.

## What this part is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question.** Several of the most famous arguments about whether a machine could be conscious run like this: imagine a system that does everything you do — same behaviour, same internal information-processing structure — but built out of something else. Then ask whether the lights are on inside it. The philosopher Ned Block asked it of a body run by tiny men, and of a billion people in China wired together by radio, and said it was at least doubtful that such a thing feels anything ("the absent qualia argument"). The philosopher David Chalmers asked it of a brain whose neurons are swapped for silicon chips one at a time, and argued that the feelings could not quietly drain away or flip over without the person noticing, so the silicon version must feel exactly what you feel. Gregg Cuda ran a similar replacement argument a decade earlier. All of them are in this corpus, in [Part A1](computational_functionalism_lit_review_A1_functionalism_origins.md) and [Part B](computational_functionalism_lit_review_B_substrate_independence.md).

Every one of those arguments takes for granted that there is a definite something whose presence or absence is being argued about — the felt quality of an experience, the *quale*: what the redness of red is like for you, what the taste of this coffee is like for you. **This part covers the paper that denies exactly that.**

**Why it matters for the history of computational functionalism.** Daniel Dennett's "Quining Qualia" (1988) argues that the ordinary, pre-theoretical idea of a felt quality — the thing the thought experiments are arguing about — does not survive contact with careful cases. It is not that he thinks the silicon brain has qualia, or that the Chinese nation does. It is that he thinks the question has no well-formed answer, because the concept it is asked with pulls apart into two incompatible things the moment you press it. The verb in his title comes from a joke dictionary of philosophers' eponyms: **to quine** is "to deny resolutely the existence or importance of something real or significant."

Without this paper in the corpus, the absent-qualia intuition reads like a shared starting point that the field argues *from*. With it, the record shows that the starting point was itself contested from within philosophy of mind, by an opponent who was writing at the same time as the people making those arguments — and who is thanked in Block's company in the paper's own acknowledgments.

**What Dennett concedes, and what he denies.** This is the thing most easily got wrong, so it is worth stating before anything else. He does **not** deny that conscious experience occurs. In his own words on the first page: "since I don't deny the reality of conscious experience, I grant that conscious experience has properties," and further, "whenever someone experiences something as being one way rather than another, this is true in virtue of some property of something happening in them at the time." What he denies is that any of those properties is *special* in the four ways the tradition says qualia are special — **ineffable** (unsayable), **intrinsic** (non-relational, not a matter of how you are disposed to react), **private** (not comparable between people), and **directly apprehensible** (you cannot be wrong about your own). And he tells us in a footnote that his flat closing line — "there simply are no qualia at all" — is a *tactical* choice about how to talk, of the same kind as a biologist's refusal to keep using "élan vital" as a name for DNA, rather than a denial that anything is going on.

**How he argues.** Not with a proof. "Rigorous arguments only work on well-defined materials," he writes, and his target is precisely a concept he thinks is not well-defined; so the tools are **fifteen numbered intuition pumps** — short imagined cases, each designed to make one assumption visible and then unattractive. The two that do the most work are a pair of brain surgeries with identical results from the inside (did your colours invert, or did only your memory of yesterday's colours invert? — you cannot tell, and neither can the neuroscientists), and two coffee tasters, Mr Chase and Mr Sanborn, who both stopped liking the coffee and disagree about whether it was the coffee-taste or their taste in coffee that changed.

**One line on where it leaves the corpus's other arguments.** It attacks a *premise about introspection* that Chalmers's argument needs and that Cuda's argument treats as obviously true — that a rational, healthy person is generally right about their own experience. It attacks the *presumption of innocence* behind Block's doubt, quoting Block by name. It leaves every one of the thought-experimental machines standing; what it removes is the thing they were supposed to be missing. And it predates Chalmers's paper by seven years, so nothing in it is a reply to him — the clash is a clash of premises stated in the opposite order in time, not an exchange.

## 1. Dennett (1988) — Quining Qualia [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Dennett 1988 - Quining qualia.pdf` — 27 pages. **Author web version**: a browser printout (Safari → Mac OS X Quartz PDFContext, dated 5 February 2009) of `http://ase.tufts.edu/cogstud/papers/quinqual.htm`, Dennett's page on the Tufts Cognitive Studies site. No book pagination. The PDF's own header line gives the source publication as "in A. Marcel and E. Bisiach, eds, Consciousness in Modern Science, Oxford University Press 1988", with reprints in W. Lycan, ed., *Mind and Cognition: A Reader* (MIT Press, 1990) and A. Goldman, ed., *Readings in Philosophy and Cognitive Science* (MIT Press, 1993). The batch assignment and the standard citation name the OUP volume *Consciousness in Contemporary Science*; the discrepancy is recorded, not resolved. **PDF version: author web version.**

**Version history, from the paper's own endnote 14 (PDF p. 23).** The paper had a long pre-publication life, which is worth recording because it bears on who had seen it when. A first version was read at University College, London, in November 1978 and "circulated widely as Tufts University Cognitive Science Working Paper #7, December 1979"; it was never published. A second version was presented at Adelaide and Sydney in 1984, and in 1985 at Harvard and Brown under the title "Properties of Conscious Experience". That second version was the basis of Dennett's presentation at the Villa Olmo workshop (Como, Italy, April 1985) out of which the Marcel and Bisiach volume came, and was circulated in preprint in 1985 under the present title. "The present version, the fourth, is a substantial revision", thanking among others **Ned Block**, Sydney Shoemaker, Kathleen Akins, Peter Bieri, William Lycan, Paul Churchland, Alan Cowey and Gilbert Harman. So the argument was in circulation from 1979 and in something close to its final form from 1985.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Dennett opens by fixing what he is talking about with examples rather than a definition, because he thinks no definition is available: "Look at a glass of milk at sunset; the way it looks to you — the particular, personal, subjective visual quality of the glass of milk is the *quale* of your visual experience at the moment" (PDF p. 1). Qualia are, in the phrase he uses throughout, "the ways things seem to us". They are supposed to be the one thing you cannot be wrong about: "let the entire universe be some vast illusion, some mere figment of Descartes' evil demon, and yet what the figment is made of (for you) will be the qualia of your hallucinatory experiences."

Then the title's joke, which is also its thesis. "Quine" here is a verb from *The Philosophical Lexicon*, the satirical dictionary of philosopher-eponyms Dennett edits: "**quine**, v. To deny resolutely the existence or importance of something real or significant." He announces the goal as "subversive" and the quarry as "frustratingly elusive; no sooner does it retreat in the face of one argument than 'it' reappears, apparently innocent of all charges, in a new guise" (PDF p. 1).

**The concession, stated before the attack.** Immediately after announcing the demolition, he marks off what he is not demolishing. He does not deny the reality of conscious experience; he grants that conscious experience has properties; he grants that "each person's states of consciousness have properties in virtue of which those states have the experiential content that they do" (PDF p. 1). The claim is narrower and stranger than "there is no experience": it is that "conscious experience has no properties that are special in any of the ways qualia have been supposed to be special."

**The burden-shift.** The standard reaction, he says, is to concede that *someone else* may have a fanatical notion of qualia while one's own is "modest, innocent". "It is just that presumption of innocence I want to overthrow. I want to shift the burden of proof, so that anyone who wants to appeal to private, subjective properties has to prove first that in so doing they are not making a mistake" (PDF pp. 1–2). The model is a term that once looked respectable and is now a warning: "no biologist would dream of supposing that it was quite all right to appeal to some innocent concept of élan vital. Of course one could use the term to mean something in good standing; one could use élan vital as one's name for DNA, for instance, but this would be foolish nomenclature."

**The method.** "Rigorous arguments only work on well-defined materials, and since my goal is to destroy our faith in the pretheoretical or 'intuitive' concept, the right tools for my task are intuition pumps, not formal arguments" (PDF p. 2). Fifteen of them follow, in a declared sequence: §2's two pumps exhibit the traditional notion; §3's four build a "paradox" out of it — "not a formal paradox, but only a very powerful argument pitted against some almost irresistibly attractive ideas"; §4's six dissolve the ideas that create the paradox; §5 tests the concept against real neurological cases; §6's three supply replacements.

**Key findings.** Three results, in the order the paper reaches them.

1. **No subject can tell, from the inside, whether his own qualia have changed.** Two different brain operations — inverting an early colour channel, versus inverting only the memory links by which you compare today's colours to yesterday's — produce exactly the same experience of waking up to a wrong-coloured world, while only one of them (by hypothesis) inverts the qualia. So on waking you should say: "Egad! Something has happened! Either my qualia have been inverted or my memory-linked qualia-reactions have been inverted. I wonder which!" (PDF p. 6). The much-praised move from comparing two people to comparing one person's yesterday and today turns out to be no improvement: the memory link plays exactly the role the imaginary cable played between two heads.
2. **Complete neuroscience does not fix this.** In the case of a patient who has fully adapted to inverted taste-bud connections, a finished physiological theory still will not say whether the compensating memory-comparison step happens *before* the qualia or *after* them, and the patient's own opinion cannot be used to decide, because his opinion was the thing being checked (PDF p. 11). Chase and Sanborn are therefore best seen "not as introspectors capable of a privileged view of these properties, but as **autopsychologists**, theorists whose convictions about the properties of their own nervous systems are based" on inference from remembered events. Your epistemic relation to your own qualia turns out to be the relation you have to room temperature or to whether you are running a fever.
3. **The escape route costs intrinsicality.** Suppose you avoid all this by saying that a change in how you *react* to a taste just is a change in the taste — Chase's wife's line, "Once you add the dislike you change the experience!" Then qualitative properties "cease to be 'intrinsic' properties, and in fact become paradigmatically extrinsic, relational properties" (PDF p. 13). Either way a supposedly essential feature is lost, and there is no fact about which way the everyday concept was meant to go: "We normally think in a confused and potentially incoherent way when we think about the ways things seem to us" (PDF p. 14).

**Initial takeaway.** The paper's significance for a history of computational functionalism is not that it settles whether machines can be conscious; it does not address machines, programs, substrate or multiple realizability at all. Its significance is that it attacks the *shared object* of the absent-qualia and inverted-qualia arguments. Block, Cuda and (later) Chalmers disagree about whether a functional duplicate has qualia; all three agree there is a determinate question. Dennett's position is the fourth corner of that debate — the one that says the argument has no subject matter, and that the right response to "we can handle those qualia you talk about just fine" was never a better theory of qualia but the question "**What qualia?**" (PDF p. 4). That fourth corner has been missing from this corpus.

### Phase 2: Argument reconstruction and technical detail

#### 2.1 What is conceded and what is denied

Because this is the point most often misreported, the concessions are collected here with page references, in the paper's own words.

**Conceded.**
- That conscious experience is real: "I don't deny the reality of conscious experience" (PDF p. 1).
- That it has properties: "I grant that conscious experience has properties" (PDF p. 1).
- That experiential content is grounded in occurrent properties of the subject: "whenever someone experiences something as being one way rather than another, this is true in virtue of some property of something happening in them at the time" (PDF p. 1).
- That experiences are **practically ineffable**: "Why does it seem that our conscious experiences have ineffable properties? Because they do have practically ineffable properties" (PDF p. 18).
- That there is a **residual first-person authority**: "we can have some authority — not infallibility or incorrigibility, but something better than sheer guessing — but only if we restrict ourselves to relational, extrinsic properties" (PDF p. 22).
- That people really do experience the world differently from one another: "And they may not be; people experience the world quite differently. But that is empirically discoverable by all the usual objective testing procedures" (PDF p. 20).

**Denied.**
- That any property of experience is special in the four traditional ways — ineffable, intrinsic, private, directly apprehensible (PDF pp. 1, 4, 22).
- That a subject has privileged access to whether his own qualia have changed (PDF pp. 6–7, 11–12).
- That "intrinsic" is currently a usable notion at all: "the insistence that qualia are the intrinsic properties of experience is an empty gesture at best" (PDF p. 18).
- That the classical inverted-spectrum hypothesis is coherent: "Spectrum inversion as classically debated is impossible, but something like it is perfectly possible" (PDF p. 15).

**The status of the slogan.** Endnote 2 (PDF p. 22) is essential and is easy to miss: "The difference between 'eliminative materialism' — of which my position on qualia is an instance — and a 'reductive' materialism that takes on the burden of identifying the problematic item in terms of the foundational materialistic theory is thus often best seen not so much as a doctrinal issue as a **tactical** issue: how might we most gracefully or effectively enlighten the confused in this instance?" Read with the main text's "Far better, **tactically**, to declare that there simply are no qualia at all" (PDF p. 2), the paper's headline denial is explicitly offered as a recommendation about vocabulary, made on the grounds that the term's confusions are not worth the salvage. A reader wanting to know whether Dennett is denying that there is something it is like to see red should weigh this endnote against the closing sentence; the paper contains both and does not obviously reconcile them.

#### 2.2 The four second-order properties, and why no milder version is available

The definition the paper attacks is assembled on PDF p. 4 and is quoted here in full, since every later step refers back to it. Qualia are supposed to be properties of a subject's mental states that are:

> (1) ineffable
> (2) intrinsic
> (3) private
> (4) directly or immediately apprehensible in consciousness

Each is derived, not stipulated. **Ineffability**: "since one cannot say to another, no matter how eloquent one is and no matter how cooperative and imaginative one's audience is, exactly what way one is currently seeing, tasting, smelling and so forth, qualia are ineffable — in fact the paradigm cases of ineffable items." **Intrinsicality** is offered by the tradition as *the explanation* of ineffability: qualia are ineffable because they are intrinsic, "which seems to imply inter alia that they are somehow atomic and unanalyzable. Since they are 'simple' or 'homogeneous' there is nothing to get hold of when trying to describe such a property." **Privacy** follows from the failure of every objective cross-check, verbal or physiological — "all interpersonal comparisons of these ways-of-appearing are (apparently) systematically impossible". **Direct apprehensibility** follows from their being the properties by which we identify our own conscious states.

Dennett then states why the tradition has cared: qualia "have seemed to provide an insurmountable and unavoidable stumbling block to functionalism, or more broadly, to materialism, or more broadly still, to any purely 'third-person' objective viewpoint or approach to the world (Nagel, 1986)" (PDF p. 4). And he faults his own side for its response: "Theorists of the contrary persuasion have patiently and ingeniously knocked down all the arguments, and said most of the right things, but they have made a tactical error, I am claiming, of saying in one way or another: 'We theorists can handle those qualia you talk about just fine; we will show that you are just slightly in error about the nature of qualia.' What they ought to have said is: 'What qualia?'"

**The anticipated strawman charge, answered in advance.** The obvious reply is that the fourfold definition is a caricature — "Who said qualia are ineffable, intrinsic, private, directly apprehensible ways things seem to one?" Dennett tests the milder alternative on offer, Shoemaker's: qualia are simply "the qualitative or phenomenal features of sense experience[s], in virtue of having which they resemble and differ from each other, qualitatively, in the ways they do" (Shoemaker 1982, p. 367, quoted at PDF p. 4). His reply is a dilemma about the word "phenomenal". Shoemaker contrasts *qualitative* similarity with *intentional* similarity — similarity in what the experience is of. Fine; but then:

- The **physiological** properties of a visual state are non-intentional too. Are they the qualia? No, Shoemaker rules them out as not accessible to introspection — "These are features of my visual state, perhaps, but not of my visual experience."
- But another non-intentional property of some of Dennett's visual states is "that they tend to make me think about going to bed", and that *is* accessible to introspection "on any ordinary, pre-theoretical construal". Is it phenomenal?

Either answer is bad for the mild version: include it and "phenomenal" means nothing more than non-intentional-and-noticeable, which nobody wants; exclude it and the exclusion can only be justified by reaching back for ineffability, intrinsicality and direct apprehension. "The term 'phenomenal' means nothing obvious and untendentious to me, and looks suspiciously like a gesture in the direction leading back to ineffable, private, directly apprehensible ways things seem to one" (PDF p. 5).

**A diagnosis of the resistance.** "Qualia seem to many people to be the last ditch defense of the inwardness and elusiveness of our minds, a bulwark against creeping mechanism. They are sure there must be some sound path from the homely cases to the redoubtable category of the philosophers, since otherwise their last bastion of specialness will be stormed by science" (PDF p. 5). He locates his position relative to Wittgenstein's beetle-in-the-box — "one can 'divide through' by the thing in the box; it cancels out, whatever it is" (*Philosophical Investigations* p. 100), hedged by Wittgenstein's "It is not a something, but not a nothing either!" (p. 102) — and takes "what may well be a more radical stand than Wittgenstein's": qualia "is a philosophers' term which fosters nothing but confusion, and refers in the end to no properties or features at all" (PDF p. 5).

#### 2.3 The master argument, reconstructed

The paper is a sequence of pumps rather than a numbered argument, but it has a determinate structure, and the pumps are announced as serving specified roles. The reconstruction below uses Dennett's own connective claims; the numbering is this review's.

- **P1 (the target).** The pre-theoretical concept of qualia is the concept of properties of a subject's mental states that are ineffable, intrinsic, private and directly apprehensible (PDF p. 4; pumps #1–#2 exhibit it).
- **P2 (no milder version is available).** Any attempt to retreat to a blander notion of "qualitative or phenomenal features" either admits properties nobody will call phenomenal, or excludes them only by re-invoking (1)–(4) (PDF pp. 4–5; §2.2 above).
- **P3 (the introspective squeeze).** For any introspectively detectable change in how things seem, there exist at least two hypotheses with identical introspective consequences — one on which the qualia changed, one on which only the memory-comparison or reaction links changed. Hence "nothing in the subject's experience can favor one of the hypotheses over the other" (PDF p. 7; pumps #4, #5, #6).
- **P4 (the third-person squeeze).** Nor does a completed physiology decide between them: it cannot say at which stage "in the stream of physiological process twixt tasting and telling to draw the line at which the putative qualia appear", and the subject's verdict cannot be used to settle it, since his reliability was the thing under test (PDF p. 11; pump #8).
- **C1 (property (4) fails).** "far from being directly or immediately apprehensible properties of our experience, they are properties whose changes or constancies are either entirely beyond our ken, or inferrable (at best) from 'third-person' examinations of our behavioral and physiological reaction patterns" (PDF p. 12). Whatever such third-person-accessible properties are, "they are not qualia, for the simple reason that one's epistemic relation to them is exactly the same as one's epistemic relation to such external, but readily — if fallibly — detectable, properties as room temperature or weight."
- **P5 (the other horn).** Suppose instead we abandon the doctrine "that sharply distinguished qualia from their (normal) effects on reactions" — Chase's wife's advice, made vivid by the experienced beer drinker (#9). Then "if it is admitted that one's attitudes towards, or reactions to, experiences are in any way and in any degree constitutive of their experiential qualities, so that a change in reactivity amounts to or guarantees a change in the property, then those properties … cease to be 'intrinsic' properties, and in fact become paradigmatically extrinsic, relational properties" (PDF p. 13).
- **C2 (property (2) fails on that horn).** Reinforced by the independent observation that seemingly-intrinsic perceptual properties are standardly relational: phenol-thio-urea (#10), the felt value of money, public redness since Boyle and Locke (PDF pp. 13, 17–18).
- **P6 (the forced choice, and the absence of a fact about it).** The two horns are exhaustive and the pre-theoretical concept selects neither. "we have to ask Chase which way he wants to go, and there really are two drastically different alternatives available to him if we force the issue. … if you complain that this forced choice drives apart two aspects that you had supposed united in your pretheoretic concept, you support my contention that there is no secure foundation in ordinary 'folk psychology' for a concept of qualia" (PDF p. 14). Note the argumentative form: the reader's own hesitation is taken as the evidence. This is why the chapter needs pumps rather than premises — the datum it appeals to is the reader's failure to have a determinate intuition.
- **P7 (independent collapse of "intrinsic").** Even setting the dilemma aside, the second-order property of intrinsicality is not in usable shape. Lewis (1983) surveys the accounts and fails by his own admission; the paradigm classroom example (the redness of Tom's ball) has been known since Boyle and Locke to be relational; and the move of conceding public redness is relational while relocating intrinsicality "into the subject's head" is question-begging, because the parallel relational treatment of monetary value requires no items of intrinsic value (PDF pp. 17–18).
- **P8 (the real cases confirm the disarray).** Four published cases of cerebral achromatopsia each admit both a qualia-intact and a qualia-drained description on identical data, and philosophers asked to describe them fall into "strident (and ultimately comic) disagreement" (PDF pp. 15–17).
- **C3 (the conclusion).** "when we look one last time at our original characterization of qualia, as ineffable, intrinsic, private, directly apprehensible properties of experience, we find that there is nothing to fill the bill. … So contrary to what seems obvious at first blush, there simply are no qualia at all" (PDF p. 22) — with the tactical qualification of endnote 2 attached (§2.1 above).

The constructive half (§2.8 below) is not a further premise but a burden the paper takes on: having denied the four properties, it owes an explanation of why they seemed to be there.

#### 2.4 The fifteen intuition pumps, and what each is for

Dennett announces the division of labour on PDF p. 2: §2's pumps "focus attention on the traditional notion"; §3's four "create and refine a 'paradox' lurking in the tradition"; §4's six "are arrayed in order to dissipate the attractiveness of those ideas"; §5 shows "how hapless those ideas prove to be when confronted with some real cases"; §6's three "introduce and motivate some suitable replacements".

| # | Name | PDF p. | What it is meant to show |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Watching you eat cauliflower | 2 | That the homely, undeniable cases (the same food tasting different to two people, or to one person after maple syrup) tempt us into one further step — isolating "the way it tastes" from its causes, accompaniments and downstream effects — and that the step, not the cases, is the mistake. "The mistake is not in supposing that we can in practice ever or always perform this act of purification with certainty, but the more fundamental mistake of supposing that there is such a residual property to take seriously." |
| 2 | The wine-tasting machine | 3 | That the intuition is not about complexity of discrimination. A Gallo Brothers expert system with chemical transducers might out-perform human tasters — "a flamboyant and velvety Pinot, though lacking in stamina" — and still "surely no matter how 'sensitive' and 'discriminating' such a system becomes, it will never have, and enjoy, what we do when we taste a wine: the qualia of conscious experience!" Dennett's use of this is diagnostic, not concessive: "**If you share that intuition, you believe that there are qualia in the sense I am targeting for demolition.**" |
| 3 | The inverted spectrum | 5 | The Lockean original (1690, II.xxxii.15): two people whose colour words match though their subjective colours are swapped. Its point in the sequence is historical — the hypothesis that verificationism was supposed to have killed. |
| 4 | The Brainstorm machine | 5 | That technology cannot rescue interpersonal comparison. A device feeding your visual experience into my brain lets me report your sky as yellow — but the technician can invert the plug, and "Which is the 'right' orientation of the plug?" Calibrating the device requires normalising the two subjects' reports, "so we would be right back at our evidential starting point. The moral of this intuition pump is that no intersubjective comparison of qualia is possible, even with perfect technology." |

#### 2.5 The two pivots in detail: alternative neurosurgery and the post-operative recovery

**Pump #6, alternative neurosurgery (PDF p. 6).** The evil neurosurgeon has (at least) two ways to produce the waking-up-to-inverted-colours effect of #5:

> (I) Invert one of the "early" qualia-producing channels, e.g., in the optic nerve, so that all relevant neural events "downstream" are the "opposite" of their original and normal values. Ex hypothesi this inverts your qualia.
>
> (II) Leave all those early pathways intact and simply invert certain memory-access links — whatever it is that accomplishes your tacit (and even unconscious!) comparison of today's hues with those of yore. Ex hypothesi this does not invert your qualia at all, but just your memory-anchored dispositions to react to them.

The inference has two steps. First, the correct thing for the victim to say on waking is a disjunction, not a report: "Egad! Something has happened! Either my qualia have been inverted or my memory-linked qualia-reactions have been inverted. I wonder which!" Second, and this is the load-bearing step, the constructors of intrapersonal-inversion thought experiments have assumed the subject's noticing — "surely a vivid experience of discovery by the subject" — must itself be a case of recognising a *shift in qualia*. "But as my example shows, we could achieve the same startling effect in a subject without tampering with his presumed qualia at all. Since ex hypothesi the two different surgical invasions can produce exactly the same introspective effects while only one operation inverts the qualia, nothing in the subject's experience can favor one of the hypotheses over the other. So unless he seeks outside help, **the state of his own qualia must be as unknowable to him as the state of anyone else's qualia**. Hardly the privileged access or immediate acquaintance or direct apprehension the friends of qualia had supposed 'phenomenal features' to enjoy!" (PDF p. 7).

And the diagnosis of why the intrapersonal version was mistaken for progress: "the link to earlier experiences, the link via memory, is analogous to the imaginary cable that might link two subjects in the original version" (PDF p. 6). The inner comparison is a comparison across a channel too, and the channel's calibration is exactly as unsettleable as the Brainstorm machine's plug.

**Pump #8, the gradual post-operative recovery (PDF p. 11).** Chase's taste-bud connections have been surgically inverted — sugar tastes salty, salt sour — and he has then *compensated*, "so thorough[ly] that on all behavioral and verbal tests his performance is indistinguishable from that of normal subjects — and from his own pre-surgical performance". The question is whether his qualia are now back to normal. Dennett's answer is that the question is undecidable in principle, not merely in practice:

> If all the internal compensatory adjustment has been accomplished early in the process — intuitively, pre-qualia — then his qualia today are restored to just as they were … If on the other hand some or all of the internal compensatory adjustment is post-qualia, then his qualia have not been renormalized even if he thinks they have. But the physiological facts will not in themselves shed any light on where in the stream of physiological process twixt tasting and telling to draw the line at which the putative qualia appear.

Even granting a complete account on which the compensation is an adjustment to the memory-accessing process, two stories remain: (I) the qualia are still abnormal and the memories have been adjusted to match; (II) the memory-comparison step precedes the qualia phase, so the same stimulation now yields the old qualia. "In (I) the qualia contribute to the input, in effect, to the memory-comparator. In (II) they are part of the output of the memory-comparator." The physiology cannot place the qualia relative to the comparator; the subject cannot either, "since ex hypothesi those stories are not reliably distinguishable by him"; and we cannot fall back on his opinion because "it was in order to confirm or disconfirm Chase's opinion that we turned to the neurophysiological evidence in the first place."

The passage that follows is the one that matters most for the rest of this corpus, because it concerns who is a better judge of your experience than you are (PDF p. 12):

> For the subject's own judgments, like the behaviors or actions that express them, are the resultant of our two postulated factors, and cannot discern the component proportions any better than external behavioral tests can. Indeed, a subject's "introspective" convictions will generally be **worse** evidence than what outside observers can gather. … Chase's intuitive judgments about his qualia constancy are no better off, epistemically, than his intuitive judgments about, say, lighting intensity constancy or room temperature constancy — or his own body temperature constancy. … Is Chase running a fever or just feeling feverish? Unless he has taken steps to calibrate and cross-check his own performance, his opinion that his fever-perception apparatus is undisturbed is no better than a hunch.

The one thing Chase *is* entitled to is the disjunctive fact he shares with Sanborn: "He is on quite firm ground, epistemically, when he reports that the relation between his coffee-sipping activity and his judging activity has changed." The allocation is precise: the relational fact survives, the intrinsic one does not.

**The scientists' ultimatum.** Dennett stages the resulting standoff between the physiologists, who may appropriate "qualia" for whatever family of detectable properties their theory of perceptual recognition and memory happens to need, and the philosophers, who object that those are not the qualia they meant. "The scientists' retort is: 'If we cannot distinguish (I) from (II), we certainly cannot support either of your claims. If you want our support, you must relinquish your concept of qualia'" (PDF pp. 11–12).

**What kind of claim this is.** It should be recorded that P4's undecidability is an *in-principle* claim, and that the paper argues for it by exhibiting cases rather than proving it. Dennett is careful about the limits of the empirical testing he does endorse: extreme cases can be separated (blind tastings and Mill's method of differences for Chase; an "early" anomaly in the taste pathway for Sanborn, on the model of Von der Heydt et al.'s 1984 finding that edge-detector neurons in visual area 18 respond to illusory contours, which pushed illusory contours earlier in the stream than the "top-down" reading had assumed). What cannot be separated is the middle: "such indirect testing cannot be expected to resolve the issue when the effects are relatively small" (PDF pp. 10–11). The example he gives of the ambiguity of even fine-grained data — "Would a longer response latency in Chase today be indicative of a process of 'attempted qualia renormalization' or 'extended aesthetic evaluation'?" — shows the shape of the claim: any measure is a resultant of the two postulated factors, so no measure decomposes it.

#### 2.6 The forced choice, and the other horn

The escape from the epistemic squeeze is to drop the doctrine that separates a quale from its normal effects on reaction — the doctrine Chase relies on when he insists the coffee tastes just the same while his reaction to "that taste" has changed. Dennett's first objection to Chase is internal: "this apprehension or recollection is itself a reaction to the presumed quale, so some sleight-of-hand is being perpetrated — innocently no doubt — by Chase" (PDF p. 13).

Then #9, the experienced beer drinker, shows what the alternative costs. If beer is an acquired taste, what flavour was acquired? Not the flavour of the first sip: "No one could like that flavor." But then "prolonged beer drinking leads people to experience a taste they enjoy, but precisely their enjoying the taste guarantees that it is not the taste they first experienced" — so, strictly, nobody ever acquires a taste for anything. (Endnote 9 records the way to save the ordinary claim: talk of *public* tastes, the taste of Maxwell House that both tasters agree has stayed constant. "Individuals can be said to acquire a taste for such a public taste." Note that this repair is available only on Dennett's own account, where the public property is the real one.)

The general consequence is the second horn: if reactions are in any degree constitutive of experiential quality, qualitative properties become "paradigmatically extrinsic, relational properties". And #10 is there to remove the sense that this is a strange result: seeming-intrinsic perceptual properties routinely turn out relational. Phenol-thio-urea's public bitterness can be created or abolished by a breeding programme that never touches the chemical. Money's felt value seems intrinsic and is not — "How much is that in real money?" — and the paper cites Elster's "tendency to overlook the implicitly relational character of certain monadic predicates" and Walzer's gloss on the ten-dollar bill (PDF pp. 13–14).

The choice is then forced (PDF p. 14), and the argument turns on the reader rather than on a premise: "Which way would you go? Which concept of qualia did you 'always have in the back of your mind,' guiding your imagination as you thought about theories? If you acknowledge that the answer is not obvious, and especially if you complain that this forced choice drives apart two aspects that you had supposed united in your pretheoretic concept, you support my contention that there is no secure foundation in ordinary 'folk psychology' for a concept of qualia."

Dennett's own description of Chase's actual state is worth recording because it is the closest the paper comes to a positive psychological claim about what is really going on: "When Chase thinks of 'that taste' he thinks equivocally or vaguely. He harkens back in memory to earlier experiences but need not try — or be able — to settle whether he is including any or all of his reactions or excluding them from what he intends by 'that taste'. His state then and his state now are different — that he can avow with confidence — but he has no 'immediate' resources for making a finer distinction, nor any need to do so."

#### 2.7 The clinical cases

Section 5 is the paper's one appeal to published empirical material, and it is used as a test of the concept's usefulness rather than as evidence for a thesis. Four case descriptions of cerebral achromatopsia (brain-based loss of colour vision) are quoted at length:

1. **Damasio et al. (1980)** — a patient for whom objects right of the vertical meridian appeared normally coloured while those to the left "were perceived only in shades of gray, though without distortions of form". He could instantly name a colour as soon as any part of the object crossed the meridian. *Acutely aware of his deficit.*
2. **Geschwind and Fusillo (1966)** — a patient who failed every task matching a seen colour to its spoken name, but "succeeded on all tasks where the matching was either purely verbal or purely nonverbal. … He could match seen colors to each other and to pictures of objects and could sort colors without error." *Quite unaware of any deficit*; he "never replied with a simple 'I don't know'".
3. **Meadows (1974)** — "everything looked black or grey"; asked to guess, he was right about half the time; of a bright red object he said "a dirty smudgy red, not as red as you would normally see red." *Awareness of the deficit problematic.*
4. **Damasio et al. (1980)**, second patient — colours "drained out" and "not true" on waking one November morning, later prosopagnosia as well, "remarkable insight about her defects". *Full insight.*

The use made of them is a dilemma about description, run in both directions (PDF p. 17). For the unaware patient, you can say his qualia are intact — "His capacities to discriminate are terribly impaired, but luckily for him, his inner life is untouched by this merely public loss!" — or that he has simply failed to notice his qualia draining away: "So slowly did his inner life lose its complexity and variety that he never noticed how impoverished it had become!" For a patient who complains but tests normally, three descriptions are equally available: her qualia really are washed out; her qualia are fine and she has "a sort of color-vision hypochondria"; or her disorder is purely verbal, an anomalous understanding of the colour words she uses.

Two further observations are made. First, a methodological one in the concept's favour-turned-against-it: having read Geschwind and Fusillo, "we realize that our first patient was never tested to see if he could still sort colors seen on the left … Those tests are by no means superfluous. Perhaps he would have passed them; perhaps, in spite of what he says his qualia are as intact for the left field as for the right!" So the concept does not merely fail to help; Dennett claims it "leads us to overlook genuine possibilities". Second, the reader who finds the "purely verbal" hypothesis impossible is thereby making verbal report sovereign, "but then you must rule out a priori the possibility of the condition I described as color-vision hypochondria".

The conclusion is about the state of the intuitions, not about the patients: "the intuitions that surround and purport to anchor the current understanding of the term are revealed to be in utter disarray when confronted with these cases." His evidence for the disarray is candidly informal — "My informal sampling shows that some philosophers have strong opinions about each case … but they find they are in strident (and ultimately comic) disagreement with other philosophers about how these 'obvious' descriptions should go" (PDF p. 17).

#### 2.8 The constructive replacement: property-detectors, pips, and practical ineffability

Having denied the four properties, the paper owes an explanation of why they seemed to be there. Section 6 supplies one for each, in order.

**Intrinsic → dropped, not replaced.** The treatment is the most deflationary of the four. "It is far from clear what an intrinsic property would be. Although the term has had a certain vogue in philosophy, and often seems to secure an important contrast, there has never been an accepted definition of the second-order property of intrinsicality" (PDF pp. 17–18). Lewis (1983) is used as the exhibit: he opens his survey by listing the option "We could Quine the lot, give over the entire family as unintelligible and dispensable", dismisses it immediately — "That would be absurd" (p. 197) — and ends, having failed to rescue Chisholm's and Kim's accounts, with "if we still want to break in we had best try another window" (p. 200). Add the fact that the intuitive classroom example of an intrinsic property, the redness of a ball, is "a quintessentially relational property … Ever since Boyle and Locke we have known better", and the seductive move becomes visible: on learning that public redness is relational, "cling to intrinsicality ('something has to be intrinsic!') and move it into the subject's head." Dennett's reply is a demand for an argument: "Why not another relational or extrinsic property? The relational treatment of monetary value does not require, for its completion, the supposition of items of intrinsic value." Verdict: "Why not give up intrinsicality as a second-order property altogether, at least pending resolution of the disarray of philosophical opinion about what intrinsicality might be?"

**Ineffable → practically ineffable, and explained.** Pump **#13, the osprey cry** (PDF pp. 18–19). Dennett has read the field-guide description — "a series of short, sharp, cheeping whistles, cheep, cheep or chewk chewk, etc; sounds annoyed" (Peterson 1947) — which "gives me a partial confinement of the logical space of possible bird cries" but leaves the actual cry hidden. Then he hears one: *So that's what it sounds like*, and he seems to be ostending an ineffable quale, which he dubs "S" ("pace Wittgenstein").

The deflation is epistemic and comes in two moves. First, from a single hearing he cannot generalise — "Would a cry that differed only in being half an octave higher also be an osprey call?" is ornithology, and which physical variations would produce an indistinguishable experience in him is psychology; neither is settled by the experience. This is "the **inscrutability of projection**", offered as "surely one of the sources of plausibility for Wittgenstein's skepticism regarding the possibility of a private language" (with Kripke 1982, p. 83 quoted on ostensive definitions always being capable of being misunderstood). Second, and crucially, the experience is still informative: "when first I hear the osprey cry, I may have identified a property-detector in myself, but I have no idea (yet) what property my newfound property-detector detects. It might seem then that I know nothing new at all … But of course this is not so. I may not be able to describe the property or identify it relative to any readily usable public landmarks (yet), but I am acquainted with it in a modest way: I can refer to the property I detected: **it is the property I detected in that event**."

Pump **#14, the Jello box** (PDF pp. 19–20) explains why this kind of reference resists elimination. The Rosenberg spy trick — tear a Jello box in two and give half to each party — works "because tearing the paper in two produces an edge of such informational complexity that it would be virtually impossible to reproduce by deliberate construction. (Cutting the Jello box with straight edge and razor would entirely defeat the purpose.)" The jagged half is "an apparatus for detecting the shape property M, where M is uniquely instantiated by its mate", and it is *of the essence of the trick* that no description could replace the dummy predicate "M": a sufficient description would be a recipe for forging another instance. "The only readily available way of saying what property M is is just to point to our M-detector and say that M is the shape property detected by this thing here."

The application: "And that is just what we do when we seem to ostend, with the mental finger of inner intention, a quale or qualia-complex in our experience. We refer to a property — a **public** property of uncharted boundaries — via reference to our personal and idiosyncratic capacity to respond to it."

**Private → idiosyncratic, and empirically checkable.** "That idiosyncracy is the extent of our privacy. If I wonder whether your blue is my blue, your middle-C is my middle-C, I can coherently be wondering whether our discrimination profiles over a wide variation in conditions will be approximately the same. And they may not be; people experience the world quite differently. But that is empirically discoverable by all the usual objective testing procedures" (PDF p. 20). This is the clearest statement of what survives translation: the interpersonal question becomes a real question with a testable answer, at the price of being about discrimination profiles rather than about inner glows.

**Pips.** Dennett credits **Peter Bieri** with a reformulation of the first three second-order properties using Dretske's (1981) sense of information — natural meaning — flagging that he has published objections to Dretske's larger programme which he takes to be irrelevant here. Call them "phenomenal information properties", or **pips**: that an internal perceptual event type carries certain information "is an objective (and hence, in a loose sense, intrinsic) matter since it is independent of what information (if any) the subject takes the event type to carry"; exactly what information is carried is practically ineffable, for the osprey/Jello-box reasons; and it is private in the sense of "proprietary and potentially idiosyncratic" (PDF p. 20).

Applied to Chase and Sanborn, this turns their speeches into testable hypotheses: both "ought to wonder whether their pips have changed". Chase believes his are unchanged — that chemically identical caffeine-rich fluids give rise to the same taste-experience types now as six years ago. Sanborn believes his detectors are deranged. Dennett adds a nice observation about what a pip is a detector *of*: "most of us treat our taste-detectors as mere M-detectors, detecting whatever-it-is that they detect. … But professional coffee-tasters are probably different. They probably have some pretty good idea of what kind of chemical-analysis transduction machinery they have in their mouths and nervous systems" (PDF p. 20).

**What makes a pip "phenomenal", and the inner-eye trap.** A pip has "the power to provoke in Chase and Sanborn acts of (apparent) re-identification or recognition. This power is of course a Lockean, dispositional property on a par with the power of bitter things to provoke a certain reaction in people. It is this power alone, however it might be realized in the brain, that gives Chase and Sanborn 'access' to the deliverances of their individual property-detectors" (PDF p. 21). The obvious next question — but what are the consciously apprehensible properties of the deliverance itself? — is answered by refusing it: "if we invoke an inner perceptual process in which we observe the deliverance with some inner eye and thereby discern its properties, we will be stepping back into the frying pan of the view according to which qualia are just ordinary properties of our inner states. But nothing requires us to make such an invocation. We don't have to know how we identify or re-identify or gain access to such internal response types in order to be able so to identify them." That point is credited to "the pioneer functionalists and materialists" — Farrell (1950) and Smart (1959) — "and has never been rebutted", together with the distinction it rests on: "The properties of the 'thing experienced' are not to be confused with the properties of the event that realizes the experiencing." The vivid version: "the physical difference between someone's imagining a purple cow and imagining a green cow might be nothing more than the presence or absence of a particular zero or one in one of the brain's 'registers'. Such a brute physical presence is all that it would take to anchor the sorts of dispositional differences … (I doubt that this is what the friends of qualia have had in mind when they have insisted that qualia are intrinsic properties.)"

**Directly apprehensible / grainless → functionally invariable, and plastic.** The last of the four is explained as an artifact of our own limits: "it is our very inability to expand on, or modify, these brute dispositions so to identify or recognize such states that creates the doctrinal illusion of 'homogeneity' or 'atomicity to analysis' or 'grainlessness' that characterizes the qualia of philosophical tradition. This putative grainlessness, I hypothesize, is nothing but a sort of functional invariability: it is close kin to what Pylyshyn (1980, 1984) calls **cognitive impenetrability**" (PDF p. 21). And the key empirical claim: the invariability "is not absolute but itself plastic over time", with an efferent-side analogy — biofeedback training decomposes what had been a "basic action" (Danto 1963, 1965; Goldman 1970) — and afferent-side evidence from the education of a wine taster's palate and musicians' ear training: "What had been 'atomic' or 'unanalyzable' becomes noticeably compound and describable; pairs that had been indistinguishable become distinguishable, and when this happens we say the experience changes."

Pump **#15, the guitar string** (PDF pp. 21–22) is the demonstration the reader can run in a few seconds. Pluck the open low E and the sound is "one and whole and ineffably guitarish". Pluck it again while touching the octave fret and a "purer" harmonic appears — some hearers say the note is entirely new, others that "the bottom fell out of the note". Then pluck open a third time: "one can hear, with surprising distinctness, the harmonic overtone that was isolated in the second plucking. The homogeneity and ineffability of the first experience is gone, replaced by a duality as 'directly apprehensible' and clearly describable as that of any chord." The analytical point is the pip/experience split: "the complexity apprehended on the third plucking was there all along (being responded to or discriminated). After all, it was by the complex pattern of overtones that you were able to recognize the sound as that of a guitar rather than a lute or harpsichord. In other words, although the subjective experience has changed dramatically, **the pip hasn't changed**."

And the guard against over-reading: "At any time, of course, there is one's current horizon of distinguishability — and that horizon is what sets, if anything does, what we should call the primary or atomic properties of what one consciously experiences (Farrell, 1950). But it would be a mistake to transform the fact that inevitably there is a limit to our capacity to describe things we experience into the supposition that there are absolutely indescribable properties in our experience."

#### 2.10 What this does to the arguments in this corpus

This section states, as precisely as the text allows, which premises of the corpus's absent/fading/dancing-qualia arguments the paper targets, and which papers it engages **by name**. Where it does not engage, that is said plainly rather than implied.

**Engaged by name.**

- **Block (1978), *Troubles with Functionalism*** — reviewed in [Part A1](computational_functionalism_lit_review_A1_functionalism_origins.md) §3. Cited twice. (i) On PDF p. 2, Block's deflection of the question "What are qualia, exactly?" by invoking Louis Armstrong — "If you got to ask, you ain't never gonna get to know" (Block 1978, p. 281) — is quoted as *the* exhibit of the presumption Dennett is attacking: "This amusing tactic perfectly illustrates the presumption that is my target. If I succeed in my task, this move, which passes muster in most circles today, will look as quaint and insupportable as a jocular appeal to the ludicrousness of a living thing — a living thing, mind you! — doubting the existence of élan vital." (ii) On PDF p. 4, Block's phrase "immediate phenomenological qualities" is listed among the formulations Dennett answers with a parenthetical "(whatever that means)".

  **What is and is not targeted.** Block's Absent Qualia Argument, as reconstructed in Part A1, has as its P3 the claim that "there is *prima facie* doubt whether the homunculi-headed system has qualia — whether … 'there is anything which it is like to be' it", from which C1 follows: doubt about the system's mentality is doubt that the qualitative state is identical to the machine-table state. Dennett's attack is aimed at the concept in which that doubt is phrased, not at the homunculi construction. **The homunculi-headed robot and the Chinese nation are not discussed anywhere in this paper.** Nor are Block's other two lines of argument — the liberalism/chauvinism dilemma and the problem of the inputs and the outputs — which do not depend on qualia and are left entirely untouched.

  The structurally closest thing in Dennett's text is pump **#2, the wine-tasting machine**, which has the same shape as the absent-qualia intuition applied to a machine rather than to a functional duplicate: however good the discrimination gets, "it will never have, and enjoy, what we do when we taste a wine". Dennett's use of it is the exact inverse of Block's: where Block treats the doubt as data requiring explanation, Dennett treats sharing the intuition as a symptom — "If you share that intuition, you believe that there are qualia in the sense I am targeting for demolition." **He does not claim the wine-tasting machine has qualia.** His position implies that the question, as asked, has no determinate answer, which withholds the negative verdict as much as the positive one.

  Block (1980), "Are Absent Qualia Impossible?", appears in the bibliography and in endnote 1's "representative sample of the most recent literature on qualia", but is not discussed.

- **Block & Fodor (1972), *What Psychological States Are Not*** — reviewed in [Part A1](computational_functionalism_lit_review_A1_functionalism_origins.md) §1. Cited once, at the historical hinge of §3: after listing the independent inventors of the intrapersonal inverted spectrum, Dennett writes "Probably Block and Fodor (1972) have it in mind when they say 'It seems to us that the standard verificationist counterarguments against the view that the "inverted spectrum" hypothesis is conceptually incoherent are not persuasive.' (p. 172)" (PDF p. 6).

  **The premise targeted** is precisely the one that quotation expresses: that moving the comparison inside one head answers the verificationist objection. Pump #6 is designed to show it does not — "the link to earlier experiences, the link via memory, is analogous to the imaginary cable that might link two subjects in the original version." On Dennett's reading, the rehabilitation of qualia as respectable properties in the 1960s and 70s rested on this move, and the move fails.

**Not engaged, and why the reader should not read a reply into it.**

- **Chalmers (1995), *Absent Qualia, Fading Qualia, Dancing Qualia*** — reviewed in [Part B](computational_functionalism_lit_review_B_substrate_independence.md) §3. **This paper predates Chalmers's by seven years and contains nothing about it.** Any relation between them is a relation between positions, not an exchange, and the arrow of influence (if any) runs the other way.

  With that said, the corpus needs the premise-level relation recorded, because it is exact. Chalmers's Fading Qualia argument concludes by weighing two hypotheses against one another: "the possibility of Fading Qualia requires either a bizarre relationship between belief contents and physical states, or the possibility of beings that are massively mistaken about their own conscious experiences despite being fully rational. Both of these hypotheses are significantly less plausible than the hypothesis that rational conscious beings are generally correct in their judgments about their experiences." The last clause is the load-bearing premise, and Chalmers defends it explicitly against the obvious counterexample: in blindness denial "anything goes", because those systems' belief-forming mechanisms are impaired, whereas Joe "is an eminently thoughtful, reasonable person, who exhibits none of the confabulatory symptoms" — hence the refined claim that "no *rational* system whose cognitive mechanisms are unimpaired can be so mistaken."

  Dennett's §§3–4 argue for the denial of that refined claim, using subjects who are rational and unimpaired by construction. Chase is a professional taster in good health; the post-operative adapter passes every behavioural and verbal test; the inverted-spectrum victim is an ordinary person waking up. Of each, Dennett argues not merely that they might be wrong but that they have no introspective resources for being right: "he must admit that he has no introspective resources for distinguishing that possibility from alternative (I)" (PDF p. 11), and "the state of his own qualia must be as unknowable to him as the state of anyone else's qualia" (PDF p. 7).

  Two further qualifications keep this from being over-read as a refutation. First, Dennett's conclusion is *not* the one Chalmers argues is implausible. Chalmers's Joe is supposed to be massively **mistaken** — to hold false beliefs about an inner matter of fact. Dennett denies there is a matter of fact to be mistaken about, which is a different position and is not directly addressed by Chalmers's reliability premise. Second, the two authors agree, for wholly different reasons, that the classical inverted-spectrum hypothesis fails; the Dancing Qualia argument's conclusion (organizational isomorphs share experience) is one Dennett's own view gives no reason to resist. The disagreement is over the route and over the status of introspection, not over whether silicon isomorphs are zombies.

  There is a further, sharper point of contact worth flagging for the synthesis. The Dancing Qualia argument's key move, as Part B reconstructs it, is a switchable backup circuit that changes physical realisation within a single subject at a single moment, so that the opponent is forced to accept **a change in experience that the subject provably cannot notice**, and Chalmers treats that as an unacceptable cost. Dennett's pumps #6 and #8 argue that a change in experience the subject cannot notice is the *ordinary* situation — so by his lights, the cost is not a cost. A synthesis that presents Dancing Qualia as decisive should record that the premise it turns on was under direct attack in the same literature seven years earlier.

- **Cuda (1985), *Against Neural Chauvinism*** — reviewed in [Part B](computational_functionalism_lit_review_B_substrate_independence.md) §2. **Not cited anywhere in this paper.** The premise-level relation: Cuda closes the gradual-fading escape route in two steps, the second of which is that if the Freds do not notice the fading, then each must be systematically mistaken about his own experience — and "If it makes sense to think that Fred_m could be mistaken in such a way, then it makes sense to think that we could be mistaken in such a way also" (Cuda p. 117), offered as a reductio. Dennett's position accepts the conditional and embraces the consequent: we *cannot know* whether our own qualia have shifted. Again the caveat from the Chalmers case applies, and applies more sharply here, because Cuda's premise is about *error* rather than *ignorance*: Dennett's claim is that there is no determinate fact of the matter, not that we hold false beliefs about one. Cuda's argument as stated is directed at the error hypothesis; whether it also reaches the no-fact hypothesis is not something either paper addresses.

- **Searle (1980), *Minds, Brains, and Programs*** — reviewed in [Part B](computational_functionalism_lit_review_B_substrate_independence.md) §1. **Not in this paper's bibliography at all**; there is no engagement, direct or indirect. (Searle's official target is intentionality and semantics rather than qualia, so the two papers do not obviously meet.)

- **Maudlin (1989), *Computation and Consciousness*** — reviewed in [Part F](computational_functionalism_lit_review_F_olympia_and_replies.md) §1. Postdates this paper by a year. One structural contact is worth recording as *this reviewer's observation*, since neither author makes it: Maudlin's supervenience thesis is defended by an appeal to the occurrent character of experience — "A pain has its entire existence and being in the event of its being perceived, and its perception is a single, unified occurrence" (Maudlin p. 409) — which is a statement in precisely the idiom Dennett argues is unsalvageable. Neither paper engages the other and neither is evidence about the other.

**Interlocutors outside this corpus.** The paper's live opponents are Shoemaker (1969, 1975, 1981, 1982, and the 1984 postscript, quoted in endnote 4 as moving reluctantly toward Dennett's conclusion: "if I am forced to make it, I reject the standard conception of qualia"), Nagel (1974, 1986), Wittgenstein (1958) via Kripke (1982), Lewis (1983) on intrinsicality, and Sellars (1981) in endnote 11 as "a heroic (and, to me, baffling) refusal to abandon intrinsicality". None is in this corpus. A synthesis that wants the 1970s–80s qualia debate at full strength should note that the corpus holds Block's side of it and now Dennett's, but not Shoemaker's.

**Net effect on the record.** The three arguments in this corpus that turn on qualia — Block's absent qualia, Cuda's replacement series, Chalmers's fading and dancing qualia — all take for granted that "does this system have qualia?" is a determinate question, and disagree about the answer. This paper argues that the question is not determinate. It leaves every machine in those arguments standing; what it denies is that anything definite is being attributed to them or withheld from them. Its presence changes how the corpus's absent-qualia material should be read: not as a datum the field argues from, but as a framing that was contested from inside philosophy of mind, by a participant in the same conversations, over a decade in which the arguments it frames were being built.

#### 2.11 Stance on computational functionalism, stated carefully

The distinction the batch assignment flags is real and the paper sits exactly on it: **opposing the qualia-based objection to functionalism is not the same as endorsing computational sufficiency.**

**What supports a "supports" reading.**
- The declared stakes are functionalism's: qualia "have seemed to provide an insurmountable and unavoidable stumbling block to functionalism, or more broadly, to materialism, or more broadly still, to any purely 'third-person' objective viewpoint or approach to the world" (PDF p. 4). Removing the stumbling block is the paper's purpose.
- Dennett writes from inside the anti-qualia camp, faulting it only for tactics — "Theorists of the contrary persuasion have patiently and ingeniously knocked down all the arguments, and said most of the right things, but they have made a tactical error" (PDF p. 4).
- He endorses a functionalist point as established: the distinction between the properties of the thing experienced and the properties of the event that realises the experiencing "was forcefully made by the pioneer functionalists and materialists, and has never been rebutted (Farrell, 1950, Smart, 1959)" (PDF p. 21).
- His positive vocabulary is information-processing vocabulary throughout: property-detectors, "information in the formal information theory sense", Dretske's natural meaning, cognitive impenetrability, and the register bit — "the physical difference between someone's imagining a purple cow and imagining a green cow might be nothing more than the presence or absence of a particular zero or one in one of the brain's 'registers'" (PDF p. 21).

**What blocks a stronger reading.**
- The paper contains **no argument that running a program is sufficient for consciousness**, and no discussion of programs, Turing machines, implementation, multiple realizability, or substrate. Computational functionalism as such is not its subject.
- It does **not** say the wine-tasting machine is conscious or has qualia. It says the intuition that the machine lacks something special is the intuition under attack. Since the attack is on the well-formedness of the property, it withdraws the affirmative claim along with the negative one.
- The information-processing vocabulary of the constructive half is assumed rather than argued for; the paper offers pips and detectors as *better* replacements for qualia, not as an independently defended theory of what consciousness is.
- The position is officially **eliminative materialism** (endnote 2), not functionalism; and the difference between eliminative and reductive materialism is said there to be "often best seen not so much as a doctrinal issue as a tactical issue".

The fixed block below therefore records **supports — defensively only**, with the limitation spelled out. A synthesis should not cite this paper as evidence that computation suffices for consciousness; it is evidence that the best-known *objection* to that thesis was disputed at the level of its subject matter.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 1988 · philosophy-of-mind (written for a volume growing out of a consciousness-science workshop, so addressed to psychologists and neuroscientists as well as philosophers; circulating in earlier versions from 1979)
- Question it asked: Is there any well-formed property answering to the pre-theoretical notion of a quale — the ineffable, intrinsic, private, directly apprehensible way a thing seems to a subject — that could serve as the subject matter of the absent- and inverted-qualia debates?
- Position in one line: No — the everyday notion of "the way it seems to me" pulls apart under pressure into an epistemic horn on which no subject can tell whether his own qualia have changed (so they are not directly apprehensible) and a constitutive horn on which reactions partly constitute experiential quality (so they are not intrinsic), with nothing in folk psychology to settle which was meant; conscious experience is real and has properties, but none of them is special in the ways qualia were supposed to be, and the ineffability, privacy and apparent grainlessness that made them seem special are fully explained by our being idiosyncratic, informationally rich, trainable property-detectors.
- Stance on computational functionalism: supports — but defensively and indirectly only. The paper's declared purpose is to remove what "seemed to provide an insurmountable and unavoidable stumbling block to functionalism, or more broadly, to materialism, or more broadly still, to any purely 'third-person' objective viewpoint" (PDF p. 4), and it endorses a functionalist distinction as never rebutted (Farrell 1950, Smart 1959). But it contains no argument that running a program suffices for consciousness, says nothing about programs, substrate, implementation or multiple realizability, and does not claim that the wine-tasting machine has qualia — its conclusion withholds the affirmative verdict as much as the negative one. Its official label is eliminative materialism (endnote 2).
- Responds to / builds on: **Block 1978** (quoted twice by name — the Louis Armstrong deflection at p. 281 as the exhibit of the presumption attacked, and "immediate phenomenological qualities"); **Block & Fodor 1972** (quoted at p. 172 on verificationist counterarguments, as the rehabilitation of the inverted-spectrum hypothesis); Block 1980 (listed, not discussed); Shoemaker 1969/1975/1981/1982/1984 (the principal live opponent); Wittgenstein 1958 via Kripke 1982; Nagel 1974, 1986; Locke 1690 and Bennett 1965 on secondary qualities; Lewis 1983 on intrinsicality; Dretske 1981 and Pylyshyn 1980/1984 for the replacement vocabulary; Farrell 1950 and Smart 1959 as the "pioneer functionalists and materialists"; Damasio et al. 1980, Geschwind & Fusillo 1966, Meadows 1974, Von der Heydt et al. 1984, Stratton 1896 / Kohler / Gregory 1977 for the empirical cases. **Does not cite Searle 1980; predates Chalmers 1995 by seven years and Maudlin 1989 by one; does not cite Cuda 1985.**
- Key move: the verb "**to quine**"; the **fifteen intuition pumps**, of which the load-bearing pair are **alternative neurosurgery** (#6 — two surgeries, identical from the inside, only one of which inverts the qualia, so "the state of his own qualia must be as unknowable to him as the state of anyone else's") and **Chase and Sanborn** (#7 — the two coffee tasters, plus the forced choice between an epistemic and a constitutive reading of "that taste"); the reclassification of introspectors as "**autopsychologists**"; and the replacement package of **practically ineffable public properties detected by idiosyncratic property-detectors** (the **osprey cry**, the **Jello box**, the **guitar string**, and Bieri's "**pips**")
- Evidence type: thought experiment, explicitly in place of formal argument ("Rigorous arguments only work on well-defined materials … the right tools for my task are intuition pumps, not formal arguments"), within conceptual analysis; supplemented by a review of four published clinical case reports of cerebral achromatopsia, and by an avowedly informal survey of philosophers' reactions to them
- Testability: yes, substantially, and this is one of the paper's selling points for its own position. It endorses concrete tests — blind re-identification of coffees, teas and wines at short intervals, and Mill's method of differences, to separate a perceptual from a judgmental change; physiological localisation of an anomaly "early" in a sensory pathway, on the model of Von der Heydt et al.'s (1984) finding that area-18 edge detectors respond to illusory contours; comparison of **discrimination profiles** across people "over a wide variation in conditions", which it says settles the interpersonal question empirically; the documented adaptation of inverting-spectacle wearers (Stratton 1896, Kohler); training studies on palate education and musical ear training, plus the guitar-harmonic demonstration, as evidence that apparent grainlessness is plastic; and the reinterpretation of Chase's and Sanborn's claims as testable hypotheses about the constancy of their own perceptual detectors ("pips"). It also carries an explicit **un**testability claim as a central premise — that no behavioural or physiological evidence can locate the putative qualia relative to the memory-comparison stage, because every measure is a resultant of two factors — and this in-principle claim is argued by cases rather than demonstrated.

## Cross-paper notes for the synthesis

Batch G contains one paper, so there are no within-batch comparisons. What follows is what the synthesis needs from it.

1. **The corpus now has four positions on absent qualia, not three.** Block says there is *prima facie* doubt whether the functional duplicate has them; Cuda and Chalmers say the doubt is untenable and the duplicate must have them; Dennett says the question is not determinate. Any narrative that treats "does the isomorph have qualia?" as the debate's organising question should record that the well-formedness of that question was itself a live dispute in the same decade, held by a participant thanked in the acknowledgments of this very paper alongside Block.

2. **The premise under attack is about introspection, and it is the premise Chalmers's argument leans on hardest.** Chalmers's Fading Qualia argument concludes by ranking hypotheses against "the hypothesis that rational conscious beings are generally correct in their judgments about their experiences", and he specifically restricts the claim to *rational, unimpaired* systems so that blindness denial does not count against it. Dennett's cases are rational and unimpaired by construction and are argued to be incapable of knowing whether their own qualia have shifted. The order in time matters and should be stated: Dennett 1988 is not a reply to Chalmers 1995, and Chalmers's defence of the reliability premise is not a reply to Dennett; they are independent statements that meet at exactly one premise.

3. **Do not overstate the collision.** Dennett's conclusion is *no determinate fact*, not *systematic error*. Chalmers's and Cuda's arguments are built against the error hypothesis (a subject who holds false beliefs about an inner matter of fact). Whether they also reach the no-fact hypothesis is not addressed by any paper in this corpus. That is a genuine gap in the record, not a resolved question.

4. **Dennett and Chalmers agree about the classical inverted spectrum, for opposite reasons.** Chalmers argues that an isomorph must share your experience; Dennett argues there is no property to be shared or swapped. Both conclude that the Lockean thought experiment as classically debated fails. A diagram that arranges the field along a single pro/anti-functionalism axis will put these two on the same side and lose what actually divides them — the status of first-person report.

5. **What the paper does not touch.** Block's liberalism/chauvinism dilemma and his problem of the inputs and the outputs are untouched, because they do not turn on qualia; so is Searle's semantics-versus-syntax argument, which is not cited. A synthesis should not present "Quining Qualia" as a general reply to the anti-functionalist literature. Its target is one premise family, precisely aimed.

6. **The deflationary strand is represented in this corpus by one paper from 1988 and nothing since.** The contemporary AI-minds papers in [Part D](computational_functionalism_lit_review_D_theories_and_tests.md) and [Part E](computational_functionalism_lit_review_E_ai_minds_and_ethics.md) — indicator-property checklists, theory-heavy assessments, welfare arguments — all presuppose that there is a determinate fact about whether a given system is conscious, which is what this strand denies. The corpus holds no paper pressing the deflationary line against those contemporary proposals. That is a gap worth naming explicitly in the synthesis rather than leaving the 1988 paper to carry a thirty-year silence.

A label reading "denies consciousness" would misstate the paper; "denies that the felt qualities the debate argues about are a well-defined target" is accurate and is the batch's own framing.
