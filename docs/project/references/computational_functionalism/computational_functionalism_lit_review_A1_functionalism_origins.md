## What this part is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question.** What kind of thing is a mental state? In the 1960s a new answer displaced the two that had been on offer. *Behaviourism* said a mental state is a disposition to behave. *Physicalism* — more precisely, "type physicalism", the claim that each **type** of mental state is one and the same as a particular **type** of physical state — said pain just *is* a certain brain state. The new answer, **functionalism**, said a mental state is defined by the *role* it plays: what causes it, what it causes, and how it interacts with other mental states. On that view, what the state is made of does not matter. This is the idea that later made "could a computer have a mind?" a serious question rather than a joke.

**Why the shape of the answer matters for this corpus.** Computational functionalism — the thesis this corpus traces — is functionalism plus a specific claim about what the functional roles are: they are *computational* roles, so running the right computation in any material at all would suffice for having the mental states. Part A1 covers the period in which the general idea was stated, given a philosophy-of-science backing, and then attacked from inside by two of its own architects.

**The story the three papers tell.**
- **1972 — the first insider objection.** Ned Block and Jerry Fodor agree with the move away from behaviourism and physicalism, and agree that the same mental state can be realised in many different physical ways (this is *multiple realizability*). But they reject one specific, very popular version of functionalism: the claim that each mental state is identical to a state in a **Turing machine's table of instructions**. They give six arguments against it. The third one is the ancestor of nearly everything in this corpus: maybe two systems could have exactly the same functional organisation while one of them *feels* something and the other feels nothing at all.
- **1974 — the philosophy-of-science backing.** Fodor asks the more general question: must psychology reduce to physics? His answer is no, and his reason is that the things psychology talks about are realised by wildly assorted physical arrangements — the same point about multiple realizability, but now made a claim about the structure of science. His illustration is money. A monetary exchange can be strings of wampum, a dollar bill, or a signature on a cheque; there is no interesting physical description that covers all three and only those. Economics is not thereby a defective science. Fodor's phrase for the punchline is that the classical "unity of science" doctrine "is at the mercy of progress in the field of computer simulation" (p. 106) — if a machine can satisfy psychology's categories while satisfying no neurological ones, reduction is finished.
- **1978 — the full attack, from the same author.** Block now argues that *no* version of functionalism works. He builds two famous thought experiments. In the first, the population of China is wired up by radio to act out your functional organisation for an hour: it would have all your functional states, but (Block claims) there is at least serious doubt that it would feel anything. He calls this the **Absent Qualia Argument** — "qualia" being the philosophers' word for how an experience feels from the inside. In the second, a rich sheik manipulates the economy of Bolivia so that its credits and debits mirror his own mental life; Block treats "the economy of Bolivia could not have mental states" (p. 294) as a fixed point. He names the two failure modes these expose: **liberalism** (a theory that wrongly grants minds to things that have none) and **chauvinism** (a theory that wrongly denies minds to things that have them), and argues every version of functionalism falls into one or the other.

**What this part does and does not settle.** All three papers grant multiple realizability. None of them defends type physicalism. The disagreement is entirely about how *abstract* the description of a mind may be before it stops describing a mind at all. Block and Fodor's 1972 verdict is "the machine-table level is the wrong level"; Block's 1978 verdict is "there may be no right level". Neither is a defence of computational functionalism, and neither is a refutation of it that its defenders accepted — Block himself calls his 1978 case a "burden-of-proof argument" rather than a conclusive one (p. 296).

**A version caveat the synthesis must carry.** The Block 1978 PDF is not the original. It is the author-revised reprint in an anthology (chapter 22, printed pp. 268–305), and its own footer says it is reprinted "with revisions by the author" from the 1978 Minnesota Studies chapter, pp. 261–325. All page citations below are to the reprint. Details in [Reading conventions](#reading-conventions).

- **Tiers.**
  - `[tier: full]` — Phase 1 (plain-language overview), Phase 2 (argument reconstruction with numbered premises), and a section-by-section backbone appendix.
  - `[tier: short]` — read in full from the PDF, summarised as Claim · Method or type · Key result or argument · Place in the field's history · Relevance.
- **No invented formalism.** None of these three papers contains derivable mathematics. Block & Fodor 1972 and Block 1978 use schematic notation (machine-table states `S₁ … Sₙ`, inputs `I₁ … Iₘ`, outputs `O₁ … O_p`, and Ramsey-sentence quantification); Fodor 1974 uses a schematic reduction template with lettered formulae. These are reproduced as the papers state them. Where a display block appears below it is the paper's own schema, not a reviewer reconstruction, unless explicitly marked.
- **Vocabulary** (each author's own terms are kept):
  - *Type identity* vs *token identity*: a claim about kinds of state versus a claim about particular dated occurrences. Block & Fodor: "type physicalism clearly entails token physicalism, but not conversely" (p. 159 n. 2).
  - *Multiple realizability*: the same psychological state type can be realised by many different physical state types.
  - *Machine table state*: a state listed as a column in a Turing machine's (or probabilistic automaton's) table of instructions.
  - *Computational state* (Block & Fodor's contrast term): "any state of the machine which is characterizable in terms of its inputs, outputs, and/or machine table states" (p. 177) — a strictly broader class.
  - *Qualia*: the felt, experiential character of a state. *Absent qualia*: functionally identical, but nothing is felt. *Inverted qualia*: functionally identical, but what is felt differs (your red is my green).
  - *Liberalism* / *chauvinism* (Block 1978, p. 292): "theories are chauvinist insofar as they falsely **deny** that systems have mental properties and liberal insofar as they falsely **attribute** mental properties."
  - *Functionalism* (capital F) vs *Psychofunctionalism* (Block 1978, p. 272): identifying a mental state by its role in a **common-sense** psychological theory versus in a **scientific** one.
- **Project vocabulary.** These papers discuss human and animal *pain*, and Block 1978 cites clinical lobotomy reports about it. That usage is theirs. In this project's own artificial agents, the internal damage signal is **nociception**, never "pain".

## 1. Block & Fodor (1972) — What Psychological States Are Not [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Block and Fodor 1972 - What psychological states are not.pdf` — published version, *The Philosophical Review* 81(2), pp. 159–181, JSTOR scan.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** The paper asks a single question: what makes two mental states *the same kind* of state? Philosophers call this the question of **type-identity conditions**. If you and I are both in pain, what does our sameness consist in?

Three answers were on the table, and the paper takes them in turn.
1. **Behaviourism** — sameness of behaviour or behavioural disposition.
2. **Physicalism** — sameness of physical (brain) state type.
3. **The functional state identity theory**, which the authors abbreviate **FSIT** — sameness of *machine table state*, where the organism is modelled as a probabilistic automaton (a generalised Turing machine whose instructions carry probabilities).

The paper's first section dismisses (1) and (2). Its long third section attacks (3) with six arguments. Its fourth section diagnoses why all three fail in a similar way.

**Key findings (as argued).**
1. **Behaviourism fails empirically.** What a creature does at any moment "is a very complicated function of its beliefs and desires together with its current sensory inputs and memories" (p. 160), so pairing one behavioural predicate with each mental state is hopeless.
2. **Physicalism fails empirically, on three grounds** the authors take from Putnam. First, *neurological equipotentiality* — the Lashley doctrine that different brain structures can serve the same psychological function (their example: language functions relocating to the right hemisphere after early trauma). Second, *evolutionary convergence* — "we have no particular reason to suppose that the physiology of pain in man must have much in common with the physiology of pain in phylogenetically remote species" (p. 161). Third, *artifacts* — for any psychophysical correlation that holds in an organism, one could probably build a machine that matches its psychology but breaks the correlation (p. 161).
3. **The "big disjunction" rescue fails.** Someone might say: pain corresponds not to one physical type but to a long disjunction of them. The authors give three objections — the disjunction may not even be distinct across mental states; a merely accidental co-extension would be of no philosophical interest ("nothing could reasonably persuade us that we had found it", quoting Davidson, p. 163); and a single behavioural or physical state that expresses different mental states at different times makes the biconditionals formally inconsistent (pp. 163–164).
4. **The diagnosis of (1) and (2):** both set the identity conditions at a level that is "insufficiently abstract" (p. 161).
5. **But FSIT is not abstract *enough* either — and in a different way, it is too fine-grained.** This is the paper's own contribution. Six arguments are given; the headline ones are:
   - A machine table lists states, but a person's possible beliefs are **productive** — "the belief that 1 + 1 = 2", "the belief that 2 + 2 = 4", and so on without end (p. 176). A list cannot match a generated set.
   - A machine table has no notion of one state being **part of** another, yet believing *P* is somehow a constituent of believing *P & Q* (p. 178).
   - A probabilistic automaton is in exactly **one** state at a time, but what a person does now depends on what they are feeling *and* thinking now (pp. 170–171). The right model would be a set of intercommunicating automata running in parallel.
   - Machine table states are individuated by their outputs and successors, which makes the criterion absurdly **fine-grained**: if your response to a stubbed toe is "damn" and mine is "darn", our pains come out as different types (p. 174) — and the argument iterates until two automata that do not share *all* their states share *none*.
   - And the one that outlives the rest: **qualia**. "It does not, for example, seem entirely unreasonable to suggest that nothing would be a token of the type 'pain state' unless it felt like a pain" (p. 172).
6. **The positive suggestion.** If you want to model psychology with automata, mental states look like *computational* states — characterisable via inputs, outputs and machine table states — rather than machine table states themselves (p. 179).

**Initial takeaway.** This is a friendly-fire paper. Its authors accept the move to functionalism and accept multiple realizability; what they deny is that the Turing-machine table is the right currency for stating it. The distinction they draw — *machine table state* versus *computational state* — is the first careful statement that "the mind is a computer program" can be read at more than one level of description, and that picking the wrong level breaks the theory. The qualia argument they flag as merely *prima facie* becomes, six years later in Block's hands, the centre of the debate.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### The target, stated precisely

The authors define their target so that the attack is not on functionalism in general. Two components:

> "FSIT claims that for any organism that satisfies psychological predicates at all, there exists a unique best description such that each psychological state of the organism is identical with one of its machine table states relative to that description." (p. 165)

They strip away the ontology: rather than argue about identity conditions for abstract objects, they will discuss only the biconditionals such identities entail —

$$\text{O is in psychological state type } \Psi \text{ at } t \;\longleftrightarrow\; \text{O is in machine table state type } S_i \text{ at } t .$$

Two trivialisation routes are explicitly closed (pp. 165–166):
- **Simulability is not enough.** If an organism's inputs and outputs are recursively enumerable (which they trivially are, once the organism is dead), some Turing machine simulates it. That gives no *unique best description*.
- **The null machine is not enough.** Putnam's own observation, reported as made "in conversation": everything realises the one-state machine that never changes state, because whether a system realises a machine depends on what counts as a change of state in it, and one may count nothing. (This is the seed of what becomes the triviality/implementation debate in Part A2.)

So FSIT has two substantive commitments: **uniqueness of the best description**, and **one-to-one correspondence** between machine table states and psychological states. The paper attacks the second.

#### The argument reconstructed

**Part I — against the predecessors.**

- **P1.** For behaviourism to be true, mind/behaviour correlationism must be true.
- **P2.** Behaviour at *t* is a joint function of beliefs, desires, sensory input and memory — so no single behavioural predicate is coextensive with a single psychological predicate.
- **C1.** Behaviourism is false, and false *empirically*, not by conceptual analysis. The authors stress the irony: "while most of the arguments that have surrounded these doctrines have been narrowly 'conceptual,' it seems increasingly likely that the decisive arguments against them are empirical" (p. 162).
- **P3.** Equipotentiality, convergent evolution and buildable artifacts each supply cases of same psychology / different physiology.
- **C2.** Type physicalism is false.
- **P4** (blocking the disjunctive repair). A disjunctive coextension would need to be (i) distinct per mental state, (ii) lawlike, and (iii) consistent with one physical state realising different mental states at different times. None of the three is secure; (iii) is the formal one, and the paper spells it out: if `p₁ ↔ α` and `p₂ ↔ β` with the same behaviour type `Sᵢ` appearing as a disjunct in both `α` and `β`, then an organism in `Sᵢ` is in both `p₁` and `p₂`, contradicting the stipulation that `Sᵢ` expresses one and not the other at different times (pp. 163–164). The only escape is to write spatiotemporal designators into the disjuncts — which "would be totally to abandon the project" of lawlike biconditionals.

**Part III — the six arguments against FSIT.** The authors are careful, after each, to state what the argument does *not* show. That structure is reproduced here because it is what makes the paper a *restriction* on functionalism rather than a rejection of it.

| # | Argument | What it establishes | What it explicitly does **not** establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Dispositional vs. occurrent.** Every machine table state is a state the organism *can be in*, hence a possible occurrent state. But "we have no 'is believing that P' corresponding to 'believes that P'" (p. 169) — some dispositions are not possible occurrents. | No 1:1 map for those dispositions. | That mental states fail to correspond to *some abstract property of* machine tables. The authors' own toy example: a belief might correspond to "the first *n* columns in its machine table … containing only odd integers" (p. 169) — but that abandons FSIT's 1:1 claim. |
| 2 | **Simultaneous interaction.** A probabilistic automaton is in only one machine table state at a time; "what an organism does at *t* may be a function of what it is feeling at *t* and what it is thinking at *t*" (p. 170). | The right model is a *set of intercommunicating automata operating in parallel* (p. 171). | That the 1:1 claim fails outright — each psychological state might still map to a machine table state of *one of* the parallel automata. The authors flag that an algorithm exists to build a single automaton equivalent to any set of parallel ones, but "it cannot be the case … that a set of parallel automata and the equivalent single automaton both provide best descriptions" (p. 171). |
| 3 | **Inverted and absent qualia.** "nothing would be a token of the type 'pain state' unless it felt like a pain, and … this would be true even if it were connected to all the other psychological states of the organism in whatever ways pains are" (p. 172). The standard verificationist rebuttals to the inverted spectrum are judged "not persuasive". | A *prima facie* objection to any functionalist type-identity criterion. | Nothing yet — the authors give the functionalist two replies and take neither off the table (below). |
| 4 | **Individuation is too fine-grained.** Machine table states differ if their outputs or successor states differ. Transferred to psychology: "if you and I differ only in the respect that your most probable response to the pain of stubbing your toe is to say 'damn' and mine is to say 'darn,' it follows that the pain you have when you stub your toe is type-distinct from the pain I have" (p. 174). And it iterates: on the assumption that there is a computational path from every state to every other, "any two automata which have less than all their states in common will have none of their states in common" (p. 175). | The individuation criterion *for machine table states as such* cannot serve as the individuation criterion for psychological states. | That no abstract property of machine tables could serve. The authors connect this to Putnam's own worry about needing "a normal form for the psychological description of organisms" (p. 175). |
| 5 | **Productivity.** The set of machine table states is "by definition, a list"; the set of a person's possible mental states is productive, generable by substituting declarative sentences into the schema **A**: "the belief (thought, desire, hope, and so forth) that S" (p. 176). Abstracting from memory and mortality, there are infinitely many. | Psychological states cannot correspond 1:1 with *machine table* states. | That they cannot correspond to *computational* states. "a sufficient condition for the existence of the latter correspondence is that the psychological states of an organism should be countable" (p. 177). |
| 6 | **Constituent structure.** "believing that P is somehow a constituent of believing that P & Q; but the machine table state model has no conceptual resources for saying that. In particular, the notion 'is a constituent of' is not defined for machine table states" (p. 178). The sentence analogy: structural similarity among sentences and their productivity are "two aspects of the same phenomenon" — both come from being a *generated set* rather than a list. | Same conclusion as (5), reached from structure rather than cardinality. | Same caveat as (5). |

**The qualia argument, expanded.** This one gets its own treatment because the paper treats it as categorically different. Having raised it, the authors hand the functionalist two defences:

- **Defence (a), nomological.** Perhaps it is a matter of natural law that anything altering the qualitative character of a state also alters its functional character, so functionally identical states cannot be qualitatively distinct. The authors' verdict: "This sort of line may strike one as blatant apriorism, but, in the absence of any relevant empirical data, it might be well to adopt an attitude of wait and see" (pp. 172–173).
- **Defence (b), methodological.** Perhaps qualitative differences that make no functional difference are simply "irrelevant to the goals of theory construction in psychology" (p. 173). The authors make this vivid: if it turned out everyone has grossly different qualia when in the machine-table state alleged to be pain, "it might be reasonable to say that the character of an organism's qualia is irrelevant to whether it is in pain."

And then they show defence (b) has a cost:

> "For all that we now know, it may be nomologically possible for two psychological states to be functionally identical … even if only one of the states has a qualitative content. In this case, FSIT would require us to say that an organism might be in pain even though it is feeling nothing at all, and this consequence seems totally unacceptable." (p. 173)

They flag the significance explicitly, and this is the sentence that sets up the rest of the corpus:

> "these 'inverted (or absent) qualia' cases in a certain sense pose a deeper problem for FSIT than any of the other arguments we shall be discussing. Our other arguments are … compatible with functionalism in the broad sense of that doctrine which holds that the type-identity conditions for psychological states refer only to their relations to inputs, outputs, and one another. The present consideration, however, might be taken to show that psychological states cannot be functionally defined at all." (pp. 173–174)

They then **set it aside** — "We will ignore this possibility in what follows, since if psychological states are not functional states at all, the question whether they are machine table states simply does not arise." That deferral is what Block 1978 takes up.

**Part IV — the diagnosis.** The paper closes with a general principle about scientific taxonomy:

> "the basic condition upon type identity in science is that it makes possible the articulation of the domain of laws." (p. 179)

Their illustration: the type-identity conditions for elementary particles refer to charge and not to distance from the North Pole, precisely because there are fundamental laws operating on charge and none operating on polar distance. Applied to the three theories:
- Behaviourism type-identifies by reference only to the laws governing behavioural *effects*, ignoring the laws governing mental-state *interaction*.
- Physicalism assumes that neurological type-distinctness tracks psychological type-distinctness; "in all probability, distinct neurological states can be functionally identical" (p. 180). The restriction has no justification except "the reductionist biases of these positions".
- FSIT is not reductionist, and does invite type-identification by nomological connections to inputs, outputs and other states. Its failure is the *extra* constraint — the 1:1 map to machine table states — which "is in fact a substantive constraint, and one which cannot be satisfied" (p. 181).

**The closing position.** Stated in full because it is frequently misremembered as an anti-computational conclusion:

> "It may be both true and important that organisms are probabilistic automata. But even if it is true and important, the fact that organisms are probabilistic automata seems to have very little or nothing to do with the conditions on type identity of their psychological states." (p. 181)

A footnote (p. 177 n. 7) makes the same concession more concretely: it might turn out that some subset of psychological states corresponds to machine table states that determine the rest; or that each machine table state corresponds to a *conjunction* of psychological states. "the claim that for each organism there is a probabilistic automaton which is its unique best model is interesting. And this latter claim neither entails FSIT nor is it by any means obviously true."

### Appendix: Section-by-Section Backbone

The paper's four sections are unnumbered in content but numbered I–IV.

| § | Printed pp. | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | 159–164 | Sets up type identity as the target question. Reviews and rejects behaviourism (behaviour is a joint function of many states) and type physicalism (three Putnam-sourced empirical grounds: Lashleyan equipotentiality, Darwinian convergence of psychology across phylogeny, buildable artifacts). Rejects the disjunctive repair on three counts, including a formal inconsistency when one behavioural/physical type expresses different mental types at different times. Verdict: both are "insufficiently abstract". |
| **II** | 164–167 | States FSIT. Organism modelled as a probabilistic automaton with a *unique best description*; psychological states identified 1:1 with machine table states. Notes: sensory transducers replace Putnam's "oracle" input tape; FSIT is not an ontological theory (persons, material objects, souls all eligible); FSIT beats behaviourism because machine table states are individuated partly by their relations *to each other*, so states with no behavioural expression can still be distinct; FSIT is compatible with token physicalism and with causal readings of the transitions. Two trivialisations excluded (mere simulability; the null Turing machine). |
| **III** | 167–179 | The six arguments, each followed by an explicit statement of its limits. (1) dispositional vs. occurrent states; (2) simultaneous rather than merely sequential interaction — a probabilistic automaton is in one state at a time, so the right model is parallel intercommunicating automata; (3) inverted and absent qualia, with two functionalist defences and the absent-qualia cost of the second, flagged as a deeper problem that would sink functionalism generally; (4) fine-grained individuation ("damn"/"darn"), iterating to the result that automata sharing less than all states share none; (5) productivity — a machine table is a list, the belief schema generates infinitely many states — plus the machine-table/computational-state distinction; (6) constituent structure, which a list cannot represent. |
| **IV** | 179–181 | Diagnosis. Type identity in science serves "the articulation of the domain of laws". Behaviourism and physicalism each restrict attention to a subset of the relevant laws, motivated by reductionism. FSIT's failure is its extra 1:1 constraint. Closing concession: organisms may well be probabilistic automata; that fact bears little on psychological type identity. |

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 1972 · philosophy-of-mind
- Question it asked: What are the conditions under which two psychological states count as states of the same type, and can a Turing machine's table of instructions supply them?
- Position in one line: Behaviourism and type physicalism set the identity conditions at too concrete a level, but the machine-table version of functionalism fails too — on six counts — so if psychological states correspond to anything in an automaton it is to its *computational* states, not its machine-table states.
- Stance on computational functionalism: restricts — it endorses multiple realizability and functionalism "in the broad sense", but denies that the Turing-machine table is the level at which mental state types live, and flags absent/inverted qualia as a possible defeater of functionalism altogether.
- Responds to / builds on: Putnam, "Psychological Predicates" (1967), "The Mental Life of Some Machines" (1966), "Brains and Behavior" (1965), "On Properties" (1970); Davidson, "Mental Events" (1970) and "Actions, Reasons and Causes" (1963); Fodor, *Psychological Explanation* (1968); Lashley (equipotentiality, cited via the doctrine)
- Key move: the distinction between a *machine table state* and a *computational state*, plus the "damn"/"darn" fine-grainedness objection, the productivity objection, and the first published statement in this corpus of the absent-qualia worry ("an organism might be in pain even though it is feeling nothing at all", p. 173)
- Evidence type: conceptual analysis (with appeal to empirical likelihoods — the authors insist the decisive arguments against behaviourism and physicalism are empirical, not conceptual)
- Testability: indirectly. The anti-physicalism premises are empirical claims that could be checked — whether a given psychological function is served by varied neural structures (equipotentiality), whether phylogenetically remote species share pain physiology, whether a machine psychologically similar to an organism but physiologically different can be built. The anti-FSIT arguments themselves are not empirically testable; argument 2's positive proposal (parallel rather than serial architecture) is a substantive modelling claim.
- PDF version: published

## 2. Fodor (1974) — Special Sciences (Or: The Disunity of Science as a Working Hypothesis) [tier: short]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Fodor 1974 - Special sciences or the disunity of science as a working hypothesis.pdf` — published version, *Synthese* 28(2), pp. 97–115, JSTOR scan. (The manifest note "chapter" does not match the held file; see [Reading conventions](#reading-conventions).)

**Claim.** The doctrine that all sciences reduce to physics ("the unity of science") is much stronger than, and does not follow from, the doctrine that everything the sciences talk about is physical ("the generality of physics"). Psychology can be irreducible to neurology while every psychological event remains a physical event.

**Method / type.** Conceptual analysis in philosophy of science, using a formal template for reduction and two worked examples (economics; psychology). No empirical data.

**Key result or argument.** Fodor's formal set-up: let a law of special science *S* be
$$S_1x \rightarrow S_2x .$$
Reducing it to physics requires *bridge laws* `S₁x ↔ P₁x` and `S₂x ↔ P₂x`, plus a physical law `P₁x → P₂x` (pp. 98–99). Read the bridge laws as contingent event identities and you get **token physicalism**: all the events the sciences talk about are physical events. Fodor then separates three doctrines that are routinely run together (pp. 100–101): token physicalism is weaker than materialism, weaker than **type** physicalism, and weaker than reductivism. "reductivism is the conjunction of token physicalism with the assumption that there are natural kind predicates in an ideally completed physics which correspond to each natural kind predicate in any ideally completed special science."

The core move is the notion of a **natural kind** — the predicates that appear as bound variables in a science's proper laws. His example of a non-kind: "is transported to a distance of less than three miles from the Eiffel Tower" (p. 101). Then the argument by example:
- **Economics.** "Some monetary exchanges involve strings of wampum. Some involve dollar bills. And some involve signing one's name to a check" (p. 103). A physical disjunction covering all of them is not a physical natural kind; if it happened to be coextensive with "is a monetary exchange", "that would be an accident on a cosmic scale" (p. 104). And bridge laws must be *laws*, so must hold across all nomologically possible monetary systems, not just actual ones.
- **Psychology.** Same structure, two supports. The Lashley-style claim that "the nervous system of higher organisms characteristically achieves a given psychological end by a wide variety of neurological means" (p. 105); and, more sharply, automata — "there are nomologically possible systems other than organisms (namely, automata) which satisfy natural kind predicates in psychology, and which satisfy no neurological predicates at all", and, per Putnam, "equivalent automata can be made out of practically anything." Hence: "the classical formulation of the unity of science is at the mercy of progress in the field of computer simulation. … The unity of science was intended to be an empirical hypothesis, defeasible by possible scientific findings. But no one had it in mind that it should be defeated by Newell, Shaw and Simon" (p. 106).

He then blocks the epistemic route from token physicalism to reductivism: type-*heterogeneous* neural correlates would support token physicalism just as well as type-homogeneous ones, provided the heterogeneous realisers agree "in respect of those properties which determine which kind of psychological event a given event is" (p. 107). The point of reduction, he concludes, "is not primarily to find some natural kind predicate of physics co-extensive with each natural kind predicate of a reduced science. It is, rather, to explicate the physical mechanisms whereby events conform to the laws of the special sciences."

The **liberalisation**: allow bridge *statements* of the form `Sx ↔ P₁x ∨ P₂x ∨ … ∨ Pₙx` where the disjunction is not a natural kind (p. 108). An objection — if each `Pᵢ → Pᵢ*` is a law, is the disjunctive conditional a law? — is answered by denying that "it's a law that —" is a truth-functional context: "I think that it is a law that the irradiation of green plants by sunlight causes carbohydrate synthesis, and I think that it is a law that friction causes heat, but I do not think that it is a law that (either the irradiation of green plants by sunlight or friction) causes (either carbohydrate synthesis or heat)" (p. 109).

Two payoffs are claimed. First, it explains why special-science laws have **exceptions** while basic physics need not: an exception arises when `S₁` happens to be realised by a physical state not lawfully connected to any realiser of `S₂` (pp. 110–112). Second, it explains **why there are special sciences at all**: not because brains are inconveniently small, but "because of the way the world is put together: not all natural kinds … are, or correspond to, physical natural kinds" (p. 113). The closing statement: special-science taxonomies **cross-classify** physical natural kinds, and "it is not further required that the taxonomies which the special sciences employ must themselves reduce to the taxonomy of physics. It is not required, and it is probably not true" (p. 114).

**Place in the field's history.** This is the paper that converted multiple realizability from a claim about minds into a claim about the architecture of science. Two consequences matter for this corpus. (i) It supplies the *autonomy of psychology* premise that computational functionalists rely on: if psychology's kinds cross-classify physical kinds, then a psychological-level (and so potentially computational-level) description is where the generalisations live. (ii) Its automata argument is the earliest item in this corpus that explicitly makes the possibility of a *non-neural* realiser the thing that breaks reduction. Note that Fodor does **not** argue that the psychological level is computational; he argues only that it does not reduce. The step from "irreducible" to "computational" is taken by others (Part A2).

It is also the paper that later anti-multiple-realizability arguments target directly — including Bechtel & Mundale (1999), named in this batch but not held.

**Relevance.** Provides the philosophy-of-science scaffolding for substrate independence without itself asserting it. Also supplies the disjunctive-realiser vocabulary ("wildly disjunctive", "an accident on a cosmic scale") that recurs in Block 1978 §3.1, where Block turns the same weapon against functionalism's specification of inputs and outputs.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 1974 · philosophy-of-mind (philosophy of science, applied to psychology)
- Question it asked: Does the claim that every event studied by any science is a physical event entail that every science reduces to physics?
- Position in one line: No — reductivism adds to token physicalism the further assumption that each special science's natural kinds correspond to physical natural kinds, and since the physical realisers of psychological and economic kinds are wildly disjunctive, that assumption is probably false; the special sciences cross-classify physical kinds and are autonomous.
- Stance on computational functionalism: supports — indirectly. It makes non-neural realisation the standard case rather than an exotic possibility, and explicitly names automata as nomologically possible satisfiers of psychology's natural-kind predicates that satisfy no neurological predicates. It does not itself claim that the autonomous psychological level is computational.
- Responds to / builds on: Putnam (equivalent automata "can be made out of practically anything"); Davidson (paraphrased: "nothing but brute enumeration could convince us of this brute co-extensivity"); Lashley (neurological variability); Gresham's law as the worked economics case; Newell, Shaw and Simon (computer simulation); acknowledges Ned Block for comments
- Key move: the argument that the physical realisers of a special-science kind form a disjunction that is not itself a natural kind ("Some monetary exchanges involve strings of wampum … a disjunction … would be an accident on a cosmic scale"), plus the claim that special-science taxonomies **cross-classify** physical ones
- Evidence type: conceptual analysis
- Testability: partly. Whether psychological kinds are in fact realised by heterogeneous neural kinds is an empirical question Fodor treats as open but likely ("There are no firm data for any but the grossest correspondence between types of psychological states and types of neurological states", p. 105) — and he says outright that "the question whether reductivism is too strong is finally an empirical question" (p. 102). The claim about disjunctive bridge statements and exception-bearing laws is not directly testable; it is a proposal about how to read reduction.

## 3. Block (1978) — Troubles with Functionalism [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Block 1978 - Troubles with functionalism.pdf` — **author-revised anthology reprint**, chapter 22, printed pp. 268–305. Image-only scan with no text layer; read from rendered page images. See [Reading conventions](#reading-conventions) for the version caveat and its effect on page citations.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Six years after co-authoring the paper above, Block widens the attack. The target is now functionalism *as such*: the view that "each type of mental state is a state consisting of a disposition to act in certain ways *and to have certain mental states*, given certain sensory inputs and certain mental states" (p. 268).

His framing device is a pair of opposite failure modes, which he names and which the field has used ever since (p. 292):
- **Liberalism** — "theories are … liberal insofar as they falsely **attribute** mental properties."
- **Chauvinism** — "theories are chauvinist insofar as they falsely **deny** that systems have mental properties."

Functionalism's own case against physicalism, Block notes, is a chauvinism charge: "In saying mental states are brain states, for example, physicalists unfairly exclude those poor brainless creatures who nonetheless have minds" (p. 270). His thesis is that the same weapon turns: functionalism is liberal, and every repair that removes the liberalism makes it chauvinist instead.

He announces the structure on p. 270: "The first [part] argues that functionalism is guilty of liberalism, the second that one way of modifying functionalism to avoid liberalism is to tie it more closely to empirical psychology, and the third that no version of functionalism can avoid both liberalism and chauvinism."

A distinction he introduces and uses throughout (p. 272): **Functionalism** (capital F) identifies a mental state with its role in a *common-sense* psychological theory; **Psychofunctionalism** identifies it with its role in a *scientific* one. The two differ in what may be cited as inputs and outputs — Functionalists are limited to commonsense-available descriptions (objects in the vicinity, movements of body parts); Psychofunctionalists may use internal parameters such as neural signals.

**Key findings (as argued).**
1. **The homunculi-headed robot.** Imagine a body like yours, but with the sensory neurons wired to a bank of lights inside a hollow head and the motor neurons wired to buttons. A crowd of little men inside implement single squares of a machine table that describes you; a bulletin board holds the current state card (p. 276).
2. **The Chinese nation.** To make the case nomologically plausible rather than merely conceivable, Block scales it: "Suppose we convert the government of China to functionalism … We provide each of the billion people in China … with a specially designed two-way radio that connects them in the appropriate way to other persons and to the artificial body" (p. 276). Satellites over China display the state card. The system could be functionally equivalent to you for an hour.
3. **The Absent Qualia Argument** (named on p. 278). If there is *prima facie* doubt that the China-body system has qualia, then there is *prima facie* doubt that the qualitative state `Q` is identical to the machine table state `S_q`, and so doubt that functionalism is true.
4. **The doubt is not just an intuition.** Block concedes intuitions are fallible and that "No physical mechanism seems very intuitively plausible as a seat of qualia, least of all a brain. Is a hunk of quivering gray stuff more intuitively appropriate as a seat of qualia than a covey of little men?" (p. 281). His asymmetry: we *know* we are brain-headed and *know* we have qualia, so we have overwhelming reason to discount the doubt about brains, and no comparable reason to discount it about homunculi-heads. He notes this makes the argument "partly empirical" (p. 281).
5. **The list-searcher.** A separate, non-homuncular case. Consider all "speakable strings of sentences" — a very large but finite set — and the subset in which at least one party is "making sense". A machine that stores these and replies by look-up would pass the Turing Test, "But this machine clearly has no mental states at all. It is just a huge list-searcher plus a tape recorder" (pp. 281–282). This is the argument later known as Blockhead.
6. **Every proposed repair fails.** Putnam's stipulation that a pain-feeling organism has no decomposition into separately-Described parts is defeated by the elementary-particle people (below) and, in a note, by split-brain and hemispherectomy cases (p. 301). Shoemaker's "paradigmatically embodied person" repair works only by retreating from Functionalism to Psychofunctionalism (p. 285). Psychofunctionalism in turn is chauvinist about Martians (p. 292); its escape route, "universal psychology", has an unsolved domain problem and may be impossible (pp. 292–293).
7. **The problem of the inputs and the outputs** (§3.1) is Block's most general argument, and it is a reflection of functionalism's own anti-physicalist argument onto its own periphery. If inputs and outputs are described in *physical* terms, the description is species-specific and so chauvinist. If they are described *only as* inputs and outputs, the theory is wildly liberal — enter the Bolivian economy, manipulated by a sheik to be functionally equivalent to himself (p. 294). Mental or action vocabulary is barred because it abandons the project of characterising mentality in nonmental terms.

**Initial takeaway.** Block does not claim to have refuted functionalism. His own summary: "I do not claim that this is a conclusive argument against functionalism. Rather, like the functionalist argument against physicalism, it is best construed as a burden-of-proof argument" (p. 296). What the paper did to the field was supply (i) a vocabulary (liberalism/chauvinism, absent qualia), (ii) three thought experiments that every subsequent participant has to answer (China nation, elementary-particle people, list-searcher), and (iii) a diagnosis — that the trouble is located at the *interface*, in how a functional theory says what counts as an input and an output — which the implementation literature in Part A2 takes up.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### The formal apparatus Block sets up before attacking

**Machine functionalism.** A Turing-machine table lists states `S₁ … Sₙ`, inputs `I₁ … Iₘ`, outputs `O₁ … O_p`, with conditionals of the form: if in state `Sᵢ` and receiving input `I_j`, emit `O_k` and go to `S_l` (p. 270). Block uses a Coke machine as the toy case: `S₁` = dime-desire, `S₂` = nickel-desire, with a four-cell table over nickel and dime inputs (p. 271). He quotes Putnam to fix the key property: "The `Sᵢ`, to repeat, are specified only *implicitly* by the description, i.e., specified *only* by the set of transition probabilities given in the machine table" (Putnam 1967, p. 434, quoted p. 271). The consequence Block draws: "machine functionalism can be said to reduce mentality to input-output structures."

**Ramsey functional correlates.** For nonmachine versions, Block uses the standard Ramsey-sentence construction (pp. 272–273). Take a psychological theory `T`, written as one conjunctive sentence with all mental-state terms as singular terms:
$$T(s_1 \ldots s_n,\; i_1 \ldots i_k,\; o_1 \ldots o_m)$$
Replace the state terms `s₁ … sₙ` — but **not** the input and output terms — with variables and existentially quantify. The *Ramsey functional correlate* of pain is then the property of being an `x` such that some assignment of states satisfies `T` and `x` is in the variable that replaced 'pain'. Block's observation: this expression "contains input and output terms … but no mental terms", so functionalism "tacks down" mental states only at the periphery (p. 269). He also notes that in general a state is *not* identical to its Ramsey functional correlate, since no theory tells us everything about the states it mentions; "The bold hypothesis of functionalism is that for at least some psychological theory, this natural supposition is false" (p. 273).

**Functional equivalence is doubly relative** — to a specification of inputs and outputs, and to a theory or machine table (p. 273) — and relative to a *level of abstractness*: "Two creatures may be functionally equivalent relative to one level of abstractness of psychological theory, but not with respect to another" (p. 274). Block sets abstractness aside until §3.1, which is where it returns as the fatal problem.

#### The Absent Qualia Argument reconstructed

- **P1.** Machine functionalism says each mental state is identical to a machine table state; in particular a qualitative state `Q` is identical to some `S_q`.
- **P2.** The homunculi-headed system (little men, or the billion Chinese with radios) realises the machine table that describes you, and so is in `S_q` whenever you are. Block defends this against three objections:
  - *"A bomb or a Yangtze flood could disrupt it, and unlike a bomb in a computer the disruption enters through what the table counts as inputs."* Reply: "The person who says what system he or she is talking about gets to say what counts as inputs and outputs" (p. 277). Neural activity in the people of Chungking counts no more as input to *this* system than tape jammed by a saboteur counts as input to a computer. Describing an object as a Turing machine means drawing a line between inside and outside, and functionalism requires only that a mental system have *some* specification under which its table justifies attributions of mentality, not every specification (p. 277).
  - *"Functional equivalence for an hour is not functional equivalence."* Reply: machine-table conditionals are "understood subjunctively" — what gives a system a functional organisation at *t* is the counterfactuals true of it at *t*, so a system realises the table "even if it exists for only an instant" (p. 277).
  - *"It would run far too slowly to converse with."* Reply: the time-lapse aliens — creatures we could only communicate with through time-lapse photography, who find us intelligible only in slowed-down movies. "To take time scale as all important seems crudely behavioristic." And in any case metaphysical possibility suffices for the argument (p. 278).
- **P3.** There is *prima facie* doubt whether the homunculi-headed system has qualia — whether, in Nagel's phrase, "there is anything which it is like to be" it (p. 278).
- **C1 (the argument named).** "if there is prima facie doubt about the homunculi-headed system's mentality, there is prima facie doubt that `Q = S_q`, i.e., doubt that the kind of functionalism under consideration is true" (p. 278).
- **P4 (upgrading the doubt from an intuition).** Three supports.
  - *The flea-head reply fails* (§1.3). "What if *you* turned out to be one?" Block shows both answers are compatible with the argument. If privileged access does not guarantee I have qualia, then my own qualia would be in doubt if I turned out to be a flea-head — fine. If privileged access *does* guarantee it, then supposing I have fleas inside is supposing a homunculi-head has qualia, "just the sort of supposition my position doubts", and to argue from it begs the question "like twitting a man who doubts there is a God by asking what he would say if he turned out to *be* God" (p. 279).
  - *The brain/homunculi asymmetry* (§1.5). We know we are brain-headed and have qualia, so we have overwhelming reason to disregard doubt about brains — a reason we lack for homunculi-heads. Block concedes this makes the argument partly empirical and says dependence on such knowledge "should not be regarded as a defect" (p. 281).
  - *The design-to-mimic point.* "The best explanation of the homunculi-heads' screams and winces is not their pains, but that they were designed to mimic our screams and winces" (p. 281). This blocks any inference-to-the-best-explanation defence of their mentality.
- **P5 (the behaviour-is-enough reply is crude behaviourism).** Demonstrated by the list-searcher: a finite store of "smart speakable strings" plus a random-selection rule passes the Turing Test yet "clearly has no mental states at all. It is just a huge list-searcher plus a tape recorder" (pp. 281–282).
- **C2.** "If there is even one possible Functional simulation of you that has no qualia, Functionalism is false" (p. 282). Block is careful about scope: "My claim is not that every sort of Functional simulation of you must lack qualia. Different causes can have similar effects, so why shouldn't it be possible that mentality can be produced by wildly different sorts of information processing?" (p. 282).

**Why the repairs fail.**

#### Qualia and the domain of psychology (§2.1)

This section is where Block separates qualitative from nonqualitative states and argues Psychofunctionalism inherits the problem.

- **Inverted qualia.** Inverting lenses; a pair of identical twins, one lensed at birth, functionally equivalent at 21. "This situation offers at least some evidence that each's spectrum is inverted relative to the other's" (p. 288).
- **The asymmetry with belief.** The analogue for propositional attitudes is hard to construct: two people differing only in believing *p* rather than *q* should differ behaviourally in some circumstance. "Qualia seem to be supervenient on functional organization in a way that beliefs are not" (p. 288). A note qualifies this: Bromberger's Word Switching + Stimulus Switching double victim is functionally normal yet has systematically wrong beliefs, so "the inverted qualia problem infects belief as well as qualia (though presumably only qualitative belief)" (p. 302).
- **The main claim.** Qualia may not be *in the domain of psychology*. Psychology decomposes information flow into primitive mechanisms — "black boxes" whose internal structure belongs to physiology (p. 288). Of what passes for the psychology of sensation, only psychophysics is about qualia at all, and "it is obvious that psychophysics touches only the *functional* aspect of sensation, not its qualitative character. Psychophysical experiments done on you would have the same results if done on any system Psychofunctionally equivalent to you, even if it had inverted or absent qualia" (p. 289). Hence: "any doubt that it has qualia is a doubt that qualia are in the domain of psychology" (p. 289). Block is explicit that this is an argument from what we now know, not an impossibility claim: "That something is currently inconceivable is not a good reason to think it is impossible."
- **Two objections answered.** The Incorrigibility objection (if the simulation believes it has a headache, how can that belief be mistaken?) — Block rejects the Incorrigibility Thesis, and adds that it cuts both ways: if the simulation is Psychofunctionally equivalent to believing it is in pain yet has no pain, then either its belief is false or "belief is not a Psychofunctional state" (p. 290). The temperature objection (temperature once seemed not to be in the domain of physics) — Block answers that it probably never was inconceivable, and that saying something is not in the domain of *psychology* is quite different from saying it is not explainable at all (p. 290).
- **Lobotomy as supporting evidence.** "After frontal lobotomies, patients typically report that they still have pains, though the pains no longer bother them (Melzack, 1973, p. 95). These patients show all the 'sensory' signs of pain (e.g., recognizing pin pricks as sharp), but they often have little or no desire to avoid 'painful' stimuli" (p. 291).
- **The composite proposal.** "each pain is actually a composite state whose components are a quale and a Functional or Psychofunctional state … each pain is a quale playing a certain Functional or Psychofunctional role." Block suggests this explains the long-running disagreement: "Proponents of behaviorism and functionalism have had one component in mind; proponents of private ostensive definition have had the other" (p. 291).

#### The chauvinism side (§3.0) and the interface problem (§3.1)

**Martians.** Psychofunctionalism, read as adverting to *human* psychology, makes it "logically impossible for a system to have beliefs, desires, etc., except insofar as psychological theories true of us are true of it" (p. 291). Block's Martians are roughly Functionally but not Psychofunctionally equivalent to us — we trade with them, read their novels, publish in each other's journals — and then the psychologists compare notes and find maximally different design choices (innate vs. learned capacities; memory-heavy vs. computation-heavy; few axioms and many rules vs. the reverse). Denying them mentality "would be crude human chauvinism" (p. 292). He is careful to state the principle he actually used against the homunculi-heads: "if the sole reason to think system *x* has mentality is that *x* was built to be Functionally equivalent to us, then differences between *x* and us in underlying information processing and/or neurophysiology are reasons to doubt whether *x* has mental states." That principle makes Psychofunctional equivalence *sufficient* for the psychology-domain aspects of mentality without making it *necessary* (p. 292).

**Universal psychology.** The obvious escape is to define Psychofunctionalism over a cross-system psychology. Block's objection is a domain-circularity: "How are we to decide what systems should be included in the *domain* of universal psychology?" Using some other theory of mentality to fix the domain makes the procedure "at least as ill-justified as the other theory used" (p. 292). He grants that other sciences bootstrap their domains from prescientific intuitions and that universal psychology might do the same — but also that it "may not be possible", and "the question of whether universal psychology is possible is surely one which we have no way of answering now" (p. 293).

**§3.1, the problem of the inputs and the outputs.** The most general argument in the paper, and a mirror of functionalism's own argument against physicalism.

- **Horn 1 — describe I/O only *as* inputs and outputs** (number the outputs and require only that the causal structure match). Wildly liberal. Economic systems have inputs and outputs (influx and outflux of credits and debits) and a rich variety of internal states (a rate of increase of GNP equal to double the Prime Rate). "It does not seem impossible that a wealthy sheik could gain control of the economy of a small country, e.g., Bolivia, and manipulate its financial system to make it functionally equivalent to a person, e.g., himself" — and the magnitudes he picks "need not be 'natural' economic magnitudes … e.g., the fifth time derivative of the balance of payments" (p. 294). Block's fixed point: "the economy of Bolivia could not have mental states, no matter how it is distorted by powerful hobbyists."
- **Horn 2 — describe I/O physically.** Chauvinist, and not only in the obvious way (neural-impulse specifications exclude machines; arm-and-leg specifications exclude brains in vats, paralytics and snakes). Block's deeper move is to apply functionalism's own anti-physicalist argument to the periphery. Consider a burn victim whose best channel to the world is modulating an EEG pattern in Morse code, or the reverse — others inducing electrical activity in your brain to communicate, or a cerebroscope reading your thoughts directly (he cites a *Boston Globe* report of 21 March 1976 on EEG-controlled machines as evidence this is not fantasy). In such cases "the brain itself becomes an essential part of one's input and output devices" (p. 295). Therefore: "on any sense of 'physical' in which the functionalist criticism of physicalism is correct, **there will be no physical characterization that applies to all and only mental systems' inputs and outputs**."
- **Blocking the disjunctive escape.** One could write a species-by-species disjunction, "and somewhere in disjunctive heaven there is a disjunctive description which will handle all species that ever actually exist in the universe (the description may be infinitely long)". But it "will not tell us what there is in common to pain-feeling organisms in virtue of which they all have pain", will not extend to merely possible pain-feelers, and is exactly what functionalists "acerbically reject" when physicalists try it. "If functionalists suddenly smile on wildly disjunctive states to save themselves from chauvinism, they will have no way of defending themselves from physicalism" (p. 295).
- **Blocking the mentalistic escape.** Using mental or action vocabulary for outputs ("punching the offending person") "would be to give up the functionalist program of characterizing mentality in nonmental terms" (p. 296).
- **C.** "the kind of functionalism held by virtually all functionalists cannot avoid both chauvinism and liberalism" (p. 295). And the closing qualification: "I do not claim that this is a conclusive argument against functionalism. Rather, like the functionalist argument against physicalism, it is best construed as a burden-of-proof argument. The functionalist says to the physicalist: 'It is very hard to see how there could be a single physical characterization of the internal states of all and only creatures with mentality.' I say to the functionalist: 'It is very hard to see how there could be a single physical characterization of the inputs and outputs of all and only creatures with mentality.' In both cases, enough has been said to make it the responsibility of those who think there could be such characterizations to sketch how they could be possible" (p. 296).

#### What Block does *not* conclude

Four limits, stated in the paper, that matter for a neutral history:
1. **Type physicalism is not rehabilitated.** Block keeps the functionalist argument against it, on a specific reading of 'physicalism' as *first-order* physicalism in the Russell–Whitehead sense (note 4, p. 296). He notes that the Putnam–Lewis–Field doctrine that pain is a *second-order* physical property "is actually a (physicalist) form of functionalism" and is incompatible with first-order type physicalism (pp. 296–297). So the space is not two-sided.
2. **Functionalism shows type physicalism false but not token physicalism false** (note 4, p. 296).
3. **The conclusion is disjunctive, not flat.** The five-point summary (p. 293): Functionalism should be tentatively rejected; Psychofunctionalist accounts of qualia are doubtful; Psychofunctionalist accounts of both qualitative and nonqualitative states are "at best unjustified". His own line: "we are not justified in holding either of the forms of functionalism."
4. **The Block & Fodor 1972 repair does not help here.** Note 14 (p. 300): moving from machine-table-state functionalism to *computational*-state functionalism "is of no use in avoiding the Absent Qualia Argument. Whatever Turing machine it is whose computational states are supposed to be identical to your mental states will have a homunculi-headed realization." Block also there extends the argument to nonmachine functionalism, with the little men carrying books, writing 'pain' on a blackboard, and grading intensity by continuously variable chalk colour — and notes that "The more intelligence exercised by the little men in simulating you, the less inclined we are to ascribe to the simulation the mental properties they are simulating" (p. 301).

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 1978 · philosophy-of-mind
- Question it asked: Can any version of functionalism specify mental states abstractly enough to avoid wrongly denying minds to unlike-us systems, without becoming so abstract that it wrongly grants minds to systems that obviously lack them?
- Position in one line: No — Functionalism is liberal (the homunculi-headed China-body system and the Bolivian economy would count as minded), Psychofunctionalism is chauvinist (it excludes Martians) unless grounded in a universal psychology we have no reason to think possible, and the problem is most general at the interface, where no specification of inputs and outputs avoids both errors.
- Stance on computational functionalism: rejects — it argues that no version of functionalism, machine or nonmachine, common-sense or scientific, is justified; but Block frames the case as a burden-of-proof argument rather than a conclusive refutation, and explicitly allows that "mentality can be produced by wildly different sorts of information processing" (p. 282), so the rejection is of functionalism as a *theory of what mental states are*, not of the possibility of non-biological minds.
- Responds to / builds on: Putnam 1967 (the machine-table version, the decomposition stipulation, and the "specified only implicitly" characterisation) and Putnam 1975; Block & Fodor 1972 (his own earlier paper, cited and partly superseded); Lewis 1966, 1972 (platitudes, functional specification); Armstrong 1968; Shoemaker 1975, 1976 (absent qualia impossible; paradigmatic embodiment); Nagel 1974 ("what it is like"); Dennett 1978 (consciousness as judgments); Kim 1972 (the temperature analogy); Lycan 1974; Fodor 1975; Melzack 1973 (lobotomy); Chisholm 1957 and Geach 1957 (the anti-behaviourist point); Bromberger and Field (personal, in notes); Kripke 1972 (metaphysical possibility)
- Key move: the **Absent Qualia Argument** built on the homunculi-headed robot and the Chinese-nation system; the **liberalism/chauvinism** pair of failure modes; the **elementary-particle people**; the Turing-Test-passing **list-searcher** (later "Blockhead"); the **Bolivian economy**; and the **problem of the inputs and the outputs**, which turns functionalism's own anti-physicalist argument against its periphery
- Evidence type: thought experiment (several), embedded in conceptual analysis; with one appeal to clinical observation (lobotomy reports via Melzack 1973) and one to reported neuroscience (split-brain and hemispherectomy cases, in a note)
- Testability: mostly no — the central arguments turn on metaphysical possibility, and Block says explicitly that metaphysical possibility is all his argument requires (p. 278). Two threads are empirically anchored: he concedes the brain/homunculi asymmetry makes his argument "partly empirical — it depends on knowledge of what makes us tick" (p. 281); and the claim that qualia are not in the domain of psychology is offered as a claim about current science that future concepts could overturn ("That something is currently inconceivable is not a good reason to think it is impossible", p. 289). He also treats the question of whether qualia correlate with cognitive processes as empirically open, while denying that such a correlation would establish identity (p. 290).

## Cross-paper notes for the synthesis

These are observations about how the three papers relate. They are not a thematic regrouping, which belongs to `literature-curator`.

1. **All three grant multiple realizability; none defends type physicalism.** The three grounds are stable across the batch: neural variability within a species (Lashley), variability across species, and buildable non-neural realisers. Fodor 1974 adds the philosophy-of-science generalisation. This means the corpus's central question is not "is multiple realizability true?" but "how abstract may a description be before it stops describing a mind?" Bechtel & Mundale (1999), named in this batch but not held, is the paper that reopens the first question.

2. **A single author changes position, and says so.** Block co-writes the 1972 paper, which concludes that mental states are better matched to *computational* states than to machine-table states. In 1978 he closes that escape himself, in note 14 (p. 300): "the move from machine-table state functionalism to computational-state functionalism is of no use in avoiding the Absent Qualia Argument. Whatever Turing machine it is whose computational states are supposed to be identical to your mental states will have a homunculi-headed realization." The synthesis should present 1972 and 1978 as one developing position, not two independent data points.

3. **The absent-qualia thread is traceable to a single deferred paragraph.** Block & Fodor 1972 raise absent and inverted qualia as argument (3) of six, note that they "pose a deeper problem … than any of the other arguments", and then explicitly set them aside (pp. 173–174). Block 1978 is that deferral cashed out. A historian can date the field's pivot from *individuation* to *consciousness* to those two paragraphs.

4. **The same weapon is fired in three directions.** The "no interesting physical property is common to all and only the realisers" argument appears as: functionalism's argument against type physicalism (Block 1978 §1.0); Fodor's argument against reductivism, with monetary exchanges standing in for mental states (1974 §II); and Block's argument against functionalism's own input/output specifications (1978 §3.1). Block makes the reflexivity explicit — he says his argument is "like the functionalist argument against physicalism … best construed as a burden-of-proof argument" (p. 296). Fodor's "wildly disjunctive" (p. 103) and Block's "wildly disjunctive states" (p. 295) are the same objection pointed at opposite targets.

5. **Two very different objections are both called "liberalism".** Block's 1978 liberalism charge covers two cases that later literature separates: the **China nation**, which has the right *causal organisation* but the wrong parts, and the **list-searcher**, which has the right *behaviour* with no internal organisation at all. The second is behaviourism's problem, not functionalism's; Block presents it as evidence against a *reply* to his argument ("this is just crude behaviorism", p. 281) rather than as an argument against functionalism itself. Any synthesis that lists "Blockhead" as an argument against computational functionalism should carry this qualification.

6. **Where each paper locates the fatal step.**
   - Block & Fodor 1972: at the choice of *grain* — machine-table individuation is simultaneously too coarse (one state at a time; a finite list) and too fine ("damn"/"darn").
   - Fodor 1974: nowhere in psychology; the fatal step is in *reductivism's* demand that bridge statements be laws.
   - Block 1978: at the *interface* — how a functional theory says what counts as an input and an output. This is the claim that connects Part A1 to the implementation debate in Part A2, where the question becomes what it takes for a physical system to *implement* a given computation at all.

7. **Vocabulary the corpus inherits from this batch.** *Liberalism* and *chauvinism* (Block 1978, p. 292); *absent qualia* and *inverted qualia* as named arguments (Block 1978, p. 278; anticipated Block & Fodor 1972, p. 173); *Functionalism vs. Psychofunctionalism* (Block 1978, p. 272); *machine table state vs. computational state* (Block & Fodor 1972, p. 177); *natural kind predicate* and *cross-classification* (Fodor 1974, pp. 101–102, 114); *token vs. type physicalism* (Fodor 1974, p. 100; Block & Fodor 1972, p. 159 n. 2).

8. **A citation caution.** Block 1978 cites Putnam 1967 at pp. 434–435 for the decomposition stipulation and at p. 434 for "specified only implicitly". Block & Fodor 1972 cite the same paper at p. 43 for the "normal form" remark. These are different reprints of Putnam's "Psychological Predicates" with different paginations. Since Putnam 1967 is not held (see below), this review reports only what the citing papers say about it, and the synthesis should not treat either pagination as canonical.
