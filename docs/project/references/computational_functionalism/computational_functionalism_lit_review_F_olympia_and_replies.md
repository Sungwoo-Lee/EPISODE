# Computational Functionalism — Part F: Maudlin's Olympia Argument and the Field's Replies

**Topic folder:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/`
**Part of:** a multi-file, neutral review of how the idea of *computational functionalism* developed — roughly, the thesis that mental states are constituted by the computations a system performs, so that running the right computation in any substrate would suffice for having a mind.
**This part:** 2 papers, reviewed one at a time in the order listed in the batch assignment, 2026-09-21. This batch was added late, after the other batches flagged the Olympia argument as a gap in the corpus.
**Reviewer:** literature-reviewer
**Stance:** this file describes positions; it does not adjudicate between them.

## What this part is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question.** Whether something counts as *running a program* depends partly on what it would have done in situations that never came up. A machine that merely replays a recording of what a calculator did last Tuesday is not calculating, because feed it a different number and it carries on replaying regardless. So computation is, in the jargon used by both papers here, a **counterfactually loaded** property: two objects doing exactly the same thing right now can differ in whether — and in what — they are computing.

Conscious experience looks like the opposite kind of property. What you are feeling over the next ten seconds seems to depend on what your brain is *actually doing* over those ten seconds, not on what some silent, idle part of it would have done had things gone otherwise. Numb a nerve that was not going to fire anyway and nothing about your experience changes.

Put those two innocuous-sounding observations together and there is a problem for anyone who says consciousness *is* computation. That is the argument this part covers.

**Why it matters for the history of computational functionalism.** In 1989 Tim Maudlin built an elaborate machine — he calls her **Olympia**, after the clockwork automaton in E. T. A. Hoffmann's *The Sandman* — designed so that every piece of machinery responsible for her counterfactual behaviour sits completely motionless while she runs. Not switched off elsewhere; *present, connected, and physically still*, gear teeth hanging in the air without touching. His conclusion is that three things a computational theory of consciousness needs cannot all be true at once, so no such theory is possible. The argument is unusual in the corpus because it is aimed at exactly the defence that the corpus's pro-computationalist papers rely on. David Chalmers's 1996 paper — the one that saves computationalism from the charge that a rock implements every automaton, by insisting that real implementation requires counterfactual-supporting connections — names Maudlin in its footnote 3 as raising a challenge that "requires an in-depth treatment in its own right", and then does not give it one. No other paper in this corpus takes it up. That is the gap this batch fills.

**The second paper** is Colin Klein's retrospective chapter, written for a handbook of the computational mind. Klein reconstructs Maudlin's argument in three lines, says plainly that he does not think it succeeds, and then spends most of the chapter on what he thinks the argument *does* prove: that computationalism cannot be stated as a claim about which mathematical function a system computes, and must instead be stated about the **architecture** a system has and the **processes** actually running in it. On that reading Olympia never was a Turing machine — she belongs to a different architecture that merely imitates one. The awkward consequence Klein accepts is that a detailed computer simulation of your brain would, by the same reasoning, not be conscious either — a conclusion he notes is "suspiciously close to what Searle (1980) says", reached by a completely different route.

**The two papers, in one line each.**
1. **Maudlin (1989)** builds Olympia to show that a machine's computational identity can turn entirely on machinery that does nothing during the run, and argues that since consciousness cannot turn on inert machinery, running a program cannot be sufficient for consciousness — while explicitly conceding that a silicon brain or a brain built out of water pipes might well be conscious, and that computational structure might still be *necessary*.
2. **Klein (2016 draft)** grants premises 1–3 of the argument their force, denies the conclusion, and relocates the dispute: Olympia fails to be a Turing machine not because of her counterfactuals but because her parts are not arranged as a Turing machine's parts are — which turns computationalism into a claim about mechanisms, and makes "which architectures can be conscious" an open empirical question.

**Version caveats.** The Maudlin PDF is a scan of the published article from JSTOR (*The Journal of Philosophy* 86(8), August 1989, pp. 407–432) with a usable text layer; the scan garbles Greek letters, and this review says so explicitly and states the reading it adopts. The Klein PDF states no journal, no volume and no publication year: its own header says it is for the *Routledge Handbook of the Computational Mind*, edited by Mark Sprevak and Matteo Colombo, and marks itself "Draft 1D, compiled October 8, 2016". It is therefore treated as an author web version of a draft chapter and dated 2016 from the PDF's own compile date, not from a publication record.

## 1. Maudlin (1989) — Computation and Consciousness [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Maudlin 1989 - Computation and consciousness.pdf` — a JSTOR scan of the published article, *The Journal of Philosophy* 86(8) (August 1989), pp. 407–432, with a JSTOR cover page (accessed 10/05/2012) as PDF page 1. Author affiliation printed at the end: Rutgers University. Four line-drawing figures (figures 1–4) are images; their captions are legible and their content is described in the text. **PDF version: published.**

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Maudlin opens on the broadest possible question: granted that brains support minds, *in virtue of which properties* do they do so? "Which properties of our gray matter are essential, and which accidental, for our mentality? What level of description or abstraction distills the essence of mental life?" (p. 407). One answer is being widely championed, he says: the **computational** level. Abstract away the biochemistry and the neurons, keep the information-processing structure, and you have kept everything that matters.

The computational programme has mostly been applied to intentionality, language and representation. Maudlin's move is to force it to face consciousness — by which he means "subjective phenomenal states or modes of awareness": tickles, pains, visual experiences, and also the particular feel of pondering that ice is made of water (p. 408). And because, as he notes citing Nagel, it is consciousness that makes the mind–body problem intractable, the question "is a computational theory of consciousness possible?" is close to the question "is a computational theory of mind possible?"

**The three claims that cannot all hold.** The paper's structure is a demonstration that three theses form, in Maudlin's word, an **inconsistent triad** (p. 413).

- The **supervenience thesis** (p. 409): "two physical systems engaged in precisely the same physical activity through a time will support the same modes of consciousness (if any) through that time." Its practical corollary, which does all the work later: introducing into the vicinity of a system "an entirely inert object that has absolutely no causal or physical interaction with the system" cannot create, destroy or alter a phenomenal state.
- The **sufficiency condition** (p. 413): there is some program π, some tape state τ and some sequence of machine states such that *anything* that runs π on τ through that sequence will, while it does so, support the phenomenal state φ.
- The **necessity condition** (p. 413): a necessary condition for a physical system to support φ is that it be describable as a Turing machine performing some *nontrivial* computation. Without this, a system with no interesting computational structure could be conscious, and computation would not be essential to consciousness after all.

**Key findings and the main apparatus.** Maudlin's guiding question is deliberately odd: "What is the laziest machine that can be conscious?" (p. 415). Running a program requires, explicitly, that the machine pass through the right sequence of states and that the tape be manipulated correctly — and, implicitly, that the machine support the whole tree of counterfactuals in its machine table, which "ramify geometrically" (p. 415). The plausible thought is that supporting all those counterfactuals must require a great deal of physical activity. Olympia is the demonstration that it does not.

The construction proceeds in three moves. (1) Start with **Klara**, an ordinary machine correctly programmed with π — clockwork, perhaps as large as the solar system, storing bits as full or empty **water troughs**. (2) Rearrange the tape so the troughs the computation will actually visit lie in a straight line, left to right, using two permitted manipulations: reordering troughs while giving Klara a map, and *bi-locating* an address by joining two troughs with a pipe so the same bit is readable at two places. Now the whole run reduces to a left-to-right sweep. (3) Replace Klara, for the actual run, with **Olympia**: a bare **armature** that travels left to right, opening a drain here and running a hose there, in the fixed sequence the correct run requires. The armature alone, Maudlin insists, computes nothing — "its operations are entirely oblivious to the state of the tape" (p. 419), and at best computes a constant function, which by the necessity condition cannot support consciousness.

Then the key step. At each trough hangs a **float**, latched in the position matching the water level that the correct run would produce there, and unlatched only as the armature passes. On the intended run τ, every float is already at the right level, so no float moves. Behind them sit **N + 2 copies of Klara**, each frozen by a wooden **block** in one of the successive machine states and positioned at the corresponding trough, each block tied by a slack **chain** to one float. Give Olympia any tape other than τ and, at exactly the step where the armature would go wrong, a float moves, a chain pulls, a block drops out, a copy of Klara wakes up in the right state at the right address and takes over — while a return chain shuts the armature down. Olympia therefore supports every counterfactual in the machine table and, by the computationalist's own criteria, is running π. But on the intended run, "an absolute stillness prevails; gears hang motionless in the air, not touching" (p. 423).

**Initial takeaway.** The bind (Maudlin's section IV) is then immediate. By the sufficiency condition Olympia is conscious. By the supervenience thesis her consciousness must supervene on the only physical activity there is — the armature and the floats. By the necessity condition the armature alone cannot support consciousness, because it computes nothing. Contradiction.

Two smaller variants make the point harder to dodge without invoking the vast machinery at all. The **argument by addition** (p. 425): slip a thin sheet of metal between the frozen gear teeth of each Klara — touching nothing, exerting no force — so that were a block ever removed the gears would jam. The counterfactuals fail, so Olympia is no longer conscious; but "how could the rest of the system know that the blocks are even there?" The **argument by subtraction** (p. 425): let the chains slowly rust until they would snap rather than pull. Nothing observable marks the moment they cross that threshold, yet the computationalist must say the toothache stops on the very next run.

What Maudlin explicitly is *not* arguing is as important as what he is. He rejects the "ploy of funny instantiation" outright — the appeal to intuitions that beer cans and water pipes just cannot be conscious — saying he "cannot think of one reason to accord those intuitions any weight" (p. 414). He rejects Searle's Chinese Room inference, siding with Searle's own commentators that a second, disjoint mentality could perfectly well arise from the card-shuffling (pp. 414–415). He grants that a silicon brain and a hydraulic brain may be conscious (p. 429). And he grants that computational structure may still be a *necessary* condition for consciousness (p. 431). The target is narrow and the concessions are large: what dies is the claim that **running a particular program is sufficient** for mentality.

### Phase 2: Argument reconstruction and technical detail

#### 2.1 The three theses, in the paper's own words

**Supervenience thesis** (p. 409). Stated in two parts. The general claim: "two physical systems engaged in precisely the same physical activity through a time will support the same modes of consciousness (if any) through that time." The application that the argument uses: if a system's activity supports a mode of consciousness and we "introduce into the vicinity of the system an entirely inert object that has absolutely no causal or physical interaction with the system, then the same activity will still support the same mode of consciousness"; and if the activity supports none, the inert object will not bring one about.

Maudlin's defence of it is an argument from the *occurrent* character of experience (p. 409): "A pain has its entire existence and being in the event of its being perceived, and its perception is a single, unified occurrence… Since the phenomenal state is entirely realized at the time of the experience, only the activity of the system at that time should be relevant to its production." He also notes it is a substantive, non-analytic thesis with direct empirical support — stimulating a brain region produces a sensation *at that time*; electroencephalography correlates concurrent activity with concurrent mental state.

**Sufficiency condition** (pp. 412–413), quoted with the OCR's Greek restored:

> "There must be some program π and some state of the machine tape τ and some sequence of machine states `S_[0]`, `S_[1]`, … `S_[N]` such that any machine programmed with π, operating on τ, and performing the computation `S_[0]`, `S_[1]`, … `S_[N]` (in accordance with π) will, during the time that the computation is taking place, support the mode of consciousness φ."

**Necessity condition** (p. 413):

> "A necessary condition for a physical system to support φ is that it be describable as a Turing machine performing some nontrivial computation."

Maudlin's notational convention (p. 413): a bracketed subscript denotes the step of *this particular* computation, so `S_[0]` is the state at step 0 and `T_[0]` the tape address visited at step 0; unbracketed subscripts number states and addresses generally. Hence `T_[5] = T_[18] = T_2` if the second address happens to be visited at steps 5 and 18. This convention is what makes the tape rearrangement in §2.3 statable.

He adds that the conditions are stated for Turing machines only because that is "the most general case" — "similar conditions can easily be stated for finite-state machines" (p. 412, n. 4). Klein later argues this choice is not innocent (entry 2, and §2.7 below).

#### 2.2 Why the computational level is the one under attack

Before the construction, Maudlin isolates his target. Functionalism *as such* is nearly vacuous, he argues (p. 410): one of the "ways" a brain does what it does is by being a neurophysiological object, so an unconstrained functionalism is "bound to succeed, at least in giving sufficient conditions for consciousness… guaranteed if physicalism is true." The computational theory earns its content precisely by fixing a definite level of abstraction — the **machine table**, which "describes how the internal states of the machine are subjunctively connected to one another and to various input and output mechanisms" (p. 410). Its advantage is precision: you can inspect two physical systems and settle whether they have the same table.

He then measures how high that level of abstraction is, by contrast with Fodor's example of a **valve lifter** (p. 411). A valve lifter need not be made of any particular material, but it does face physical constraints — it must transmit enough energy to lift a valve, so "a weak photon cannot be a valve lifter… because it cannot provide the physical wherewithal". Functional concepts of that kind are ultimately anchored to a physical goal. A machine table is not. The only physical requirements it imposes are three, and they are thin (p. 411):

1. there must be at least as many physically distinguishable states of the system as there are machine states in the table;
2. the system must be capable of reacting to and changing the state of the tape;
3. there must be enough physical structure to support the subjunctive connections specified in the table.

Requirement 3 is the one Olympia is engineered to satisfy on the cheap. Footnote 3 (p. 411) makes the substrate-independence consequence explicit and traces it to Socrates the Younger via Aristotle's *Metaphysics*, quoting Searle's biological claim and Pylyshyn's reply about "anthropocentric chauvinism" — and then plants a marker that will be collected in §2.6: "there are other levels of description less abstract than the computational level which also sustain the possibility that the functional organization can be realized in almost any material."

#### 2.3 The construction, step by step

**The three things running π on τ requires** (p. 415). First, the machine must pass sequentially through N machine states. Second, the read/write head must visit `T_[0]` through `T_[N]`, changing entries where required. Third — and this is the implicit one — "the physical state of the machine must also support all of the counterfactuals inherent in the machine table." Not only must it go from `S_[0]` to `S_[1]` given that `T_[0]` actually holds a 0; it must be built so it *would have* gone to the state the table specifies had `T_[0]` held a 1, and so on for every state it might have entered, recursively. "The counterfactuals ramify geometrically."

**The anti-cheat restrictions** (p. 416). Maudlin rules three tricks out of order before starting, so the result cannot be dismissed as verbal:

- Machine states must be physically meaningful and state transitions must be physical activities. (This kills the degenerate case of a rock on a table whose "states" are *sitting there from 12:00 to 12:01* and *from 12:01 to 12:02*.)
- Tape addresses must be physically distinct entities, and an address's informational state must be specifiable in terms of its *intrinsic* physical state.
- The state of the machine must be definable without reference to the state of the tape, and vice versa.

"Even with these restrictions," he writes, "we shall see that any program can be run with an uncomfortably small amount of physical activity. Without them, the activity could be reduced even further."

**Klara and her tape** (pp. 416–417). Klara is any machine correctly programmed with π; her composition is unconstrained — "silicon or protein, electro-chemical or hydraulic", imagined as clockwork and possibly solar-system sized, and none of this matters to the argument. Her tape is constrained: a potentially infinite series of **water troughs**, each holding one bit by being full or empty, read by dropping a float in, written by opening a drain or opening a hose.

**Two permitted tape manipulations** (p. 417), each explicitly compensated so Klara's computational structure is untouched:

- *Reordering.* Rearrange any finite number of troughs arbitrarily, provided Klara gets a map. Where she used to move two troughs right, she now reads her address, computes the target address, looks it up on the map and moves her head there. "So the spatial sequence of the troughs need not reflect their 'computational sequence'."
- *Bi-location.* Join two troughs at different places with a pipe. They now jointly constitute one address, readable and writable at either end, "to any finite degree" of multiplicity.

**The block** (pp. 417–418): a device that halts Klara and freezes her in a particular machine state — for a clockwork, a piece of wood jammed between the gears. "The gear teeth of a blocked machine may well not even physically contact each other, remaining suspended motionless in space."

**Step 1 — straighten the tape** (p. 418). Using reordering, put `T_[0]`, `T_[1]`, `T_[2]`, … physically side by side in the order the computation visits them. Where the computation revisits an address (`T_[i] = T_[j]` for some `i < j`), bi-locate it: two troughs joined by a pipe, one at each position in the line. Klara's run on τ now consists, however elaborately she computes her next address, of her head moving one trough to the right each step.

**Step 2 — the armature** (pp. 418–419). Build the simplest device that performs *exactly that* sequence of tape manipulations: an armature that travels left to right, striking the rod on a drain plug where a trough must be emptied, and letting a hose run where one must be filled, with a barrier diverting the water where it must not. Define the armature's position over trough `T_[i]` as a distinct physical state. Then the armature already satisfies requirements (1) and (2): it passes through N distinct physical states in order, and manipulates the tape correctly and in order.

**Why the armature alone is not enough — by the computationalist's own lights** (pp. 419–420). "One cannot simply by fiat command that the armature position constitutes the appropriate machine state. A particular physical state only becomes interpretable as a machine state of a system programmed with π in virtue of standing in the right counterfactual or subjunctive relations to the tape and to the whole constellation of other states in the machine table for π." As it stands, "its operations are entirely oblivious to the state of the tape", so any computation ascribed to it "would be just a constant function that gives the same output for any input" — trivial "in as strong a sense as possible". And the *necessity condition* says trivial computation cannot support consciousness. Maudlin is emphatic that this is not the funny-instantiation ploy in disguise: "For all I know, appropriately constructed troughs and water hoses could have toothaches… The point is rather that the armature does not have the right kind of computational or information-processing structure to be conscious according to the computationalist's own thesis" (p. 420).

**Step 3 — floats, blocked Klaras and chains** (p. 421). This is the crux, and Maudlin states it as such: "the counterfactuals can be supported by machinery that is physically inert during the computation."

- At each trough `T_[i]`, attach a float, latched in place, the latch released only as the armature passes. The float is pre-set to the water level that `T_[i]` has at step *i* of the correct run on τ — up if full, down if empty. Hence on a run on τ, no float moves: when its latch opens, it is already at the right level. (Footnote 7, p. 421: the latch re-engages before the armature itself alters the level, so only the level at the moment of arrival matters.) On any tape τ′ whose contents diverge from τ, at least one float moves.
- Make **N + 2 copies of Klara**. Block the first in state `S_[0]` with its head in `T_[0]`; the second in `S_[1]` at `T_[1]`; and so on up to `S_[N+1]`.
- Chain the float at `T_[i]` to the block of the copy frozen in `S_[i]`, for `i = 0 … N`. A moving float pulls a block, and that copy of Klara — already in the right state at the right address — resumes.
- The copy blocked in `S_[N+1]` is set to run if the armature completes its trip from `T_[0]` to `T_[N]`, i.e. it takes over after the stretch of computation Olympia was built to cover.
- A **return chain** shuts the armature down the instant any blocked machine starts.

**The definition of Olympia's states** (p. 422; the scan garbles the subscripts, which are restored here from the surrounding text):

> Olympia is in state `S_i` if either one of the copies of Klara is unblocked and is in state `S_i`, or `S_i = S_[j]` for some `j` and the armature is running and passing over trough site `T_[j]`.

**Why this is enough to be running π** (p. 422). Start Olympia on τ: by construction she passes through exactly the machine states and performs exactly the tape manipulations π demands. Start her on any other tape: at exactly the step where the armature would diverge, a copy of Klara takes over and continues correctly. "Thus, no matter what the state of the tape, the sequence of machine states as defined and the sequence of manipulations of the tape will be in exact accordance with π. But being programmed with π is nothing over and above having just these dispositions."

#### 2.4 The bind, as numbered premises

Maudlin's section IV (pp. 422–423) can be laid out as follows. Every premise is either one of the three theses or an observation about the construction.

1. **(Sufficiency condition.)** Any system running π on τ through `S_[0]` … `S_[N]` supports φ.
2. **(Construction.)** Olympia, fully assembled, runs π on τ through `S_[0]` … `S_[N]`.
3. From 1 and 2: Olympia supports φ during that run.
4. **(Construction.)** During that run, the only physical activity anywhere in Olympia is the armature's traverse and the momentary unlatching of floats. In the N + 2 copies of Klara, "absolutely no physical activity occurs"; the floats, released, all remain still; "no physical or causal process need flow along the chains, that rest, we may imagine, inert and with links not even in physical contact."
5. **(Supervenience thesis.)** Any mode of consciousness occurring during the computation must supervene on the physical activity occurring during it. Therefore φ supervenes solely on the armature (and perhaps the floats); "the masses of idle machinery form no part of the supervenience space, since nothing happens there."
6. **(Established at p. 419–420.)** The armature alone cannot be interpreted as performing any nontrivial computation.
7. **(Necessity condition.)** Therefore φ cannot supervene on the armature's activity alone.
8. From 5 and 7: contradiction.

Maudlin's compressed statement (p. 423): "the computationalist is committed to the claims that the armature moving without the extra machinery hooked up cannot be conscious and that the system composed of the armature moving with the machinery hooked up must be conscious. But the physical activities that occur with and without the idle machinery connected are exactly identical, so these two claims contradict the supervenience thesis."

And the diagnosis he draws (p. 423): "the supervenience space of Olympia's computational description, indeed whether she is computing at all, depends vitally on the counterfactuals that the idle machinery supports. Hence, her conscious phenomenal states cannot derive from her computational structure."

#### 2.5 The replies Maudlin anticipates, and his answers

He observes that the computationalist cannot drop the sufficiency condition (it is the thesis) and that dropping the necessity condition "would tear the guts out of the notion that mental states arise from a complex computational structure" (pp. 423–424). So the pressure falls on the supervenience thesis, and there are two ways to take it.

**Reply A — "the physical activity is not the same in the two cases, because the counterfactuals differ."** Maudlin calls this the argument *by ipso facto*, "pleasingly short and crisp" but carrying "little conviction" (p. 424). Two objections.

*The slippery-slope objection.* If the computationalist may build whatever is handy into the individuation of physical process types, then "even the Cartesian dualist can embrace the supervenience thesis: wherever there is a difference in the state of a thinking substance, ipso facto there is a difference in physical activity." The thesis would become empty.

*The counterexample.* A deterministic pinball machine (credited to Kevin Kelly, p. 424, n. 8). Launch the ball identically and it traces an identical path. Remove some pins it never touches. The path is unchanged, and "the physicist's explanation of why it traces just that path will remain exactly the same. No motion or energy transfer or change of state occurs anywhere outside the path of the ball." Yet the counterfactuals differ: had the ball been pushed differently, its path would now diverge between the two setups. Footnote 9 pre-empts the subatomic quibble: "the facts would remain the same in a Democritean world where the matter is just solid and totally inert stuff." Conclusion (p. 425): "although the counterfactuals supervene on the entire physical state of the system, differences in counterfactuals about the evolution of the system need not imply differences in physical processes that are evolving at a time."

*The two refinements.* Because the sheer bulk of Olympia's machinery might make its removal feel like a physical change, Maudlin gives two variants in which the counterfactual support is destroyed by a change nobody could call a change in physical activity (p. 425):

- **Argument by Addition.** Add a second block to each Klara — a thin sheet of metal suspended between the frozen gear teeth, touching nothing. Were the first block ever removed, the gears would jam on it. The counterfactuals fail, so on the computationalist's view Olympia is now not conscious. "Given that the second blocks in fact never even touch any part of the machinery, exerting no physical influence or force at all, how could the physical activity taking place in Olympia during the first run be said to differ from that in the second? Speaking loosely, how could the rest of the system know that the blocks are even there?"
- **Argument by Subtraction.** Let the chains rust across many runs until they would break rather than pull. "No unusual physical effect accompanies the passage of the point of critical weakness, nor does the progressive rusting cause any other alteration in the structure of the device. Still, according to the computationalist, once the critical point has been passed, suddenly no toothache accompanies the passage of the armature from left to right."

**Reply B — "renounce the supervenience thesis altogether."** Maudlin's answer is in two parts (pp. 425–427). First, it does not help: "even if mental states supervene on more than just the physical activity of a system, the crucial role of the entirely isolated block remains inexplicable." Second, it costs the computationalist the research tradition the view grew out of. Translate the position to brains: suppose a perfected electroencephalograph records every neuron's firing across two periods and finds them identical — same neurons, same rates, same pattern. Researchers would infer the same conscious state. "But the computationalist now must raise a doubt. Perhaps some synaptic connection has been severed in the interim. Not a synaptic connection of any of the neurons which actually fired during either period" — yet it changes the counterfactuals, so it may change, or abolish, the experience. "I see no reason to endorse such possibilities that directly contradict all that we do know about brain process and experience" (p. 426).

There is also a *tu quoque*, aimed directly at the argument for computationalism that the corpus's pro-functionalist papers use (pp. 426–427). The standard silicon-prosthetic-neuron thought experiment — replace the brain piece by piece and note that nothing seems to change — "rests on a tacit appeal to supervenience. How could it matter, one asks, whether the electrical impulses are carried by neurons or by doped silicon?" If the computationalist now says that an untouched sheet of metal hanging in frozen machinery makes the difference between consciousness and none, "who knows what enormous changes in psychical state may result from replacing axons and dendrites with little copper wires? Should the computationalist reject the extremely general intuitions at play in assessing Olympia's case, no means of judging the plausibility or implausibility of any theory of mind seems to remain."

#### 2.6 The ladder of abstraction — where exactly Olympia bites

The most consequential passage for the field's history is Maudlin's own statement of how far up the abstraction ladder his conclusion reaches (pp. 427–428). He constructs four rungs and locates Olympia on the fourth.

| Rung | Supervenience base | Maudlin's verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The organic, electrochemical brain | Not in dispute |
| 2 | The pattern of electrical or electrochemical activity, however carried (the silicon brain) | "Has some plausibility"; a *stronger* supervenience thesis than Olympia's is needed to attack it, and Maudlin does not attack it |
| 3 | The pattern of causal interaction, with the physical nature of the causal process abstracted away (the hydraulic brain, pipes and water pulses for axons and impulses) | "Nothing has been said for the supervenience theses associated with these levels of abstraction, but neither has any objection been brought against them" (p. 429) |
| 4 | A counterfactual or information-theoretic connection, with no requirement that any causal chain connect the two events | This is the rung the computational theory occupies, and the one Olympia attacks |

His reason for the rung-2 exemption is precise and is the key to how his argument relates to Chalmers's fading-qualia argument: the silicon-brain case "requires a stronger supervenience thesis than ours, for the occurrent processes in the silicon brain are physically distinguishable from those in the organic brain in a way that the activities in Olympia with and without the extra blocks are not" (p. 427).

Rung 4 is characterised by the chains. "Our chains, lying slack and inert, support no physical activity at all, but they are nonetheless transmitting information. The intact chain transmits information to the blocked machine about the state of the tape, since, had the tape been different, the block would have been pulled. The rusted chain, lying equally slack and also with no causal process coursing through it, fails to transmit that information, since it would have broken if pulled" (p. 428, citing Dretske's *Knowledge and the Flow of Information* for the claim that information transmission needs no causal process). Hence: "two physical systems engaged in precisely the same physical activity can be processing information differently. And now Olympia can make her entrance."

Footnote 12 (pp. 428–429) distinguishes the argument from two neighbours.

- *Searle's Chinese Room.* Maudlin rejects Searle's inference (it establishes at most that any mental state arising would be disjoint from the man's), but notes: "the conclusion at which Olympia's tale arrives, however, is exactly that which Searle set out to prove: having a mind is not merely a matter of instantiating a program."
- *Zuboff's "The Story of a Brain".* Zuboff scatters a brain's causally disconnected neurons across the universe, driving them with "impulse cartridges" so the same firing pattern occurs. Maudlin argues the computationalist can answer this one, and that it in fact *helps* them: once the causal links are cut, the subjunctive connections that define the program fail, so the disintegrated collection no longer instantiates the same program. Computational structure is the stronger criterion that defeats Zuboff. "Olympia, however, shows that even this stronger criterion of identifying 'patterns of neural activity' is too weak."

#### 2.7 Assessing the damage — the dispositional retreat, and what survives

Section V (pp. 429–432) asks what else falls. Maudlin's own framing of why consciousness is the right target: it is *occurrent*. "Toothaches and other subjective experiences are events — they take place and are completely realized at or through a particular period of time" (pp. 429–430). Other mental properties — intelligence, understanding, intentionality — "seem much more dispositional", and "the more dispositional a property appears, the easier it is to contend that it supervenes not only on the activities but also on the dispositions of a system" (p. 430). So the computationalist has an escape: give up consciousness, "such a spooky phenomenon is usually not high on the list anyway", and keep intelligence and meaning.

Maudlin blocks it. "Although intelligence is more a capacity than an occurrent phenomenon, still capacities are realized in particular acts and activities." If having a program suffices for being intelligent, then solving a problem by running that program suffices for solving it *intelligently*. So build an Olympia whose armature-traverse instantiates just the stretch of program used on that problem — which the construction always permits, for any program and any finite run. Then: "When the armature moves with the secondary blocks not in place, Olympia solves the problem intelligently, but, if we move the blocks over a few centimetres and repeat the process, she will solve the problem stupidly." Or make the output linguistic: "Blocks out, and Olympia speaks with true intentionality and insight; blocks in, and she produces the very same strings in the very same way but does not mean anything by it" (pp. 430–431). Footnote 14 notes this is the shape of the AI proponents' own silicon-brain argument, quoting Pylyshyn's version against Searle, and leaves the reader to weigh the two intuitions against each other.

**The verdict on functionalism.** The paper's most quoted sentence about the relation between computationalism and functionalism is here (p. 431): "computationalism, which first seemed to be a version of functionalism, actually passes to a level of abstraction which undercuts functionalism's central insight. The functionalist demands that we look beyond the surface behavior to the causal structure of the processes that produce it… But, for a computationalist, the detailed structure of these inert and silent jungles of machinery makes all the difference. The verdict of the causal role functionalist is immensely the more plausible."

**What is conceded.** The paper is unusually explicit about the size of its own conclusion:

- "Our Olympia demonstrates that running a particular program cannot be a sufficient condition for having any form of mentality. But computational structure may yet be a necessary condition for consciousness or intelligence" (p. 431).
- "Nothing in these arguments suggests that consideration of counterfactuals or dispositions is irrelevant to questions of mentality and consciousness."
- What a computationally-necessary condition must be supplemented with is stated as a constraint: "it is a further constraint that the counterfactual relations that constitute the program structure must be supported *directly by the nature of physical activity in the system*."
- "The doleful tale of Olympia, then, does not purport to establish that things with minds must be wet and squishy. The silicon brain and the hydraulic brain may, for all we have said, be conscious" (p. 429).
- The construction's own cost is acknowledged: "The segregation of causally-active from counterfactual-supporting structure in Olympia is bought at a great price" — Olympia is "bloated with a vast galaxy of apparently redundant machinery" (p. 432).
- And AI practice might not go there: "Perhaps the pragmatic constraints that govern real AI research will naturally drive workers to create instantiated programs with just the right blending of computational and causal structure to support consciousness. But as yet we have no proof that pragmatic constraints should have any tendency to lead to this fortuitous result" (p. 432).

**The closing image**, which is how the argument is usually remembered (p. 432): Olympia is a cinema projector. "Like still frames of celluloid, each blocked copy of Klara has captured in an inert object a moment in the career of her operation. And as the projector's light brings momentarily to life each still photo in sequence, so the movement of the armature, the successive unlatching of the floats, momentarily makes the whole dispositional structure of each copy of Klara the dispositional structure of Olympia herself. The problem with computationalism is that it does not contain the conceptual resources to distinguish the flickering illusion from reality."

- Year · community: 1989 · philosophy-of-mind
- Question it asked: Can conscious experience be constituted by a system's computational structure, given that computational identity depends on counterfactuals while an occurrent experience appears to depend only on what a system actually does during the interval?
- Position in one line: The computationalist's sufficiency condition, necessity condition and the supervenience of occurrent consciousness on concurrent physical activity form an inconsistent triad, so running a particular program cannot be sufficient for any form of mentality.
- Stance on computational functionalism: rejects — but narrowly: it denies *computational sufficiency* while explicitly allowing that computational structure may be necessary, and while leaving substrate independence at the level of causal processes (silicon brains, hydraulic brains) untouched.
- Responds to / builds on: Searle 1980 "Minds, Brains and Programs" and its BBS commentators (Wilensky, Sloman & Croucher, Minsky, Pylyshyn), whose reply about disjoint mentalities Maudlin adopts against Searle; Fodor's *Psychological Explanation* (1968) for the valve-lifter contrast; Nagel on why consciousness makes the mind–body problem intractable; Dretske's *Knowledge and the Flow of Information* (1981) for information transmission without a causal process; Zuboff's "The Story of a Brain" (1981), which he argues the computationalist can answer; Glymour's "Silicon Reflections"; Aristotle's *Metaphysics* on Socrates the Younger.

- Evidence type: thought experiment
- Testability: The paper proposes no experiment, and its central claims are about what is conceptually possible. Maudlin offers this as a reductio, but as stated it is a disagreement about what a sufficiently complete recording of neural activity would settle, and the paper's positive demand — that program-constituting counterfactuals "must be supported directly by the nature of physical activity in the system" — is a constraint an account of implementation could be checked against.

## 2. Klein (2016 draft) — Computation, Consciousness, and "Computation and Consciousness" [tier: short]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Klein - Computation consciousness and Computation and Consciousness (context piece).pdf` — 19 pages, typeset in LaTeX, author Colin Klein (Macquarie University). **Venue and year as recorded from the PDF itself:** the title-page footnote reads "For *Routledge Handbook of the Computational Mind*, ed Mark Sprevak and Matteo Columbo. Draft 1D, compiled October 8, 2016." No journal, volume, page range, publisher or publication year is stated anywhere in the document; the only date it carries is the compile date, which the file's own metadata confirms (2016-10-08). It is therefore cited here as a 2016 draft chapter. The acknowledgements record support from Australian Research Council grant FT140100422. **PDF version: author web version** (a pre-publication draft of a handbook chapter).

**Claim.** Klein's thesis has two halves. Negatively: "To be clear, I do not think Maudlin's argument succeeds" (PDF p. 7). Positively: the argument nonetheless "has the feature of all beautiful thought experiments in philosophy: pushing back helps us clarify our assumptions", and what it forces is a reformulation of computationalism — away from input–output *functions* and towards *architectures* and the *processes* actually running in them, individuated mechanistically. On that reformulation, Olympia is not a Turing machine at all, and the reason has nothing to do with counterfactuals.

**Method or type.** A survey-and-position chapter for a reference handbook: conceptual analysis, built from three lay illustrations, a three-premise schematic reconstruction of Maudlin, a taxonomy of the neighbouring arguments it is confused with, and a constructive proposal.

**Key result or argument.** The chapter opens with three preliminaries, each isolating one premise. **Ms W** is a viola de gamba player in an orchestra that never programmes early music; subscribers argue over whether her silent presence enhances the performances. The question, Klein says, is whether aesthetic value is a *counterfactually loaded* property — one that depends on what could have happened, not only what did. **The Action Max** was a 1980s light-gun "console" that played an ordinary VCR tape: nothing you did changed the outcome. It is not a video game, whatever the screen shows, because "what makes something a game is options and contingency" — being a video game is counterfactually loaded. Generalised to computation: the Turing machine Simon factors his input properly; the discount model Theodore merely replays a recording of what Simon did on one input τ. "Theodore is not just worse than Simon; his lack of counterfactual sensitivity means he doesn't even count as computing in the first place" (PDF p. 3). Klein stresses this is not an optional nicety: counterfactual requirements are what stop the triviality arguments of Searle (1990) and Putnam (1991), on which buckets of water and stretches of brick implement arbitrary automata. **Anaesthetics** isolate the other premise: whatever inhalational anaesthetics do to neural membranes, "what makes a difference is that neurons that *would have* fired are silenced… the already quiet ones aren't making a difference to what you experience" (PDF p. 4). Phenomenal experience over an interval supervenes on what the brain actually does over it. Klein grants this is not universally held, and names the dissenter: integrated information theory, whose cause–effect space means "both active and inactive elements count" — quoting Fekete and Edelman's complaint that on Tononi's view reversibly cooling silent units should alter experience, and that "making experience depend on a potentiality without explaining how it actually comes to pass falls short of completing the explanatory move".

Maudlin's argument is then given in three lines (PDF p. 6):

> 1. If computationalism is true, then there is some Turing machine π which, when run on input τ, is sufficient to have phenomenal experience.
> 2. Two things can differ in whether they implement π because of differences in wholly inert machinery.
> 3. Two things cannot differ in whether they are conscious because of differences in wholly inert machinery.
> [Contradiction] ∴ Computationalism is false.

Premise 2 is what Olympia is built to secure, and Klein gives a stripped-down version: **Alvin** is Theodore (the replay machine) with an extra sensor and a powered-down copy of Simon strapped to his back; deviate from τ and the sensor switches Theodore off and Simon on. Alvin has Simon's full counterfactual range, so he computes; insert a passive piece of insulation in the right place and he does not; and on a run on τ his actual activity is identical to non-computing, non-conscious Theodore's.

Klein then separates Maudlin from two arguments it is routinely mistaken for — a separation he says is why "the reception of the argument has been blunted". Against the **exploding-implementation** family (Searle 1990; Putnam 1991; Chalmers 1996b; Bishop 2009a,b): "Maudlin's argument does not require exploding implementation to be true… Indeed, he presupposes (at least for the sake of argument) that appropriate counterfactual restrictions are sufficient to avoid explosions. One might instead reframe his argument as a dilemma for the computationalist: either one accepts explosion, or else one accepts a modal mismatch between computation and consciousness" (PDF p. 8). Against the **funny-implementation** family (Block's Chinese Nation): Klein agrees with Maudlin that the force is different — "the argument is not merely that machines like Alvin look strange. Rather, the argument is that Theodore isn't conscious by the computationalist's own lights because he does not compute, and Alvin does not differ from Theodore in ways that ought to matter for consciousness." He notes Fekete and Edelman (2011) as a case of the misreading. Against **Searle**: Searle's worry is that something must interpret a system *as* a computer, whereas "it doesn't make sense to ask whether something 'counts as' conscious"; Maudlin's argument is "related, but more sparse", with no issue about symbol interpretation.

The constructive half (PDF pp. 9–16) starts from an oddity: Maudlin's necessity condition reads computationalism narrowly as a claim about Turing machines, with no defence given. Quoting Sprevak (2007): "it is not true that a universal computer can run any program. The programs that a computer (universal or otherwise) can run depend on that machine's architecture." Klein then argues that consciousness cannot depend on which *mathematical function* a system computes, by a primitive-operation argument: any computable function can be made a primitive in some architecture — chip designers do exactly this, and he cites the Intel 8087's primitive trigonometric approximations. So take any function whose computation is supposed to suffice for an extended conscious episode, and posit an architecture with it as a single primitive. "But surely, executing a single primitive in a single computational step is not enough for conscious experience. From the computational point of view, there's no structure at all there" — and phenomenal experience plainly has combinatorial structure. The same diagnosis is offered for Block's lookup-table objection: lookup tables are suspect not because they are too big to build or because they fail to be computations, but because lookup requires "a batch of information to be present as a computational primitive", collapsing the structure. The working vocabulary he proposes: an **architecture** is a set of primitive operations and basic resources (after Pylyshyn 1984); a **computational process** is a temporally extended series of primitive operations; a **program** is a set of directions for building a process, which must itself be interpreted or compiled. He warns that compilation is why computationalism should not be stated over programs either — an optimising compiler can unroll loops or bake control logic into a lookup table, turning an apparently complex program into a process with the complexity packed into one primitive (the point Bishop 2009b exploits).

That machinery yields the diagnosis of Olympia (PDF pp. 13–16). Consider an architecture **A** with the Turing machine's primitives plus one extra *lookup* instruction: in a given state, transform the tape according to a pre-determined sequence, switching to a machine table at the point where input diverges. Olympia and Alvin belong to A. Nothing constrains A machines to track what a Turing machine would do — some could change the tape in ways no Turing machine could — so "A machines are different in kind from TMs, even when they happen to act in similar ways". The deeper objection is to Maudlin's implicit criterion: "On Maudlin's view of computation, there is no space between 'acting like a Turing machine' and 'being a Turing machine.' The latter is just defined in terms of the former. But there's good reason to reject this notion, precisely because it can't distinguish between implementation and emulation." Klein's preferred account makes an architecture a set of *mechanisms* (Piccinini 2007, 2015) with spatiotemporal parts interacting causally — and by that standard Olympia and Alvin "don't have the right set of parts interacting in the right way". A diagnostic test is offered: making Alvin compute a different function on τ requires changing only the recorded sequence, whereas changing a Turing machine's function "can require arbitrarily many changes to the machine table". The motivation is interventionist (Woodward 2003; Craver 2007): an architecture's primitives are "the basic loci of intervention upon a computational system", which is why this departs from accounts that rest on isomorphism between machine tables and implementing machinery, Chalmers (2011) named. Klein places his own earlier work and the rest of the reply literature in the same mechanistic family: Barnes (1991) on the causal chain from input to output, Klein (2008) on dispositions needing a categorical basis in well-defined parts, Bartlett (2012) objecting that Klein never says what a well-defined part is.

The consequence Klein accepts is stated without hedging (PDF p. 16). Emulating a Turing machine on a laptop does not give the laptop a Turing machine architecture — "its spatiotemporal parts are just as they were when they left the factory"; at best it has a *virtual* architecture running on the actual one. "For if the path we've been led down is the right one, then we computationalists must say something that is suspiciously close to what Searle (1980) says — there is no reason to think that a simulation of an architecture is sufficient for consciousness. Virtual machines, even the virtual machine that would arise from a simulation of my brain in all computationally relevant detail, are not the right kinds of thing to support conscious experience. The fact that a virtual machine has similar 'organizational invariants' (Chalmers 1996a) to an actual architecture does not show that it is the same as a process that actually has that architecture." He declines Searle's pessimism, however: "if (as Maudlin claims) computation depends on actual activity, then we really ought to look at the actual activity of actual computing processes. If I think that a certain kind of TM is enough to be conscious, then something actually has to get down on its knees and move around the tape in order to be conscious. Once we do, I suspect, we may well find that the constraints on architectures are actually pretty minimal." Which architectures satisfy the constraints "is a nontrivial empirical question". A closing footnote registers a reservation about Fekete and Edelman's stricter proposal (that only architectures describable as trajectories through state space qualify), on the ground that it would rule out any architecture with discrete-state transitions, including Turing machines, and that a sequence of disconnected images seems to produce non-continuous phenomenal transitions.

**Place in the field's history.** This is the corpus's only retrospective on Olympia, and it is written from inside computationalism by someone who has published on the implementation question for a decade (Klein 2008, 2012, 2013, 2015). It has three historiographic functions. First, it records the reception: Klein says the argument "remains relatively obscure, in part because the bulk of his paper is devoted to constructing an elaborate example that is easily misinterpreted", and names the misreadings. Second, it maps the reply literature — Barnes (1991) in *The Journal of Philosophy*, Klein (2008) in *Synthese*, Bishop (2009a,b), Bartlett (2012) in *Erkenntnis*, described as "a task mostly done, albeit in a Maudlin-sympathetic way, by (Bartlett, 2012)" — which is the nearest thing the corpus has to a bibliography of the debate. Third, it converts an anti-computationalist argument into a constraint *within* computationalism, aligning the outcome with the mechanistic account of physical computation that Piccinini and Coelho Mollo develop elsewhere in this corpus. Footnote 4 also flags a separate result of Klein's own: "I have argued elsewhere that Olympia is herself actually an oracle machine (Klein, 2015)" — i.e. that the construction, properly analysed, computes a function no Turing machine can.

**Relevance.** For the synthesis, Klein supplies the bridge between the 1989 argument and the modern implementation literature, and a clean statement of the fault line: computation is counterfactually loaded, consciousness is not, and every response must give up, restrict or re-describe one of those. It also supplies a striking convergence to record. Klein, a computationalist reasoning from mechanistic individuation, ends at the same verdict about whole-brain simulation that Tononi and Koch reach from integrated information theory and Searle reaches from biological naturalism — that simulating a brain in full computational detail would not produce consciousness — while sharing no premise with either. Three incompatible routes to one conclusion is a fact about the field's history worth recording as such.

- Question it asked: Does Maudlin's Olympia argument refute computationalism about consciousness, and if not, what does it force the computationalist to say?
- Position in one line: The argument fails, but only because computationalism was mis-stated: once computation is individuated mechanistically by architecture and actual process rather than by input–output function or by what a machine would do, Olympia turns out to *emulate* a Turing machine rather than implement one, and the surviving computationalism must locate consciousness in actual computational processes.
- Stance on computational functionalism: restricts — it defends computationalism against Maudlin while narrowing it to claims about mechanistically individuated architectures and the processes actually running in them, and explicitly denies that a simulation or virtual machine is a candidate for consciousness.
- Responds to / builds on: Maudlin 1989 (the target); Sprevak 2007 on program portability and architecture-relativity; Piccinini 2007, 2015 on computing mechanisms; Pylyshyn 1984 for the notion of architecture; Cantwell Smith 1996 on programs as prescriptions; Woodward 2003 and Craver 2007 for interventionism and mutual manipulability; Chalmers 1996b, 2011 and 1996a (*The Conscious Mind*, organizational invariants), the isomorphism accounts he departs from; Searle 1980, 1990 and Putnam 1991 on triviality, distinguished from Maudlin; Block 1978 (Chinese Nation) and 1981 (lookup tables), both re-diagnosed; Fekete & Edelman 2011 against Tononi 2004 and Tononi & Koch 2015 on whether inactive elements contribute to experience; Barnes 1991, Klein 2008, 2012, 2013, 2015, Bartlett 2012, Bishop 2009a,b, the reply literature; Dennett 1998 on lookup tables; Aaronson 2015 on computational complexity for philosophers.
- Key move: The architecture-versus-function reformulation, and with it the **implementation/emulation distinction** — Olympia and Alvin belong to an architecture with a lookup primitive, so they act like a Turing machine without being one — supported by the primitive-operation argument that any computable function can be a single architectural primitive, which makes function-level computationalism collapse conscious episodes into structureless single steps.
- Evidence type: conceptual analysis (handbook review chapter with a constructive position)
- Testability: No experiment is proposed, and the chapter is explicit that the empirical question is downstream: which architectures meet the constraints "is a nontrivial empirical question", and architectural differences matter because they fix the time and space required to compute a function, so computational-complexity profiles "may well matter for consciousness". The framework also yields a discriminating criterion that could in principle be applied to a real device — whether changing the computed function requires many changes to a machine table or a single change to a recorded sequence — and an interventionist criterion, that an architecture's primitives are the basic loci of intervention on the system.
- PDF version: author web version

## Cross-paper notes for the synthesis

**1. The single fault line, in one sentence.** Both papers agree on a mismatch and disagree about what it costs: computation is a *counterfactually loaded* property (what a system computes depends on what it would do), while occurrent conscious experience appears *not* to be (what you feel now depends on what your brain is actually doing now). Maudlin concludes that computationalism about consciousness is therefore impossible; Klein concludes that computationalism must be re-stated so that Olympia never counted as a computer in the first place. Every other reply in the literature Klein surveys — Barnes 1991, Klein 2008, Bartlett 2012 — takes the second route, appealing to the causal structure of the implementing mechanism.

**2. Relation to Chalmers 1996 (`Does a rock implement every finite-state automaton?`).** This is the tightest link in the corpus and the reason batch F exists. Chalmers 1996 saves computationalism from Putnam's and Searle's triviality arguments by requiring that implementation involve **strong conditionals** — state-transition conditionals with modal force, which "are required to support counterfactuals: *if* the system were to be in state p, then it would transit into state q" (Chalmers 1996, pp. 312–313). Maudlin's argument takes that very requirement as its premise 2: if counterfactuals are constitutive of computational organization, then two systems whose actual physical activity is identical can differ in what they compute, and computational organization cannot fix an occurrent phenomenal state. Chalmers sees this and says so, in note 3 (p. 332):

> "Maudlin (1989) raises some very interesting questions about along these lines, questioning the relevance of counterfactual-involving conditionals to conscious experience. For instance, if one blocks the connection that supports some counterfactual conditional in a currently static part of the system, is it plausible that this could change or remove the system's conscious experience? Maudlin suggests that it could not. We have seen, however, that such conditionals are constitutive of computational organization. Using an elaborate construction, Maudlin makes an argument along these lines to the conclusion that cognition is not based in computation. This argument requires an in-depth treatment in its own right."

Two things to record. First, the note *concedes the conjunction* — Chalmers grants both that Maudlin's intuition is plausible and that the conditionals are constitutive — and then defers. No in-depth treatment appears in that paper, and none appears anywhere else in this corpus. Second, Klein's framing sharpens what the deferral is a deferral *of*: because Maudlin grants for the sake of argument that counterfactual restrictions do successfully block triviality, his argument is best read as a **dilemma**, not a refutation — "either one accepts explosion, or else one accepts a modal mismatch between computation and consciousness" (Klein, PDF p. 8). Chalmers's paper is the canonical defence of the first horn while leaving the second horn unaddressed. Anyone drawing the corpus's argument graph should draw this edge and mark it open.

**3. Relation to Chalmers 1995 (`Absent qualia, fading qualia, dancing qualia`).** Chalmers 1995 argues for a **principle of organizational invariance** — "any system that has the same functional organization at a fine enough grain will have qualitatively identical conscious experiences" — by gradually replacing neurons with functionally identical silicon components and arguing that neither fading nor dancing qualia are coherent. Maudlin addresses this argument form directly (pp. 426–427, 429), and his treatment is the opposite of a refutation:

- It is a *tu quoque*. The gradual-replacement argument, he says, "rests on a tacit appeal to supervenience. How could it matter, one asks, whether the electrical impulses are carried by neurons or by doped silicon?" So the computationalist who answers Olympia by saying that an inert sheet of metal changes the facts has thereby weakened the very intuition the silicon-brain argument relies on.
- It concedes the case. The silicon-brain argument "requires a **stronger** supervenience thesis than ours, for the occurrent processes in the silicon brain are physically distinguishable from those in the organic brain in a way that the activities in Olympia with and without the extra blocks are not." Maudlin's thesis says nothing about physically *different* activity; it says only that physically *identical* activity supports identical experience. So Olympia leaves fading/dancing qualia untouched: "The silicon brain and the hydraulic brain may, for all we have said, be conscious" (p. 429).

The right statement of the relation is therefore that the two arguments are aimed at different rungs of Maudlin's ladder of abstraction. Chalmers 1995 defends rung 2 (any substrate carrying the same pattern of activity) and, at a stretch, rung 3 (any causal process with the same pattern of interaction). Maudlin attacks only rung 4 (a counterfactual or information-theoretic connection with no causal chain required). A synthesis that presents Olympia as an argument against substrate independence would be misreading it — the paper's own words are that it "does not purport to establish that things with minds must be wet and squishy."

There is also a scope difference worth noting. Chalmers 1995's own conclusion is explicitly the weaker, *nonreductive* functionalism — "functional organization suffices for conscious experience with natural necessity" rather than constitutively — because the arguments' premise is empirical. Maudlin's target, the sufficiency condition, is a claim of the same natural-necessity strength, so the two are in genuine contact rather than talking past each other.

**4. What Maudlin concedes, catalogued.** The paper is often cited as a blanket anti-computationalist argument. It is not, and the concessions are load-bearing for the field's history:
- Funny-instantiation intuitions carry no weight ("I cannot think of one reason to accord those intuitions any weight", p. 414).
- Searle's Chinese Room inference fails; disjoint mentalities are perfectly possible (pp. 414–415). Olympia reaches Searle's *conclusion* by a route that rejects Searle's *argument* (n. 12).
- Slowed clocks and periodically disassembled machines are odd but not an objection (p. 415).
- Zuboff's scattered brain is answerable by the computationalist, and even supports them (n. 12).
- Silicon brains and hydraulic brains may be conscious (p. 429).
- Computational structure may still be a *necessary* condition for consciousness or intelligence (p. 431).
- Real AI practice may happen to produce the right blend of computational and causal structure — there is simply no proof that it would (p. 432).
What dies is exactly one thesis: **computational sufficiency**.

**5. A convergence across incompatible premises.** Klein, defending computationalism, concludes that "even the virtual machine that would arise from a simulation of my brain in all computationally relevant detail" is not a candidate for consciousness, because a virtual architecture is not an actual one. Tononi and Koch (batch D) reach the same verdict from integrated information theory ("a simulation of our conscious brain will not have consciousness"), and Searle (batch C) from biological naturalism. Three mutually inconsistent positions converge on the simulation question. This is a useful structural fact for the synthesis, and a caution: agreement that a simulated brain would not be conscious is *not* evidence of a shared underlying view, and the routes should be kept distinct on any diagram.
