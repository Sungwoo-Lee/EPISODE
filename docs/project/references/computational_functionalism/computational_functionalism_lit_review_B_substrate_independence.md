# Computational Functionalism Field History — Part B: Substrate Independence and Its Thought Experiments (1980–2003)

**Topic folder:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/`
**Part of:** a multi-file, neutral review of how the idea developed that a mind is constituted by the *computations* a system performs. Companion parts cover classical functionalism and multiple realizability (A1), implementation and the computational theory of cognition (A2), the biological-naturalism challenge (C), theories of consciousness used as tests (D), and the recent AI-minds literature (E).
**This part:** 4 papers, reviewed one at a time in chronological order, 2026-09-21. 2 more are named but not reviewed because no PDF is held.
**Reviewer:** literature-reviewer
**Neutrality:** this document reports positions; it does not adjudicate between them. The two sides here disagree sharply and both are stated at full strength.

## What this part is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question.** Suppose you built a machine out of silicon chips — or wires, or water pipes, or a billion people passing radio messages — that did *exactly* what your brain does, step for step, at whatever level of detail you like. Would it have a mind? Would it feel anything? "Substrate independence" is the name for the answer *yes, the material doesn't matter, only the pattern of activity does.* Part B collects the four arguments in this corpus that fight over that answer.

**The two positions, at their strongest.**
- **Against (Searle, 1980).** Running a program cannot be *enough* for understanding, because you can be the program and still not understand. Searle asks you to imagine yourself locked in a room, shuffling Chinese symbols according to an English rulebook, producing answers that native Chinese speakers accept as fluent — while you understand no Chinese whatsoever. He calls the room's lesson: a program has "only a syntax but no semantics" (p. 422). His positive claim is biological: brains cause minds the way the pancreas causes insulin, and any other machine would have to have "causal powers equal to those of the brain" (p. 417).
- **For (Cuda 1985; Chalmers 1995).** Both take the same route: instead of imagining an all-at-once replacement, replace your brain *one neuron at a time*, with each replacement doing the departing neuron's job exactly. Now ask the opponent to say *when* the lights go out. Every available answer is uncomfortable. If consciousness vanishes abruptly at one neuron, nature contains a cliff-edge law found nowhere else. If it dims gradually, then somewhere in the middle there is a person whose experience has faded to a whisper who nonetheless sincerely reports vivid colour and cannot tell anything is wrong — because, by construction, his brain works exactly as yours does. Chalmers adds a second, sharper version: wire a silicon backup circuit into your head with a **switch**, and if substrate matters your experience would flip between red and blue as the switch is thrown while you notice nothing at all. He calls these **Fading Qualia** and **Dancing Qualia**.

**The fourth paper is different in kind.** Bostrom (2003) is not arguing about substrate independence at all. His famous conclusion — that at least one of three things must be true: nearly all civilisations die before maturing technologically, or nearly none of the mature ones bother running simulations of their ancestors, or we are almost certainly in such a simulation — *rests* on substrate independence as an imported premise. He says so plainly and declines to defend it: "Arguments for this thesis have been given in the literature, and although it is not entirely uncontroversial, I shall here take it as given" (p. 244). He is included here because he shows the thesis doing load-bearing work in a field outside philosophy of mind, and because he states the *weakest* version of it anybody needs.

**What the four together settle, and what they don't.** Nobody in this batch denies that a machine could in principle have a mind. Searle says so explicitly — "only a machine could think" (p. 417), and whether silicon has the right causal powers "is an empirical question" (p. 452). The genuine dispute is narrower and sharper: is *organisation alone* sufficient, or is organisation only a symptom of something else — the causal powers — that a real mind needs? Cuda and Chalmers both argue that the gradual-replacement scenarios make the "something else" view very expensive. Neither claims a proof. Chalmers is explicit that his arguments establish only *empirical* impossibility, not logical impossibility, and that they leave his position "just as compatible with certain forms of property dualism about experience as with certain forms of physicalism" (§5).

The Chalmers PDF is the author's own web version and its header names a different publication than the manifest does. Details in [Reading conventions](#reading-conventions).

  - *Intentionality* (Searle, p. 424 n. 3): "that feature of certain mental states by which they are directed at or about objects and states of affairs in the world." Beliefs, desires and intentions are intentional; undirected anxiety is not.
  - *Intrinsic intentionality* vs *observer-relative ascriptions of intentionality* (Searle, Author's Response, pp. 451–452): actual mental states versus the way we speak of things that lack them ("the word 'Carter' refers to the president").
  - *Syntax* vs *semantics* (Searle, p. 422): formal symbol shape versus meaning. "The computer, to repeat, has a syntax but no semantics."
  - *Causal powers of the brain* (Searle, p. 417): whatever it is about brains that produces intentionality; Searle leaves its physical nature an open empirical question.
  - *Circuit functional equivalence* (Cuda, p. 125): reproducing the functional role of each neuron in a normal human brain — the *finest* useful grain, and the only one Cuda claims to establish.
  - *Functional organization* (Chalmers, §1): "the abstract pattern of causal interaction between the components of a system, and perhaps between these components and external inputs and outputs", specified by a number of abstract components, their possible states, and the dependency relations among them — with "the nature of the components and the states … left unspecified".
  - *Principle of organizational invariance* (Chalmers, §1): any system with the same fine-grained functional organization as a conscious system has qualitatively identical experiences.
  - *Empirical (natural, nomological) possibility* vs *logical/metaphysical possibility* (Chalmers, §2): the distinction the whole paper turns on. Chalmers concedes the logical possibility of everything he argues against.
  - *Substrate-independence* (Bostrom, p. 244): "mental states can supervene on any of a broad class of physical substrates."
  - *Qualia*, *absent qualia*, *inverted qualia*: as in [Part A1](computational_functionalism_lit_review_A1_functionalism_origins.md) — the felt character of experience; its total absence despite identical organisation; its systematic difference despite identical organisation.
- **Project vocabulary.** Searle mentions "pain" and "love" as examples of mental states, and Chalmers uses a throbbing headache as a running example. That usage is theirs. In this project's own artificial agents the internal damage signal is **nociception**, never "pain".

## 1. Searle (1980) — Minds, Brains, and Programs [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Searle 1980 - Minds brains and programs.pdf` — published version, *The Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 3, pp. 417–457, Cambridge Core scan. The scan includes the open peer commentary section, which is **not** reviewed here; see [Reading conventions](#reading-conventions). Searle's own Author's Response (pp. 450–455) **is** reviewed and is marked as such throughout.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Searle opens by drawing a line that the rest of the paper depends on (p. 417):
- **Weak AI:** the computer is "a very powerful tool" for formulating and testing hypotheses about the mind. Searle has no objection to this.
- **Strong AI:** "the appropriately programmed computer really is a mind, in the sense that computers given the right programs can be literally said to understand and have other cognitive states." Here "the programs are not mere tools … rather, the programs are themselves the explanations."

Only strong AI is the target. His example program is Schank and Abelson's (1977) script-based story understander — the one that answers "Did the man eat the hamburger?" correctly for a story that never says so — but he stresses that "nothing that follows depends upon the details of Schank's programs. The same arguments would apply to Winograd's SHRDLU, Weizenbaum's ELIZA, and indeed any Turing machine simulation of human mental phenomena" (p. 417).

**The thought experiment.** Searle proposes a test: ask what it would be like if your own mind actually worked the way the theory says all minds work. Applied to Schank's program, that gives the **Chinese room** (pp. 417–418). You are locked in a room and handed batches of Chinese writing which, to you, is "just so many meaningless squiggles". You also get a rulebook *in English* for correlating one batch of symbols with another, purely by their shapes. Unknown to you, the people outside call the first batch a script, the second a story, the third questions, your outputs answers, and the rulebook the program. You get good enough that your Chinese answers are indistinguishable from a native speaker's. They also hand you English stories, which you understand and answer normally.

The verdict: "it seems to me quite obvious in the example that I do not understand a word of the Chinese stories. I have inputs and outputs that are indistinguishable from those of the native Chinese speaker, and I can have any formal program you like, but I still understand nothing" (p. 418).

**Key findings (as argued).**
1. **Instantiating a program is not sufficient for understanding.** That is the conclusion the room is built to deliver, and Searle states the form of the argument abstractly: "whatever purely formal principles you put into the computer, they will not be sufficient for understanding, since a human will be able to follow the formal principles without understanding anything" (p. 418).
2. **Nor is there evidence that it is necessary or contributory.** "no reason has been given to suppose that when I understand English I am operating with any formal program at all" (p. 418).
3. **Six standard replies fail** — the Systems reply, the Robot reply, the Brain Simulator reply, the Combination reply, the Other Minds reply, and the Many Mansions reply. The single most-cited counter is to the first: **internalise the whole system**. Memorise the rulebook and the data banks, do all the calculations in your head, work outdoors. "there isn't anything in the system that isn't in him" (p. 419).
4. **The positive claim is biological, not chauvinist about silicon.** "I see no reason in principle why we couldn't give a machine the capacity to understand English or Chinese, since in an important sense our bodies with our brains are precisely such machines" (p. 422). Martians with differently-constituted brains might have intentionality: "That is an empirical question, rather like the question whether photosynthesis can be done by something with a chemistry different from that of chlorophyll" (p. 422).
5. **The slogan.** "they have only a syntax but no semantics" (p. 422). And: "Such intentionality as computers appear to have is solely in the minds of those who program them and those who use them."
6. **The diagnosis.** Three things make strong AI look plausible and all three are mistakes (pp. 423–424): an ambiguity in "information processing"; a residual behaviourism ("The Turing test is typical of the tradition in being unashamedly behavioristic and operationalistic"); and — the most provocative claim in the paper — a **residual dualism**. "strong AI only makes sense given the dualistic assumption that, where the mind is concerned, the brain doesn't matter … the same program could be realized by an electronic machine, a Cartesian mental substance, or a Hegelian world spirit."

**Initial takeaway.** The paper is widely remembered as "Searle says machines can't think". It says the opposite: "only a machine could think, and indeed only very special kinds of machines, namely brains and machines that had the same causal powers as brains" (p. 424). The real thesis is that *the program is the wrong thing to point at.* Whatever the brain does to produce intentionality, "it cannot consist in instantiating a program since no program, by itself, is sufficient for intentionality" (p. 424). Whether some *other* artificial system could do it is, on Searle's own account, an open empirical question — a point he has to repeat several times in his Author's Response because the commentators kept missing it.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### The argument's official structure

Searle sets it out in the abstract as five numbered propositions (p. 417), and it is worth reconstructing them as stated because the logical dependencies are explicit.

- **(1)** *Intentionality in human beings (and animals) is a product of causal features of the brain.* Status: "an empirical fact about the actual causal relations between mental processes and brains. It says simply that certain brain processes are **sufficient** for intentionality."
- **(2)** *Instantiating a computer program is never by itself a sufficient condition of intentionality.* Status: "The main argument of this paper is directed at establishing this claim. The form of the argument is to show how a human agent could instantiate the program and still not have the relevant intentionality."
- **(3)** *The explanation of how the brain produces intentionality cannot be that it does it by instantiating a computer program.* "This is a strict logical consequence of 1 and 2."
- **(4)** *Any mechanism capable of producing intentionality must have causal powers equal to those of the brain.* "This is meant to be a trivial consequence of 1."
- **(5)** *Any attempt literally to create intentionality artificially (strong AI) could not succeed just by designing programs but would have to duplicate the causal powers of the human brain.* "This follows from 2 and 4."

Note the shape. Only (2) is argued for by the Chinese room; (1) is asserted as empirical fact; (3)–(5) are presented as consequences. A critic who grants (2) but denies (1) — or who reads "causal powers equal to those of the brain" as satisfiable by organisation alone — is not touched by the room. Both Cuda and Chalmers take approximately that route.

#### The six replies, as Searle states and answers them

Searle names each reply by its geographic origin, which is itself a small piece of field history.

| Reply | The objection (Searle's statement of it) | Searle's counter |
|---|---|---|
| **I. Systems** (Berkeley) | The man doesn't understand, "but the fact is that he is merely part of a whole system, and the system does understand" — the man plus ledger, scratch paper and data banks (p. 419). | **Internalisation.** "let the individual internalize all of these elements of the system. He memorizes the rules in the ledger and the data banks of Chinese symbols, and he does all the calculations in his head. … We can even get rid of the room and suppose he works outdoors." Against the *subsystems* variant: the English subsystem "knows that 'hamburgers' refers to hamburgers"; the Chinese subsystem "knows only that 'squiggle squiggle' is followed by 'squoggle squoggle'" (p. 419). Two further charges: the reply's **only** motivation is that the system passes the Turing test, "but precisely one of the points at issue is the adequacy of the Turing test", so it begs the question; and it has "consequences that are independently absurd" — stomach, heart and liver become understanding subsystems, since "there is no principled way to distinguish the motivation for saying the Chinese subsystem understands from saying that the stomach understands" (pp. 419–420). |
| **II. Robot** (Yale) | Put the computer in a robot with a television camera and limbs, so that its symbols are causally connected to perception and action (p. 420). | Two moves. First, the reply "tacitly concedes that cognition is not solely a matter of formal symbol manipulation." Second, run the room inside the robot: some incoming symbols now come from the camera and some outgoing ones drive the motors, "without knowing either of these facts. I am the robot's homunculus, but unlike the traditional homunculus, I don't know what's going on" (p. 420). |
| **III. Brain simulator** (Berkeley and MIT) | Don't simulate world-knowledge; simulate "the actual sequence of neuron firings at the synapses of the brain of a native Chinese speaker", even in parallel. "At the level of the synapses, what would or could be different about the program of the computer and the program of the Chinese brain?" (p. 420). | First a jab: "it is an odd reply for any partisan of artificial intelligence (or functionalism, etc.) to make: I thought the whole idea of strong AI is that we don't need to know how the brain works" (p. 421). Then the **water pipes**: the man operates valves; "Each water connection corresponds to a synapse in the Chinese brain", and the Chinese answers pop out the far end. He can internalise this too — "the man can internalize the formal structure of the water pipes and do all the 'neuron firings' in his imagination." The diagnosis: "The problem with the brain simulator is that it is simulating the wrong things about the brain. As long as it simulates only the formal structure of the sequence of neuron firings at the synapses, it won't have simulated what matters about the brain, namely its causal properties, its ability to produce intentional states" (p. 421). **This is the exact passage Cuda 1985 quotes as his target.** |
| **IV. But the attribution rests on appearance and behaviour, not on the program: "We wouldn't need to know in advance that its computer brain was a formal analogue of the human brain." Learn that a symbol-shuffling man is inside and "we would regard the robot as an ingenious mechanical dummy". The contrast case is animals: with apes and dogs "we can see that the beasts are made of similar stuff to ourselves — that is an eye, that a nose, this is its skin" (p. 421). |

Many mansions** (Berkeley) | Whatever the causal processes are that you say matter, eventually we will build devices that have them — and that will be AI (p. 422). | "it in effect trivializes the project of strong AI by redefining it as whatever artificially produces and explains cognition. The interest of the original claim … is that it was a precise, well defined thesis: mental processes are computational processes over formally defined elements. … If the claim is redefined so that it is no longer that thesis, my objections no longer apply because there is no longer a testable hypothesis for them to apply to" (p. 422). |

#### Where "mind : brain :: program : hardware" breaks down

Searle grants that the analogy is what makes strong AI attractive — "Precisely that feature of AI that seemed so appealing — the distinction between the program and the realization — proves fatal to the claim that simulation could be duplication" (p. 422) — and then lists three breaks (pp. 422–423):

1. **Crazy realizations.** The same program has realisations with no intentionality at all. Searle cites Weizenbaum (1976, ch. 2), who "shows in detail how to construct a computer using a roll of toilet paper and a pile of small stones", and adds water pipes, wind machines, and a monolingual English speaker. "Stones, toilet paper, wind, and water pipes are the wrong kind of stuff to have intentionality in the first place."
2. **Form versus content.** "the program is purely formal, but the intentional states are not in that way formal. They are defined in terms of their content, not their form. The belief that it is raining … is not defined as a certain formal shape, but as a certain mental content with conditions of satisfaction, a direction of fit" — and "one and the same belief can be given an indefinite number of different syntactic expressions in different linguistic systems."
3. **Product relation.** "mental states and events are literally a product of the operation of the brain, but the program is not in that way a product of the computer."

#### The three diagnoses of the illusion

Searle asks why so many people believe the converse, and offers three causes (pp. 423–424).

- **The "information processing" ambiguity, put as a dilemma.** Either we construe "information processing" so that it implies intentionality, or we don't. "If the former, then the programmed computer does not do information processing, it only manipulates formal symbols. If the latter, then, though the computer does information processing, it is only doing so in the sense in which adding machines, typewriters, stomachs, thermostats, rainstorms, and hurricanes do information processing" — that is, observer-relatively. "And no similarity is established between the computer and the brain."
- **Residual behaviourism and operationalism.** "The Turing test is typical of the tradition in being unashamedly behavioristic and operationalistic."
- **Residual dualism.** The sharpest move in the paper: "strong AI only makes sense given the dualistic assumption that, where the mind is concerned, the brain doesn't matter. In strong AI (and in functionalism, as well) what matters are programs, and programs are independent of their realization in machines; indeed, as far as AI is concerned, the same program could be realized by an electronic machine, a Cartesian mental substance, or a Hegelian world spirit." He adds that this "is not the traditional Cartesian variety", and that "AI literature contains frequent fulminations against 'dualism'; what the authors seem to be unaware of is that their position presupposes a strong version of dualism" (p. 424).

Closing biological analogy, which sets up Part C of this corpus: "Whatever else intentionality is, it is a biological phenomenon, and it is as likely to be as causally dependent on the specific biochemistry of its origins as lactation, photosynthesis, or any other biological phenomena. No one would suppose that we could produce milk and sugar by running a computer simulation of the formal sequences in lactation and photosynthesis" (p. 424). And a concession that matters for Part A2: "Of course the brain is a digital computer. Since everything is a digital computer, brains are too."

#### The Author's Response (pp. 450–455) — what is new, and what is walked back

This is not a restatement. Three things appear here that are not in the target article, and one important clarification is issued.

**New: the intrinsic / observer-relative distinction** (pp. 451–452). Asked why people are tempted to say thermostats have beliefs, Searle distinguishes *intrinsic intentionality* — "cases of actual mental states" — from *observer-relative ascriptions*, "ways that people have of speaking about entities figuring in our activities but lacking intrinsic intentionality". His uncontroversial examples: saying "I am hungry" ascribes intrinsic intentionality; saying that the word "Carter" refers to the president does not ascribe a mental state to a word. "Observer-relative ascriptions of intentionality are always dependent on the intrinsic intentionality of the observers." He then extends this to a one-sentence verdict on the whole functionalist programme:

> "Functionalism, by the way, is an entire system erected on the failure to see this distinction. Functional attributions are always observer relative. There is no such thing as an intrinsic function, in the way that there are intrinsic intentional states." (p. 452)

**New: the internalist account of content** (p. 452). Against commentators who wanted external causal relations to supply meaning, Searle works through a visual example — photons from a tree, specialised visual-cortex neurons, a resulting conscious visual experience with intentional content. "Now I could be having exactly that visual experience even if there were no tree there. … If I were a brain in a vat I could have exactly the same mental states I have now; it is just that most of them would be false or otherwise unsatisfied." So internal causes fix the *content*; external ones fix whether it is *satisfied*.

**New: the tire analogy for his own ontology** (p. 455). "Mental states are as real as any other biological phenomena. They are both caused by and realized in the brain. That is no more mysterious than the fact that such properties as the elasticity and puncture resistance of an inflated car tire are both caused by and realized in its microstructure. … Mental states are no more epiphenomenal than are the elasticity and puncture resistance of an inflated tire." He labels this "monist interactionist" and distances himself explicitly from Eccles's dualist interactionism.

**The clarification that keeps being missed.** Twice Searle says his position is *not* a claim that only carbon works:

> "Some of the commentators seem to suppose that I take the causal powers of the brain by themselves to be an argument against strong AI. But that is a misunderstanding. It is an empirical question whether any given machine has causal powers equivalent to the brain. My argument against strong AI is that instantiating a program is not enough to guarantee that it has those causal powers." (p. 452)

> "I offer no a priori proof that a system of integrated circuit chips couldn't have intentionality. That is, as I say repeatedly, an empirical question. … On my account it is a testable empirical claim whether in repairing a damaged brain we could duplicate the electrochemical basis of intentionality using some other substance, say silicon. On the position of strong AI there cannot be any empirical questions about the electrochemical bases necessary for intentionality since any substance whatever is sufficient for intentionality if it has the right program." (p. 453)

**One reply that matters directly for Part B's later papers.** Haugeland's commentary proposed coating a Chinese speaker's neurons so they cannot fire, with "Searle's demon" stimulating them as if they had. Searle's answer (pp. 452–453):

> "Her neurons still have the right causal powers; they just need some help from the demon. More generally if the stimulation of the causes is at a low enough level to reproduce the causes and not merely describe them, the 'simulation' will reproduce the effects."

That sentence concedes a low-enough-level replacement that preserves causal powers — which is precisely the territory Cuda and Chalmers occupy. Whether the concession is large enough to grant their conclusion is exactly what the rest of this batch disputes.

**On intuitions** (pp. 450–451). Several commentators charged that the argument rests on intuition. Searle denies the framing: "When I now say that I at this moment do not understand Chinese, that claim does not merely record an intuition of mine, something I find myself inclined to say. It is a plain fact about me that I don't understand Chinese." Against Dennett's my-own-body version of the robot reply, he argues the case is underdescribed: if the program says "when somebody holds up the squiggle-squiggle sign, pass him the salt", the agent starts *learning Chinese* by attaching semantic content, and so is no longer a mere program-instantiator.

**His own summary** (p. 455): "I conclude that the Chinese room has survived the assaults of its critics. The remaining puzzle to me is this: why do so many workers in AI still want to adhere to strong AI? Surely weak AI is challenging, interesting, and difficult enough."

## 2. Cuda (1985) — Against Neural Chauvinism [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Cuda 1985 - Against neural chauvinism.pdf` — published version, *Philosophical Studies* 48(1), pp. 111–127, JSTOR scan. Received 25 June 1984.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Cuda takes one paragraph of Searle 1980 as his target and quotes it on his first page — the brain-simulator passage about simulating "the wrong things about the brain" (Searle p. 421, quoted at Cuda p. 111). His characterisation of Searle's view: "For Searle one thing that is necessary for an organism to have conscious states is that it be made of the proper material (neurons being the most obvious choice)."

He is careful to bound his own project. "It is not an argument that functionalism is true. For example, I say nothing of any dualistic criticisms of functionalism. Rather the paper is directed only at those who, like Searle, are physicalists, but not functionalists on the grounds that functionalism attributes conscious states to things that don't have them" (p. 111).

**The construction.** Imagine a pool of homunculi who know a great deal about neurophysiology, each equipped with a tiny device that can both *read* and *change* the state of a neuron. Fred agrees to a series of operations. In the first, one neuron (call it A) is removed, and a homunculus takes over its functional role: it continuously reads the states of A's neighbours B₁ … Bₙ, notes A's state at the moment of removal, and thereafter computes what state A *would* have been in and adjusts each Bᵢ to the state A's signal would have put it in. Repeat about a trillion times. At the end, "there is nothing left of the original matter of Fred's brain", the homunculi mostly just call out to each other what state their neuron would have been in, and the peripheral ones handle the input and output neurons (pp. 112–113).

Call the sequence Fred₁ (original) through Fredₙ (all homunculi). Cuda also defines a **control sequence** Fred₁* … Fredₙ*: ordinary humans who go through all the same life events, including being put on the operating table and told about the operations, but who have no neurons removed. The key relation is that "Each neuron in each Fredᵢ has a counterpart in Fredᵢ* and is in the same state as its counterpart", and he claims this "is uncontroversial and does not depend on any theory of mind" (p. 113).

**Key findings (as argued).**
1. **Anyone who says Fredₙ is unconscious is committed to a cliff-edge claim.** In its refined form (p. 119): no matter in what order the neurons are removed, there is always some *m* such that Fred*ₘ* is fully conscious and Fred*ₘ₊₁* is "completely unconscious, or lacking a great deal of Fred*ₘ*'s conscious abilities."
2. **The gradual-fading escape route is closed, in two steps.** If the Freds *notice* their consciousness fading, the noticing must be tokened somewhere — and their neurons are, by construction, in exactly the same states as their normal counterparts', who are not noticing anything (pp. 114–115). If they *don't* notice, then each Fred must be systematically mistaken about his own experience — and "If it makes sense to think that Fredₘ could be mistaken in such a way, then it makes sense to think that we could be mistaken in such a way also" (p. 117), which Cuda takes to be senseless.
3. **The remaining option cascades.** To avoid the mistaken-belief problem, the fader must lose not just (say) red qualia but every belief and desire that goes with them — and then he cannot believe or disbelieve much of what he hears or reads, cannot believe that he believes it, and "will have to lose the ability to understand much of his behavior" (p. 118). He picks the red card correctly but cannot believe it "looked red".
4. **The cliff-edge claim is counterintuitive, has evidence against it, and could never have evidence for it** (§VI). The redundancy rebuttal fails; actual clinical cases of neuron loss provide inductive evidence against it; and no possible observation could support it, since Fred*ₘ₊₁* behaves exactly like a normal human and the only remaining appeal — to the material difference — "is precisely what is at issue" (p. 124).
5. **The conclusion is deliberately modest in one direction and pointed in another.** What is established is that **circuit functional equivalence** — reproducing the functional role of each neuron — suffices for consciousness. What is *not* established is sufficiency at any coarser grain. But the paper "should help to weaken intuitions that are based on what the organisms are made of", so that anyone maintaining Block's or Searle's cases "is going to have to do this on the grounds that the functional equivalence that they share with a human is not at the relevant level, and not on the grounds that they are not made of the proper material" (p. 125).

**Initial takeaway.** This is the paper that converted the substrate debate from a clash of intuitions into an argument with a burden. Searle's cases work by inviting you to look at a strange object — water pipes, a roomful of paper — and find the ascription of a mind absurd. Cuda's move is to deny the opponent the strange object: at every step the Freds look and act like Fred, the change is one neuron, and the opponent must nominate a step at which everything goes dark. The *form* of this move — gradual replacement plus a demand to locate the discontinuity — is the form Chalmers takes over ten years later, and Chalmers cites Cuda for it.

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### Why the homunculus can do the job

The mechanism deserves restating because the whole argument depends on it being uncontroversial. After A is removed, the homunculus (p. 112):
- knows A's state at removal, call it `Sᵢ`;
- continuously reads the state of every neuron B₁ … Bₙ that was directly connected to A;
- knows enough neurophysiology to compute, for each Bᵢ in a given state, what message it would have sent to A and how that message would have changed A;
- therefore "can always keep track of what state A would have been in";
- and therefore knows whether A would have signalled any Bᵢ and how, and adjusts Bᵢ accordingly.

"This is why A is no longer necessary to the functional organization of Fred."

Two technical worries are handled in note 2 (p. 126). If the *operations* would perturb the remaining neurons, stipulate that all the Freds and all the Fred*s have their brains "frozen" during the corresponding interval and unfrozen after. If *quantum indeterminacy* would desynchronise the two sequences, note that there remains a non-zero probability of their staying synchronised, and stipulate the Fred* sequence to be one in which they do. Cuda adds that "my introduction of the Fredᵢ* sequence is only a heuristic device, that the point of the paper could be made without assuming such a sequence."

#### The argument reconstructed

- **P1 (the synchrony relation).** For every *i*, each remaining neuron in Fredᵢ is in the same state as its counterpart in Fredᵢ*. This holds because the homunculi always adjust the remaining neurons to the state they would have been in had no neurons been removed, and because the two sequences undergo the same circumstances.
- **P2 (the endpoints).** Fred₁ = Fred₁* is conscious. Searle's view entails that Fredₙ is not.
- **P3 (the only escapes from a discontinuity).** Either consciousness drops off a cliff somewhere, or it fades gradually. Fading splits into *fading-with-noticing* and *fading-without-noticing*.
- **P4 (fading-with-noticing is unavailable to Searle).** Noticing a loss is itself a conscious state; on Searle's view it must be tokened in the remaining neurons; those are in the same state as the counterparts in Fredᵢ*, who has no loss and so no such state (pp. 114–115). Three sub-escapes are closed:
  - *"The homunculi can't actually do their jobs."* "such a claim is incredibly ad hoc. It amounts to the claim that the homunculi can't do their jobs because it's not possible for anything but a neuron to change the state of a neuron, or read the state of a neuron, in the relevant ways. But nothing that we know about neurons warrants such a belief, and in fact there is strong evidence against it, since there are at present devices which are not neurons that manipulate and read the states of neurons to a certain extent" (p. 115).
  - *"The total brain state differs because homunculi are present, so extra states can be tokened."* Then "the homunculi must play some role in tokening them" — and "one would have to wonder just how many neurons are necessary to token a conscious state. Might not the homunculi be able to do so on their own, or with the help of just one or two neurons? It would be wildly ad hoc to simply assert that homunculi can play a role in tokening conscious states, but that they cannot do so on their own" (p. 115). Worse, the presence of the homunculi would have to bring about not just *any* extra state but precisely the right one, "that noticing of the loss of some conscious capacity" (p. 116).
  - *"The extra state is tokened entirely in the remaining neurons, merely because there are fewer of them"* (note 3, p. 126). Cuda: "if I were to notice that I was blind (as opposed to caused to become blind) simply because some neurons were removed, while the remaining neurons did not change state, I would not even be able to utter the sentence, 'I am blind', for there would be no change in any neurons that could trigger its utterance."
  - And a general cost: any such view entails conscious states with **no behavioural role at all**. "Fredᵢ's sight would begin to fail (or he might go blind altogether) and he would notice this, yet he would behave just like Fredᵢ* (drive a car, say he can see fine, etc.) and not be able to express his loss of vision, not even his knowledge of the loss, in any way" (p. 116).
- **P5 (fading-without-noticing cannot proceed one capacity at a time).** Take Fredₘ, mentally equivalent to Fredₘ* except that he lacks red qualia and does not notice. Then "Fredₘ would have to, at times, believe that he is having a red quale, behave just like he had them, desire to have red qualia and then believe that the desire has been fulfilled". Cuda's lever is a **parity argument**: "if it makes sense to think that Fredₘ could be mistaken in such a way, then it makes sense to think that we could be mistaken in such a way also. Hence we would have no reason to think that things made of neurons (i.e., ourselves) have red qualia. But clearly it makes no sense to think that we act like we have red qualia, believe that we have them, etc., but that we are all mistaken and really never have any red qualia" (pp. 116–117). He explicitly blocks the reply "we know we have red qualia because we are made of neurons" as missing the point. Note 4 (p. 126) credits a similar argument to Shoemaker, 'Functionalism and Qualia' (*Philosophical Studies* 27, 1975, pp. 291–315), noting that Shoemaker leaves open whether the qualia must be the *same*.

  The same runs for intentional states: a Fredₘ with no memory of his childhood who believes he remembers it, acts as if he does, and has that desire seem fulfilled (p. 117).
- **P6 (the cascade).** To avoid the mistaken-belief problem the opponent must say that losing an ability also loses all the beliefs and desires that go with it. Cuda then shows how far that spreads for a Fred*ₖ* who has lost red qualia or childhood memories (pp. 118–119):
  - Being functionally equivalent to Fred*ₖ*, he still says "I am having a red quale" and still tells childhood stories — so he "does not form beliefs about what he says concerning the part of consciousness that he has lost."
  - Nor about much of what others say or what he reads, since those may contain assertions about his childhood or his current experience.
  - Nor can he *disbelieve* them — "For if he did, he would be noticing the loss of a part of consciousness."
  - Nor can he believe that he believes them, on pain of regenerating the original problem.
  - He loses collateral states: "the belief that such and such a feeling was like one that he experienced during his childhood".
  - And he loses self-understanding. Asked to pick the red card from a pile, he does it correctly, "But he will not be able to understand how he did it. … He can say, 'I was able to pick out the red card because it looked red', but this is something he will not be able to believe … and hence it cannot serve as an explanation." Nor can he notice that he doesn't understand, since that would be noticing the loss.
- **C (the commitment).** Proposition **(2)**, p. 119: *No matter in what order we remove neurons from the Fredᵢ sequence, there will always be some m, such that Fredₘ is mentally equivalent to Fredₘ*, and hence fully conscious, and such that Fredₘ₊₁ is completely unconscious, or lacking a great deal of Fredₘ's conscious abilities.*

#### Why (2) should not be believed: three properties

**(i) It is counterintuitive, and redundancy does not save it.** The obvious rebuttal is that brains have redundant circuitry, so many neurons could go with no effect until the backups run out and the last one matters hugely. Cuda's counter (pp. 121–122): consider Fred*ⱼ*, who has had *all and only* the redundancy destroyed, so his conscious life is still completely normal. Then (2) entails that removing **any** remaining neuron causes total or near-total loss, since some loss must occur and, by (2), any loss must be huge. He finds it hard to believe "that every single neuron left in Fredⱼ would be so vitally important" — surely some neuron is vital only to vision, so that its removal blinds him while leaving him able to taste, hear, reason, and notice that he is blind.

And Fred*ⱼ* is arbitrary: there may be many proper subsets of Fred's original neurons constituting a person with all and only the redundancy gone. So (2) "denies that there is any proper subset of the neurons of Fred₁ which constitute a person who is slightly impaired (e.g. blind, or unable to understand Gödel's theorem, but otherwise normal) but asserts that some subsets of the neurons of Fred₁ constitute organisms which are so horribly impaired that they would not even qualify as persons, and that other subsets of those neurons constitute persons that are completely normal" (pp. 122–123).

**(ii) There is inductive evidence against it.** From actual neuron loss — "the removal of some tumors, or the destruction of some neurons by a mild stroke" — "In many cases the loss of conscious abilities is slight, and may even be hard to detect by anyone but an expert"; or consider someone blinded by brain damage but otherwise normal (p. 123). Since (2) denies that *any* removal order yields a slightly impaired Fred, such cases tell against it.

Cuda handles the disanalogies deliberately, and they cut his way: in the Fredᵢ sequence homunculi *preserve* functional organisation and neurons go one at a time, whereas real cases destroy organisation and remove many at once — so "the addition of homunculi and the fact that neurons are removed one at a time, should make us feel even stronger that the Fredᵢ sequence would not lose huge chunks of consciousness at once" (p. 123). He also insists the test is **symmetric**, which is what makes it evidence rather than rhetoric: "if we found that all ways of removing neurons made it the case that patients only lost conscious abilities if they lost all of them, or a great deal of them, at once, then we would be justified in believing (2). Therefore actual cases serve as a real test" (p. 124).

He then blocks the mirror-image inference. Do real cases support the claim that the Fredᵢ sequence *fades*? No — because the inference would require the very premise at issue: "with the Fredᵢ sequence homunculi replace the removed neurons, so functional organization is preserved, whereas in actual cases of neuron removal functional organization is not preserved. … To claim that actual cases of neuron removal are a basis from which to infer that the Fredᵢ sequence's consciousness fades, would be to do nothing more than beg the question. For one would have to already know that what is necessary for consciousness is the having of neurons" (p. 124).

**(iii) There could never be evidence for it.** Stand Fred*ₘ* in front of us and make the one-neuron change (p. 124). Behaviour cannot be evidence, since Fred*ₘ₊₁* behaves exactly like a normal human. The material difference cannot be evidence, since "whether or not a change in material that preserves functional organization makes a difference to an organism's conscious states, is precisely what is at issue." Intuition cannot be evidence over so slight a difference:

> "Would we glance within the skull of Fredₘ₊₁, notice that there is one less neuron than in Fredₘ and exclaim, 'Aha! it's obvious that Fredₘ₊₁ is a mindless, or near mindless thing?'" (p. 124)

He adds that softening (2) to "lacking a great deal" rather than "unconscious" does not help: "Either way, the change from Fredₘ to Fredₘ₊₁ would be too great."

Note 5 (pp. 126–127) answers the sceptical escape — that real patients might be unconscious too, and merely functionally similar to us — by pointing out its price: anyone taking it "would be embracing a very radical scepticism in order to maintain that Fredₙ is unconscious and would simply be reducing potential problems with functionalism, to the problem of other minds."

#### The scope of the conclusion

Cuda states what he has and has not shown (pp. 124–125). Established: **circuit functional equivalence** — "reproducing the functional role of each neuron in a normal human brain" — is sufficient for conscious states. Not established: sufficiency at any coarser level, which he defines precisely as "any level of description X, such that circuit functional equivalence entails equivalence at the X level, but equivalence at the X level does not entail circuit functional equivalence."

What he claims to have shifted is the *kind* of reason an opponent may give. He distinguishes two grounds for denying consciousness to Block's or Searle's creatures: that the functional equivalence is at the wrong level, or "and I believe the dominant reason … that one feels at first glance, that they are just not made of the right kind of stuff (e.g., they are made of homunculi)." The paper targets the second: "what is important is not what an organism is made of, but rather functional organization at some level."

Note 1 (p. 126) is worth recording because it is a careful, and sceptical, reading of Searle. Cuda quotes Searle's "only very special kinds of machines, namely brains and machines that had the same causal power as brains" and the lactation/photosynthesis line, and comments: "I am not sure, but I would imagine that Searle is claiming here that organisms made of things other than neurons might be able to have consciousness, so long as their components have the right causal powers. Unfortunately, he does not tell us what the components must have in common with neurons in order to have the right causal powers, except that it has something to do with biochemistry." His stated minimum: "all that is important for this paper, is that Searle does not consider functional organization alone to be sufficient for having conscious states, and that he would not consider things made of homunculi as candidates for consciousness, regardless of their functional organization."

It establishes sufficiency only at "circuit functional equivalence", the finest grain, and Cuda says explicitly that it does not establish sufficiency at any coarser level; what it claims to do more broadly is disqualify *material* as a ground of objection, leaving only *level*.
- Responds to / builds on: Searle 1980 (the named target, quoted at p. 111 and in note 1); Block 1978 (cited as giving similar coarse-grained counterexamples); Shoemaker 1975, 'Functionalism and Qualia' (note 4, for the argument that a system with all a normal human's intentional states cannot lack qualia)
- Key move: the **gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement by homunculi** plus the **matched control sequence** Fredᵢ*, which converts "is this thing conscious?" into "at which single neuron does it stop being conscious?"; and the **parity argument** that if a functional duplicate could be systematically wrong about its own qualia, so could we
- Evidence type: thought experiment, within conceptual analysis; with an explicit **inductive** appeal to clinical cases of neuron loss (tumour removal, mild stroke, cortical blindness), offered as a genuine two-sided test
- Testability: partly, and unusually so for a paper of this kind. Cuda stakes proposition (2) on an empirical pattern and states the falsifier in both directions: if clinical neuron loss always produced all-or-nothing changes in conscious ability, "we would be justified in believing (2)"; since it often produces slight, hard-to-detect losses, the evidence runs the other way (pp. 123–124). He also notes that devices which are not neurons already "manipulate and read the states of neurons to a certain extent" (p. 115), making the homunculus mechanism a matter of degree rather than principle.

## 3. Chalmers (1995) — Absent Qualia, Fading Qualia, Dancing Qualia [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Chalmers 1995 - Absent qualia fading qualia dancing qualia (author web version).pdf` — **author web version**, not a typeset publisher file. Its own header reads "[[Published in *Conscious Experience*, edited by Thomas Metzinger. Imprint Academic, 1995.]]"; the corpus manifest instead notes "JCS 1995". No journal pagination, so citations below are by **section number**. Author affiliation on the file: Department of Philosophy, University of Arizona.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Chalmers separates two questions about how experience depends on the physical (§1). The first is about the **strength** of the psychophysical laws: are they metaphysically necessary, or merely contingent like the law of gravity? That question drives the physicalism / property-dualism debate. The second is about the **shape** of the laws: which physical properties enter their antecedents, so that which kinds of physical system can be conscious? This paper addresses only the second.

His answer is the **principle of organizational invariance**: "given any system that has conscious experiences, then any system that has the same functional organization at a fine enough grain will have qualitatively identical conscious experiences."

He defines the terms precisely. A functional organization is specified by three things: a number of abstract components; for each, a number of possible states; and a system of dependency relations fixing how each component's state depends on the previous states of all components and on inputs, and how outputs depend on previous component states. Crucially, "Beyond specifying their number and their dependency relations, the nature of the components and the states is left unspecified." "Fine enough grain" means fine enough to determine the system's behavioural dispositions — for the brain, "it is likely that the neural level suffices". Two systems sharing organisation at that grain *and* in corresponding states at the time in question are **functional isomorphs**; the corresponding-states clause is needed because otherwise "my sleeping twin might count as sharing my organization, but he certainly does not share my experiences."

**The dialectical situation.** §2 states the two standard objections at full strength: **absent qualia** (Block's Chinese population; Searle's "sequence of water-pipes, or a set of wind-machines") and **inverted qualia** (Shoemaker 1982, Horgan 1984). Then Chalmers narrows the target in a way that shapes everything that follows. He concedes the logical and metaphysical possibility of both scenarios — "It seems difficult to deny that the absent qualia and inverted qualia scenarios are at least intelligible" — and says he is concerned only with their **empirical (natural, nomological) possibility**, because "Mere intelligibility does not bear on this, any more than the intelligibility of a world without relativity can falsify Einstein's theory."

He is also even-handed about the intuitive stand-off: yes, many find it counterintuitive that the population of China could be conscious, but "it seems equally counterintuitive that a mass of 10¹¹ appropriately organized neurons should give rise to consciousness, and yet it happens. Intuition is unreliable as a guide to empirical possibility." His stated method is not proof but plausibility-shifting: "I use these thought-experiments as a plausibility argument for the principle of organizational invariance, by showing that the alternatives have implausible consequences. If an opponent wishes to hold on to the possibility of absent or inverted qualia she can still do so, but the thought-experiments show that the cost is higher than one might have expected." The analogy he offers for the method is Schrödinger's cat: not decisive, but something "every interpretation must ultimately come to grips with."

**Key findings (as argued).**
1. **Fading Qualia (§3).** Assume absent qualia are empirically possible; then a silicon isomorph, Robot, has none. Build a series from you to Robot by replacing neurons with chips one (then two, then more) at a time. Either consciousness blinks out abruptly — **Suddenly Disappearing Qualia**, which "would require brute discontinuities in fundamental laws" and an arbitrary cut point — or it fades. Fading gives you **Joe**, halfway along, who "exclaims about the vivid bright red and yellow uniforms of the basketball players" while in fact experiencing "faded pink", who complains about the noise while hearing "a distant rumble". "In short, Joe is utterly out of touch with his conscious experience, and is incapable of getting in touch."
2. **Searle's own version of the scenario is answered directly.** Chalmers quotes *The Rediscovery of the Mind* (pp. 66–67), where Searle imagines you going blind while your voice, out of your control, says "I see a red object in front of me". Chalmers's reply: "There is simply no room in the system for any new beliefs to be formed" — the neurons are identical to a subset of the original ones, and "Searle is surely not suggesting that the silicon replacement is itself supporting the new beliefs!"
3. **Dancing Qualia (§4).** The stronger argument, and the one aimed at *inverted* qualia, which Fading does not touch. Assume inverted qualia are possible. Over the replacement series there must be two systems differing by at most a tenth of their makeup whose experiences differ noticeably — "there is no way to get from red to blue by ten non-noticeable jumps." Take the differing silicon circuit and install it in your own head as a **backup**, with a switch. Flipping the switch changes nothing about your functional organisation (the disconnected circuit "plays no role in affecting other components of the system"), yet by hypothesis it changes your experience from red to blue. Flip it repeatedly and "the red and blue experiences 'dance' before my eyes" — while "I do not notice any change. … There is no room for a sudden start, for an exclamation, or even for a distraction of attention."
4. **A self-undermining corollary.** If qualia depend on implementational details rather than organisation, then since the low-level physical properties of our own mechanisms change constantly while functional role is preserved, "it may well be that our qualia are in fact dancing before our eyes all the time." The only defence against that sceptical conclusion is the very principle — that a significant change in experience is noticeable — which the opponent has just abandoned.
5. **The conclusion is explicitly modest (§5).** The arguments establish only that absent and inverted qualia are *empirically* impossible, not logically or metaphysically impossible, for two stated reasons. The resulting view is **nonreductive functionalism**: "conscious experience is determined by functional organization without necessarily being reducible to functional organization" — a position "just as compatible with certain forms of property dualism about experience as with certain forms of physicalism."

**Initial takeaway.** Chalmers's contribution is to identify the specific price of denying substrate independence: it is not absurdity, it is a **dissociation between consciousness and cognition**. If organisation does not fix experience, then there can be perfectly rational systems, with no cognitive pathology, that are systematically and irremediably wrong about what they are experiencing. That is the cost he asks the opponent to pay, and he grants that they can pay it. The payoff line for the rest of the corpus: "systems that duplicate our functional organization will be conscious even if they are made of silicon, constructed out of water-pipes, or instantiated in an entire population. The arguments in this paper can thus be seen as offering support to some of the ambitions of artificial intelligence" (§5).

### Phase 2: Graduate-Level Deep Dive

#### The Fading Qualia argument reconstructed

- **P1 (reductio assumption).** Absent qualia are empirically possible. Without loss of generality, let the isomorph be silicon: **Robot** has my functional organisation but "there is nothing it is like to be Robot".
- **P2 (the series exists).** Construct intermediate cases. First one neuron is replaced by a chip "equipped with tiny transducers that take in electrical signals and chemical ions and transforms these into a digital signal upon which the chip computes". Two neighbouring replacements let you drop the transducers between them and use a direct digital connection. Continue until nothing biochemical remains. Throughout, the system is connected to a body via transducers and effectors, and "Each system in the sequence will be functionally isomorphic to me at a fine enough grain to share my behavioral dispositions." Chalmers explicitly brackets glial cells: if they matter, they are components of the organisation and are replaced too.
- **P3 (the dichotomy).** Either **Suddenly Disappearing Qualia** or **Fading Qualia**.
- **P4 (Suddenly Disappearing Qualia is very implausible).** "the replacement of a single neuron could be responsible for the vanishing of an entire field of conscious experience. If so, we could switch back and forth between a neuron and its silicon replacement, with a field of experience blinking in and out of existence on demand." Three costs: brute discontinuities "unlike those we find anywhere else"; arbitrariness of the cut ("50 percent neural? 25 percent?"); and the experiment can be re-run at finer grain, "so that ultimately the replacement of a few molecules produces a sudden disappearance of experience."

  A footnote pre-empts the obvious rebuttal from nonlinear dynamics: sensitive dependence there is still *continuous*, "so there will be intermediate cases in which the dependent magnitude takes on intermediate values; the analogy therefore leads to Fading Qualia". And: "In all fundamental laws known to date, the dependence of one magnitude on another is continuous in this fashion, and there is no way to compound continuity into discontinuity."
- **P5 (Fading Qualia is also very implausible).** Consider **Joe**, halfway along. By construction he is functionally isomorphic to me and "says all the same things about his experiences as I do about mine". Chalmers fixes the imagery: I am at a basketball game with bright red and yellow uniforms, junk-food smells and a throbbing headache; Joe "experiences faded pink where I see bright red, with many distinctions between shades of my experience no longer present"; where I have loud noise, "perhaps Joe is experiencing only a distant rumble"; and "Not everything is so bad for Joe: where I have a throbbing headache, he only has the mildest twinge."

  Two structural constraints are derived rather than assumed: experiences must stop being bright *before* they vanish, and fine distinctions must drop out *before* the rest — otherwise we are back to a version of Suddenly Disappearing Qualia.

  The cost: "This is a being whose rational processes are functioning and who is in fact conscious, but who is completely wrong about his own conscious experiences. … For a sentient, rational being that is suffering from no functional pathology to be so systematically out of touch with its experiences would imply a strong dissociation between consciousness and cognition."
- **P6 (real fading is disanalogous).** Falling asleep; moving back the evolutionary chain "from people to trilobites"; a dog's colour vision. In all of these "the fading is accompanied by a corresponding change in functioning. … Joe's mechanisms can still discriminate subtly different wavelengths of light, and he certainly judges that such discriminations are reflected in his experience, but we are to believe that his experience does not reflect these discriminations at all."
- **C.** "the possibility of Fading Qualia requires either a bizarre relationship between belief contents and physical states, or the possibility of beings that are massively mistaken about their own conscious experiences despite being fully rational. Both of these hypotheses are significantly less plausible than the hypothesis that rational conscious beings are generally correct in their judgments about their experiences." Therefore absent qualia are very unlikely to be empirically possible.

**The reply to Searle, in detail.** Searle's *Rediscovery of the Mind* passage is quoted in full: you find your conscious field shrinking while "you hear your voice saying in a way that is completely out of your control, 'I see a red object in front of me.'" Chalmers reads this as Searle accepting Fading Qualia but denying that the system is *mistaken* — it has true beliefs that are simply impotent to affect behaviour. His counter is that the beliefs have nowhere to live: "Unless one is a dualist of a very strong variety, beliefs must be reflected in the functioning of a system — perhaps not in behavior, but at least in some process. But this system is identical to the original system (me) at a fine grain. There is no room for new beliefs like 'I can't see anything', new desires like the desire to cry out, and other new cognitive states such as amazement." And the scale of the required change matters: "While it might just seem plausible that an organization-preserving change from neurons to silicon might twist a few experiences from red to blue, a change in beliefs from 'Nice basketball game' to 'I seem to be stuck in a bad horror movie!' is of a different order of magnitude. If such a major change in cognitive contents were not mirrored in a change in functional organization, cognition would float free of internal functioning like a disembodied mind."

A footnote sets aside Searle's alternative suggestion that the subject might be reduced to paralysis: "Such a scenario is irrelevant to the truth of the invariance principle, however, which applies only to systems with the appropriate functional organization."

**The extension to Block's and Searle's own scenarios.** The argument generalises "to other sorts of functional isomorphs, including those that differ in shape, size, and physical makeup", and, importantly, to the population case: "we simply need to imagine neurons replaced one-by-one with tiny homunculi, ending up with a network of homunculi that is essentially equivalent to the population controlling a robot. (If one objects to tiny homunculi, they can be external and of normal size, as long as they are equipped with appropriate radio connections to the body when necessary.)" And then to the single-room case: "One can also imagine going from a multiple-homunculi case to a single-homunculus case, yielding something like Searle's 'Chinese room' example. We need only suppose that the homunculi gradually 'double up' on their tasks, leaving written records of the state of each component, until only a single homunculus does all the work." With a caveat that answers Searle's internalisation move on its own terms: "Of course, we should not expect the homunculus itself to have the experiences; it is merely acting as a sort of causal facilitator."

**Four objections, answered.**

| Objection | Chalmers's answer |
|---|---|
| Neural replacement is technologically impossible | Possibly, but not nomologically impossible — "We already have prosthetic arms and legs. Prosthetic eyes lie within the foreseeable future". And a technical obstacle would have no bearing on the principle: "There will surely be some systems between which gradual replacement is possible; will the objector hold that the invariance principle holds for those systems, but no other? If so, the situation seems quite arbitrary". |
| Silicon could not even duplicate a neuron's local function (perhaps neural function is uncomputable) | "There is little evidence for this, but it should be noted that even if it is true, it does not affect the argument for the invariance principle. If silicon really could not even duplicate the function of a neural system, then a functional isomorph made of silicon would be impossible, and the assessment of silicon systems would simply be irrelevant to the invariance principle." |
| There are real cases of people massively mistaken about their experiences — blindness denial | "In systems whose belief-formation mechanisms are impaired, anything goes. Such systems might believe that they are Napoleon, or that the moon is pink." Joe by contrast "is an eminently thoughtful, reasonable person, who exhibits none of the confabulatory symptoms of those with blindness denial." The refined claim: "no *rational* system whose cognitive mechanisms are unimpaired can be so mistaken." |
| This is a Sorites (slippery-slope) argument, and those are suspect | "Sorites arguments gain their force by ignoring the fact that some apparent dichotomy is in fact a continuum; there are all sorts of vague cases between heaps and non-heaps, for instance. The Fading Qualia argument, by contrast, explicitly accepts the possibility of a continuum, but argues that intermediate cases are impossible for independent reasons. The argument is therefore not a Sorites argument." |

Chalmers states the opponent's remaining option fairly: "the only tenable way for an opponent of organizational invariance to respond to this argument is to bite the bullet and accept the possibility of Fading Qualia … or perhaps to bite another bullet and accept Suddenly Disappearing Qualia and the associated brute discontinuities."

#### The Dancing Qualia argument reconstructed

Chalmers first explains why Fading cannot be recycled against *inverted* qualia. Intermediate cases of mild inversion are unproblematic: "These systems will not be systematically wrong about their experiences. Where they claim to experience distinctions, they may really be experiencing distinctions … The difference between these cases and the Fading Qualia cases is that these cases preserve the structure of experience throughout."

- **P1 (reductio assumption).** Inverted qualia are empirically possible: two functional isomorphs — me (red) and my silicon isomorph (blue).
- **P2 (a small-difference pair must exist).** Along the replacement series, consider the 10%, 20%, … 90% points. "Red and blue are sufficiently different experiences that some neighboring pairs here must be significantly different … there is no way to get from red to blue by ten non-noticeable jumps." Call the pair **me** and **Bill**. "This substitution of a silicon circuit for a neural circuit is the only physical difference between me and Bill."
- **P3 (the backup-circuit construction).** Take a silicon circuit just like Bill's and install it in my head as a backup, functionally isomorphic to a circuit already present, with transducers and effectors but not directly hooked up. Install a **switch**. "Upon flipping the switch, the neural circuit becomes irrelevant and the silicon circuit takes over. … When it is switched, the connections from the neural circuit are pushed out of the way, and the silicon circuit's effectors are attached." Chalmers notes a variant (disconnect both transducers and effectors to isolate the backup fully) and says "the moral would be the same".
- **P4 (organisation is unchanged).** "my functional organization is exactly the same as it would have been if we had not flipped the switch. The only relevant difference between the two cases is the physical makeup of one circuit within the system. There is also a difference in the physical makeup of another 'dangling' circuit, but this is irrelevant to functional organization, as it plays no role."
- **P5 (but experience must change, by hypothesis).** Before installation I experience red; after installation but before flipping, still red, since the circuit is not involved in processing at all — "for all the relevance it has to my processing, I might as well have eaten it." After flipping, "I am more or less the same system as Bill", so I experience blue. Flip repeatedly and the qualia dance.
- **P6 (and I cannot notice).** "My functional organization remains normal throughout. In particular, my functional organization after flipping the switch evolves just as it would have if the switch had not been flipped. There is no special difference in my behavioral dispositions. I am not suddenly disposed to say 'Hmm! Something strange is going on!'" Even rejecting a functional account of judgment does not help: "it is extremely implausible that a simple organization-preserving replacement of a neural circuit by a silicon circuit could be responsible for the addition of significant new judgments such as 'My qualia just flipped'."
- **C.** "It seems entirely implausible to suppose that my experiences could change in such a significant way, even with me paying full attention, without my being able to notice the change. It would suggest once again an extreme dissociation between consciousness and cognition. If this kind of thing could happen, then psychology and phenomenology would be radically out of step, much further out of step than even the Fading Qualia scenario would imply."

**The self-undermining corollary.** "if we are to suppose that Dancing Qualia are empirically possible, we are led to a worrying thought: they might be actual, and happening to us all the time. The physiological properties of our functional mechanisms are constantly changing. The functional properties of the mechanisms are reasonably robust; one would expect that this robustness would be ensured by evolution. But there is no adaptive reason for the non-functional properties to stay constant. From moment to moment there will certainly be changes in low-level molecular properties." Hence: "If we allow that qualia are dependent not just on functional organization but on implementational details, it may well be that our qualia are in fact dancing before our eyes all the time. There seems to be no principled reason why a change from neurons to silicon should make a difference while a change in neural realization should not; the only place to draw a principled line is at the functional level."

He also notes that the experiment is in principle performable — "one could install such a circuit in me and I could see what happened, and report it to the world" — and then, with evident dryness, that there is no point: "we know what the result will be. I will report that my experience stayed the same throughout, a constant shade of red, and that I noticed nothing untoward."

**Four loopholes, conceded and priced.** This is the most careful part of the paper and the synthesis should not omit it.

| Loophole | What it leaves open | Why Chalmers thinks it is unattractive |
|---|---|---|
| **Speed** | A much faster or slower isomorph cannot have a circuit swapped in, "so at most we have left open the possibility that a change in speed might invert qualia." | "it would seem quite arbitrary that a change in speed would invert qualia when nothing else could." |
| **History** | "History cannot be varied in a Dancing Qualia scenario (although it can be varied in a Fading Qualia scenario), so the argument does not bear on the hypothesis that qualia supervene on the past" — e.g. a physical twin born in the Southern rather than Northern Hemisphere. | It would require "a 'nonlocal' effect of distal history on present qualia, unmediated by anything in physical structure or nearby in space and time." |
| **Very mild inversion** | Nine intermediate shades between dark red and a slightly darker red, each pair indistinguishable, would slip through — "if the system notices no difference on flipping the switch, that is just what we would expect." The one-tenth figure cannot be raised too far: at one half, "it might reasonably be suggested that upon flipping the switch, we are creating a new person"; perhaps 20–25% is safe. | Mitigated empirically: "it is very unlikely that experience depends equally on all areas of the brain. If color experience depends largely on a small area of the visual cortex, say, then we could perform any qualia inversion in one fell swoop while only replacing a small portion of the system." And in any case "any differences between isomorphs would be so slight as to be uninteresting", and a world in which only a small inversion is possible "would seem quite arbitrary." |
| **Unattended qualia** | An inversion on the fringe of the visual field might pass unnoticed. | "to exploit this loophole would leave one in the unattractive position that qualia are organizationally invariant when they are central enough in one's attention, but dependent on other features when they are not. (Presumably an inverted green experience on the fringe will flip back to red when one attends to it?) Such an asymmetric position would be theoretically unsatisfying in the extreme." |

Finally, Dancing works against absent qualia as well: "If absent qualia are possible, then on the path to absent qualia we can find two slightly different systems whose experience differs significantly, and we can install a backup circuit in the same way … the hypothesis implies that switching will cause my qualia to dance before my eyes, from vivid to tepid and back, without my ever noticing any change." Chalmers rates it the stronger of the two arguments: "Where an opponent might bite the bullet and accept the possibility of Fading Qualia, Dancing Qualia are an order of magnitude more difficult to accept. The very immediacy of the switch makes a significant difference, as does the fact that the subject cannot notice something so striking and dynamic."

#### The two reasons the conclusion cannot be strengthened (§5)

The summary chain: if absent qualia are possible then Fading Qualia are possible; if inverted qualia are possible then Dancing Qualia are possible; if absent qualia are possible then Dancing Qualia are possible. Fading is implausible; Dancing is extremely implausible. So absent and inverted qualia are extremely implausible, and organizational invariance is very likely true.

But not logically or metaphysically necessary, for two reasons Chalmers states carefully:

1. **Both scenarios remain intelligible.** "Some might dispute their logical possibility, perhaps holding that it is constitutive of qualia that subjects can notice differences between them. This conceptual intuition would be controversial."
2. **The arguments carry an empirical premise.** "the arguments take as an empirical premise certain facts about the distribution of functional organization in physical systems: that I have conscious experiences of a certain kind, or that some biological systems do. If we established the logical impossibility of Fading and Dancing Qualia, this might establish the logical necessity of the conditional: if one system with fine-grained functional organization F has a certain sort of conscious experiences, then any system with organization F has those experiences. But we cannot establish the logical necessity of the conclusion without establishing the logical necessity of the premise, and the premise is itself empirical." Attempting to secure that via analytic functionalism would be circular in this context.

Hence **nonreductive functionalism**: "functional organization suffices for conscious experience with natural necessity", holding "that conscious experience is determined by functional organization without necessarily being reducible to functional organization. As things stand, the view is just as compatible with certain forms of property dualism about experience as with certain forms of physicalism."

And a closing statement of what is *not* delivered: "The principle of organizational invariance is not the last word in constructing a theory of conscious experience. There are many unanswered questions: we would like to know just what sort of organization gives rise to experience, and what sort of experience a given organization gives rise to. Further, the principle is not cast at the right level to be a truly fundamental theory of consciousness."

Of course the practical difficulties would be immense, but at least in principle, one could install such a circuit in me and I could see what happened, and report it to the world. But of course there is no point performing the experiment: we know what the result will be." More usefully for a research programme, the paper makes a concrete prediction about prosthetic neural components — that organization-preserving replacement preserves experience — and makes the empirical claim that no unimpaired rational system is massively mistaken about its own experience, which he contrasts with the impaired case of blindness denial.

## 4. Bostrom (2003) — Are We Living in a Computer Simulation? [tier: short]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Bostrom 2003 - Are we living in a computer simulation.pdf` — published version, *The Philosophical Quarterly* 53(211), pp. 243–255, Oxford Academic download. See [Reading conventions](#reading-conventions) for the numeric-extraction caveat: the PDF's text layer dropped superscripts, so every power of ten below has been reconstructed from context and from the paper's own footnote 9.

**Claim.** At least one of three propositions is true: (1) the human species is very likely to go extinct before reaching a "posthuman" stage; (2) any posthuman civilisation is extremely unlikely to run a significant number of simulations of its evolutionary history; (3) we are almost certainly living in a computer simulation. The paper is included in this corpus not for that conclusion but for §II, where **substrate-independence is imported as a premise and explicitly not defended**.

**Method / type.** Philosophical argument with a short probability derivation, preceded by an order-of-magnitude feasibility estimate. No empirical study.

**Key result or argument — the part this corpus needs (§II, pp. 244–245).** Bostrom states the thesis plainly:

> "A common assumption in the philosophy of mind is that of substrate-independence. The idea is that mental states can supervene on any of a broad class of physical substrates. Provided a system implements the right sort of computational structures and processes, it can be associated with conscious experiences. It is not an essential property of consciousness that it is implemented on carbon-based biological neural networks inside a cranium: silicon-based processors in a computer could in principle do the trick too."

And then declines to argue for it: "Arguments for this thesis have been given in the literature, and although it is not entirely uncontroversial, I shall here take it as given."

What makes the section worth a full entry is the three **weakenings** he applies, which together give the minimal version of the thesis anyone needs:
- *Not necessary.* "I need not assume that the thesis of substrate-independence is necessarily true (either analytically or metaphysically) — merely that a computer running a suitable program would in fact be conscious." This is exactly Chalmers's *empirical* rather than logical claim (see §3 above).
- *Not behavioural.* "I need not assume that in order to create a mind on a computer it would be necessary to program it in such a way that it behaves like a human in all situations, including passing the Turing test."
- *Structural replication at a stated grain.* "I need only the weaker assumption that it would suffice for the generation of subjective experiences that the computational processes of a human brain are structurally replicated in suitably fine-grained detail, such as on the level of individual synapses. This attenuated version of substrate-independence is quite widely accepted."

He also forestalls the obvious chemical objection: "The substrate-independence thesis is not that the effects of these chemicals are small or irrelevant, but rather that they affect subjective experience only via their direct or indirect influence on computational activities. For example, if there can be no difference in subjective experience without there also being a difference in synaptic discharges, then the requisite detail of simulation is at the synaptic level (or higher)."

**The rest of the paper, in brief.** §III argues that the computing power exists: a sugar-cube-sized nanotech system at roughly 10²¹ instructions per second (Drexler), a planetary-mass computer at roughly 10⁴² operations per second, against a human brain estimated at roughly 10¹⁴ operations per second (Moravec, scaled from retinal contrast enhancement) or 10¹⁶–10¹⁷ (synapse count times firing rate), and a whole "ancestor-simulation" of human history at roughly 10³³–10³⁶ operations. Environment costs are kept down by compressing distant astronomy, filling in microscopic detail only when observed, and — if an anomaly is noticed — editing the observer's brain state or rerunning a few seconds. §IV gives the derivation: with `f_p` the fraction of civilisations reaching posthumanity, `N` the average number of ancestor-simulations run, and `H` the average pre-posthuman population,
$$f_{\text{sim}} = \frac{f_p N H}{(f_p N H) + H}$$
and writing `N = f_I N_I` for the interested fraction and their average output,
$$f_{\text{sim}} = \frac{f_p f_I N_I}{(f_p f_I N_I) + 1}$$
from which, given that `N_I` is enormous, at least one of `f_p ≈ 0`, `f_I ≈ 0`, `f_sim ≈ 1` must hold. §V defends the step from `f_sim = x` to a credence of `x` via a "bland indifference principle", distinguished from the Doomsday argument's much stronger self-sampling premise and illustrated with a junk-DNA analogy. §VI interprets the three disjuncts, including stacked virtual machines, a "naturalistic theogony", and — relevant here — "me-simulations" populated by "shadow-people", on which Bostrom remarks: "It is not even obvious that it is possible for an entity to behave indistinguishably from a real human and yet lack conscious experience."

**Place in the field's history.** Two things make this entry useful to a history of computational functionalism.

First, it is a clean instance of substrate independence functioning as a **load-bearing premise outside philosophy of mind**. By 2003 the thesis is stable enough to be cited, weakened to its minimum, and used as a foundation for an argument in an entirely different area — while its author openly notes it "is not entirely uncontroversial". That is a marker of how the idea had settled.

Second, Bostrom's weakened formulation is a useful benchmark for the synthesis. It asks for less than Chalmers's organizational invariance in one respect (it needs only that *some* suitable program would in fact be conscious, not that *every* isomorph is) and fixes a concrete grain ("the level of individual synapses") that Chalmers leaves open ("fine enough to determine behavioural dispositions"; "it is likely that the neural level suffices"). Anyone trying to state "what computational functionalism claims" for a general audience will find Bostrom's three weakenings the most compact statement in the corpus.

**Relevance.** Supplies the minimal statement of substrate independence; supplies an order-of-magnitude framing of what "simulating a brain" would cost, which recurs in the AI-consciousness literature of Part E; and supplies, in its "shadow-people" passage, an incidental acknowledgement that the philosophical-zombie question is unsettled even for someone assuming substrate independence.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2003 · philosophy-of-mind (published in a general philosophy journal; the argument itself is closer to ethics-and-policy / futures analysis, but the premise reviewed here is philosophy of mind)
- Question it asked: If future civilisations could run vast numbers of detailed simulations of their ancestors, what follows about whether we are in one?
- Position in one line: At least one of three propositions holds — almost all civilisations die before technological maturity, or almost none of the mature ones run ancestor-simulations, or we are almost certainly in a simulation — and the argument requires only a weak, explicitly non-necessary version of substrate independence at roughly the synaptic level.
- Stance on computational functionalism: supports — but as an **imported assumption, not a conclusion**. Bostrom asserts substrate independence, notes it is not uncontroversial, declines to defend it, and states the weakest sufficient version: that a computer replicating a brain's computational processes "in suitably fine-grained detail, such as on the level of individual synapses" would in fact be conscious.
- Responds to / builds on: the substrate-independence literature generally, cited without specific attribution ("Arguments for this thesis have been given in the literature"); Drexler (*Engines of Creation*, *Nanosystems*), Moravec (*Mind Children*, *Robot*), Kurzweil, Bradbury and Lloyd for the computation estimates; Bremermann and Bekenstein for physical bounds; Bostrom's own *Anthropic Bias* and 'The Doomsday Argument, Adam and Eve, UN++, and Quantum Joe' for the indifference principle; Leslie for the Doomsday argument he distinguishes himself from
- Key move: the **tripartite disjunction** derived from `f_sim = f_p f_I N_I / (f_p f_I N_I + 1)`, together with the "bland indifference principle"; for this corpus, the key move is the **explicit weakening** of substrate independence to a contingent, synapse-level, non-behavioural claim
- Evidence type: conceptual analysis with a probability derivation, supported by order-of-magnitude feasibility estimates drawn from other authors
- Testability: indirectly, and Bostrom says so. Creating our own ancestor-simulations would be "strong evidence against (1) and (2)", forcing the conclusion that we are simulated. He also notes that "If we learn more about posthuman motives and resource-constraints … then the hypothesis that we are simulated will come to have a much richer set of empirical implications." The substrate-independence premise itself is not tested here; it is assumed.
- PDF version: published

## Cross-paper notes for the synthesis

These are observations about how the four papers relate. They are not a thematic regrouping, which belongs to `literature-curator`.

1. **The Searle of the textbooks is not the Searle of the text.** Three claims that are routinely attributed to this paper are explicitly denied in it or in the Author's Response:
   - *"Searle says machines can't think."* He writes: "only a machine could think, and indeed only very special kinds of machines, namely brains and machines that had the same causal powers as brains" (p. 424).
   - *"Searle rules out silicon."* He writes: "I offer no a priori proof that a system of integrated circuit chips couldn't have intentionality. That is, as I say repeatedly, an empirical question" (p. 453).
   - *"Searle objects to AI."* He writes: "I am all in favor of weak AI, at least as a research program" (p. 453), and calls a working Chinese story-understander "a 'great achievement' requiring a 'great understanding of the nature of language'."

   The synthesis should quote these when characterising the anti-computationalist position, or it will state it at less than full strength.

2. **Cuda and Chalmers run the same argument form, and Chalmers cites Cuda.** Both replace the brain gradually while preserving each unit's functional role, then demand that the opponent locate the discontinuity. Chalmers's footnote in §3 names Cuda 1985 alongside Pylyshyn 1980, Savitt 1980 and Searle 1992 as prior neural-replacement scenarios. Four differences are worth recording:

   | | Cuda 1985 | Chalmers 1995 |
   |---|---|---|
   | Replacement units | homunculi with neuron read/write devices | silicon chips with transducers |
   | Comparison device | a **matched control sequence** of unoperated humans (Fredᵢ*), so "same state as its counterpart" is stipulable | none needed; the isomorphism is stated directly |
   | Against fading | argues fading is *incoherent*: noticing has nowhere to be tokened, and not-noticing requires senseless self-ignorance that would generalise to us | argues fading is *implausible*: it requires a rational, unimpaired system massively mistaken about its own experience |
   | Against inversion | not addressed | the Dancing Qualia switch, purpose-built for it |
   | Empirical appeal | explicit and two-sided: clinical neuron loss (tumour, stroke, cortical blindness) as a real test of proposition (2) | the reverse move: uses the *constant* low-level physical change in normal brains to argue that the opponent's view implies our qualia dance all the time |

3. **Chalmers's Dancing Qualia argument answers something Cuda's cannot.** Cuda's construction rules out *absence* but says nothing about *inversion*, and Chalmers explains why the fading form cannot be adapted: intermediate mild inversions "preserve the structure of experience throughout", so no dissociation arises. The backup-circuit switch is the new machinery, and it is the reason Chalmers rates Dancing as "an order of magnitude more difficult to accept" than Fading.

4. **Searle conceded, in 1980, something close to the premise these arguments need — and it is in the Author's Response, not the target article.** Replying to Haugeland's demon commentary: "if the stimulation of the causes is at a low enough level to reproduce the causes and not merely describe them, the 'simulation' will reproduce the effects" (pp. 452–453). Whether Cuda's homunculi or Chalmers's chips "reproduce the causes" or "merely describe them" is exactly the disputed point; Searle's own criterion does not settle it. A synthesis that treats Searle and the replacement arguments as talking past each other should cite this sentence as the place they actually meet.

5. **Searle answered Chalmers's scenario seven years before Chalmers published it — in a different book, and Chalmers quotes him.** The *Rediscovery of the Mind* (1992) passage in Chalmers §3 has Searle accepting fading while denying that the subject is *mistaken*: the subject has true beliefs that cannot reach behaviour. Chalmers's counter is that there is nowhere in an organisation-preserving system for those beliefs to live. This is a rare case in the corpus of a direct, quoted exchange between the two principals, and the synthesis should present it as such rather than as two independent positions.

6. **Everyone in this batch uses the word "empirical", and they do not all mean the same thing.**
   - *Searle*: whether a given non-brain material has the brain's causal powers is an empirical question, to be settled by finding out what brains do.
   - *Chalmers*: the target is *empirical (natural, nomological) possibility* as opposed to logical or metaphysical possibility; he concedes the logical possibility of everything he argues against.
   - *Cuda*: proposition (2) is treated as having genuine inductive evidence for and against it, drawn from clinical neurology.
   - *Bostrom*: "merely that a computer running a suitable program would in fact be conscious" — a contingent claim about the actual world.

   Three of the four are making the same move — restricting the claim to contingent, actual-world necessity — and the synthesis should note that this restriction is what makes the debate tractable rather than a clash of modal intuitions.

7. **The grain question is left open by everyone, at four different settings.**
   - Searle: whatever level "reproduces the causes and not merely describes them" — unspecified, and Cuda complains about exactly this in his note 1: Searle "does not tell us what the components must have in common with neurons in order to have the right causal powers, except that it has something to do with biochemistry."
   - Cuda: **circuit functional equivalence** — each neuron's functional role. He says explicitly that he has established sufficiency at this grain and at no coarser one.
   - Chalmers: "fine enough to determine the behavioral capacities and dispositions"; "In the brain, it is likely that the neural level suffices, although a coarser level might also work."
   - Bostrom: "suitably fine-grained detail, such as on the level of individual synapses."

   This is the single most important unresolved parameter in the batch, and it is where Part A2's implementation literature picks up.

8. **The same three example systems recur as the currency of the debate.** Searle's water pipes and wind machines (p. 421) and Weizenbaum's toilet-paper-and-stones computer (p. 423); Block's Chinese population, imported by Chalmers in §2; and the homunculus, which appears as Searle's robot-homunculus (p. 420), as Cuda's neuron-replacing worker, and as Chalmers's intermediate case between silicon chips and a whole population. Chalmers's §3 explicitly chains them: neurons → tiny homunculi → an external population with radio links → homunculi "doubling up" with written records → "a single homunculus does all the work", "yielding something like Searle's 'Chinese room' example." That chain is the single clearest statement in the corpus of why the two camps are arguing about the same object.

9. **Bostrom is the outlier and should be presented as one.** He contributes no argument for or against substrate independence. His value is as evidence about the *status* of the thesis in 2003 — cited, weakened, assumed, and described by its user as "quite widely accepted" in its attenuated form while "not entirely uncontroversial" in general. A synthesis that treats his entry as a fourth position in the argument will misread it.

10. **Version and provenance flags, collected.**
    - The Searle PDF contains 27 open peer commentaries that were **not** reviewed. Any claim in the synthesis about what Block, Dennett, Fodor, Hofstadter, Pylyshyn or the others said in 1980 must go back to the source; this document reports only Searle's characterisations of them.
    - The Chalmers PDF is an **author web version** with no pagination, and its header names *Conscious Experience* (ed. Metzinger, Imprint Academic, 1995) while the corpus manifest notes "JCS 1995". The venue should be checked before citation.
    - The Chalmers reference list and body text disagree on two dates: "Savitt (1980)" in the body vs. "Savitt, S. (1982)" in the list; "Block (1980)" in the body vs. "Block, N. (1981)" in the list (the *Readings in the Philosophy of Psychology* reprint — which is the same reprint reviewed in [Part A1](computational_functionalism_lit_review_A1_functionalism_origins.md), where it is dated 1978 by its own source note and carries printed pages 268–305).
    - The Bostrom PDF's text extraction **dropped all superscripts**. Every power of ten in §4 above is a reconstruction.
