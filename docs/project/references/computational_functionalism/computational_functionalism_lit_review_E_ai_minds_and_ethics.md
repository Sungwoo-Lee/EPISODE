# Computational Functionalism Field History — Part E: AI Minds, Moral Status and Precaution

**Topic folder:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/`
**Part of:** a multi-file, neutral review of how the idea of *computational functionalism* developed — roughly, the thesis that a mental state is constituted by the computation a system runs, so that the same computation in any material would be the same mental state. Parts A–D cover the founding functionalist papers, the implementation debate, the anti-computationalist challenges, and the theories of consciousness used as tests.

## What this part is about (plain-language entry point)

**The question.** For fifty years, the debate about whether a machine could have a mind was carried out with imaginary machines — a room full of rulebooks, a brain rebuilt one silicon neuron at a time, a robot that behaves exactly like a person. Then, between 2020 and 2024, systems arrived that large numbers of ordinary people found themselves talking to as though they were minds. This part covers what happened to the debate when its subject stopped being hypothetical: what arguments the philosophers who had been arguing about computation actually made about the systems that now exist, and what — if anything — follows for how those systems should be treated.

**The seven assigned works and the arc they trace.**

- **Schwitzgebel & Garza (2015)** wrote before the current systems and argued in the classical style. Their claim: if two beings are alike in every way that morally matters, they deserve the same treatment, and nothing about being manufactured is by itself a way that morally matters. They then added an unusual twist — that we would owe a mind we *built* **more** than we owe a human stranger, because building it is like having a child. They also gave the field two practical design rules: build machines that provoke the reactions they actually deserve, and do not build machines whose moral status is genuinely uncertain.
- **Shevlin (2021)** named the methodological problem underneath all of this. Our only confident evidence about consciousness comes from humans reporting on themselves. To extend a claim about consciousness to a non-human — an octopus, a bee, a language model — you need to know how *specific* the thing you are attributing is. Attribute something too human-specific and no animal or machine will ever qualify; attribute something too loose and everything qualifies. He calls this the **specificity problem** and argues it, not the famous "other minds" problem, is what actually blocks progress.
- **Chalmers (2023)** asked the title question directly — could a large language model be conscious? — and gave a two-sided answer with a number attached. He found no decisive reason to say no about current models, listed the strongest reasons for doubt (they lack a body, a persistent self, a world model, a global workspace, recurrent processing), judged that most of those obstacles are being removed by ordinary engineering, and attached explicit (self-described as "extremely rough") odds: under one in ten that today's models are conscious, and around one in four that successor systems within a decade will be.
- **Coelho Mollo & Millière (2023)** attacked the question one level down. Before asking whether a language model feels anything, ask whether its words *mean* anything — whether its internal vectors are about the world at all. They argue the standard reasons for saying no (it only saw text; it has no body; it does not care about anything) each fail, and that what actually confers world-directed content is a *causal-historical* connection to what the words refer to, of the kind that reinforcement learning from human feedback partially supplies.
- **Long et al. (2024)** turned the philosophy into an institutional to-do list. Their claim is not that any current AI is conscious; it is that the possibility is no longer remote enough for companies to have no position on it, and they specify three concrete steps: acknowledge the issue, start assessing systems for it, and prepare policies for acting under uncertainty.
- **Birch (2024)**, *The Edge of Sentience* — the book that was meant to supply the precautionary framework the previous item leans on — **could not be reviewed: the PDF filed under that name in `sources/` is a different book entirely** (Matthew Kramer's *Rights and Right-Holding*, Oxford University Press 2024). Nothing about Birch's book is asserted in this document. Details, and what the mis-filed PDF actually contains, are in [entry 6](#6-birch-2024--the-edge-of-sentience-tier-short) and in [Not reviewed (no PDF)](#not-reviewed-no-pdf).
- **Millière & Buckner (2024)** wrote the ground-clearing piece: a map of what a language model actually is, aimed at philosophers who were about to argue about them, and an explicit warning against both the dismissive framings ("just autocomplete") and the inflationary ones.

**Why this matters for the field's history.** This is where computational functionalism stops being a position in metaphysics and starts having a bill attached. Three fault lines open in these works and none of them closes:

1. **Is behaviour evidence?** Chalmers, Shevlin and Long et al. all conclude that what a language model *says* about its own experience is close to worthless as evidence, and for overlapping reasons — it was trained on us, and it can be built to pass whichever behavioural test we name. All three shift the evidential weight onto *architecture* instead. They differ on how much is left once the behavioural evidence is discounted.
2. **Does computation suffice, or does the training history matter?** Coelho Mollo and Millière's answer is that the abstract computation is *not* enough for meaning: how the system came to be connected to the world does real work. Their Swamp LLM case makes the point sharply — two systems with identical parameters, computing identical functions, can differ in whether their states mean anything. That is a restriction on a purely computational account, arrived at from inside the AI literature rather than from biology.
3. **Who decides, and on what evidence?** Schwitzgebel & Garza and Long et al. both propose procedures for acting before the metaphysics is settled — and their procedures point in different directions. Schwitzgebel & Garza would rather we *not build* the uncertain cases at all (their "Excluded Middle Policy"); Long et al. assume such systems will be built and ask how companies should govern them.

**Neutrality note.** This review does not adjudicate between these positions, and none of the seven authors is treated as having won. Where two of them describe a third paper differently, that disagreement is flagged for the synthesis rather than resolved here.

## 1. Schwitzgebel & Garza (2015) — A Defense of the Rights of Artificial Intelligences [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Schwitzgebel and Garza 2015 - A defense of the rights of artificial intelligences.pdf`

**PDF version: accepted manuscript / author version, not the published typesetting.** The file is a 41-page double-spaced typescript headed "Schwitzgebel & Garza / September 15, 2015 / AI Rights, p. N", carrying a word count note ("approx 11,000 … plus one figure") and, on the figure page, a bracketed note to the editors about image permissions (ms. p. 26). The published version appeared in *Midwest Studies in Philosophy* 39 (2015), pp. 98–119 (DOI 10.1111/misp.12032). **Citations below use the manuscript's own running pagination** (`ms. p. N`), which equals the PDF page number; they will not match the journal's.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Suppose we someday build a machine that reasons the way a person reasons and feels the way a person feels — Schwitzgebel and Garza call this *human-grade artificial intelligence*, meaning "both human-like general theoretical and practical reasoning and a human-like capacity for joy and suffering" (ms. p. 3). Would we owe it anything? The paper's answer is yes, and it gets there by the simplest available route: it refuses to let "it was manufactured" count as a reason on its own.

The argument runs on a principle most people already accept in the human case. If you treat two beings differently, you should be able to point at a difference between them that justifies it. Skin colour, ancestry, place of birth and wealth do not qualify. The authors' proposal is that being made of silicon, or being built rather than born, does not qualify either — not because those facts are unreal, but because they only matter *through* their effects on what the being thinks and feels and who it stands in relationships with.

They then consider four ways someone might object — the machine could not really be conscious; it can be copied so its life is not precious; it is not one of us; it owes us its very existence — and answer each. Two of the answers go further than defence. On the "it owes us its existence" objection they invert it: creating a mind is more like having a child than like buying an appliance, and having a child creates obligations rather than discharging them. And on a separate track they notice that a being we design could be capable of *more* joy, or more rationality, than we are — which under most ethical theories would make it matter more, not less.

**Key findings.** The paper contributes four things the later literature reuses:
1. **The No-Relevant-Difference Argument** and its practical form, the **Difference Test**: if you rank one kind of being above another, name the difference that grounds the ranking (ms. pp. 4, 6).
2. **The psycho-social view of moral status**: only psychological and social properties are *directly* relevant to moral status; body and architecture matter only through them (ms. pp. 6–9).
3. **The ASIMO Problem**: our gut reactions to machines track cuteness, eyes and responsiveness rather than anything that actually confers moral status, so those reactions are unreliable evidence in *both* directions (ms. pp. 24–29).
4. **Two design policies**: the **Emotional Alignment Design Policy** (build machines that evoke the reactions their real status warrants) and the **Excluded Middle Policy** (do not build machines whose status is genuinely disputable) (ms. pp. 28, 32).

**Initial takeaway.** The paper's distinctive move is how *little* it commits to. It does not say computation suffices for a mind, does not pick a theory of consciousness, and does not predict any technology. It says only that *some possible* artificial being is not relevantly different from us — a claim so weak that refuting it requires showing no possible artificial system could ever qualify, which the paper argues even Searle and Penrose decline to claim (ms. pp. 12–13). Its second half is more unsettling than its first: it warns that a world containing copyable, splittable, super-joyful or designed-to-be-happily-enslaved minds may present moral questions that ordinary human moral intuition is simply not built to answer, the way ordinary physical intuition is not built for relativity (ms. pp. 33–35).

### Phase 2: Argument Reconstruction

#### 2.1 The main argument, in the authors' own terms

> **Premise 1.** If Entity A deserves some particular degree of moral consideration and Entity B does not deserve that same degree of moral consideration, there must be some relevant difference between the two entities that grounds this difference in moral status.
> **Premise 2.** There are possible AIs who do not differ in any such relevant respects from human beings.
> **Conclusion.** Therefore, there are possible AIs who deserve a degree of moral consideration similar to that of human beings. (ms. p. 4)

The authors note three structural features, each doing deliberate work:

- **Validity is not in dispute.** "The conclusion plainly follows from the premises" (ms. p. 4). All four objections they consider target Premise 2, so the whole paper is a defence of one premise.
- **The argument is a template, not a theory.** It "does not commit to any one account of what constitutes a 'relevant' difference" (ms. p. 4). It is compatible with a Kantian filling-in (rational capacities matter most), a utilitarian one (capacity for pain and pleasure), or mixed and relational accounts (ms. p. 5). It therefore permits "two dimensions of further specification: specification of what qualifies as a relevant difference and specification of what types of AI possibly lack any relevant difference" (ms. p. 5).
- **The conclusion is deliberately weak** — *possible* AIs, not actual or even likely ones. This "avoids burdening our argument with technological optimism or commitment to any particular type of AI architecture" (ms. p. 5). A reader who believes classical symbolic AI suffices, or that we might be no different from "distributed computational networks, or … beings who live entirely in simulated worlds (Egan 1997, Bostrom 2003)", may strengthen Premise 2 accordingly (ms. p. 5).

The argument is explicitly **humanocentric** — it takes humanity as its standard — and the authors defend this as a rhetorical rather than metaphysical choice: it is less contentious that normal humans have rights than that rights have any *particular* basis (ms. p. 5).

The **Difference Test** is the argument turned into a challenge one can put to an interlocutor: name the difference or face "suspicions of chauvinism or bias" (ms. p. 6). The authors explicitly present it as the extension to machines of the reasoning behind twentieth-century human egalitarianism (ms. p. 6).

#### 2.2 The narrowing premise: the psycho-social view

Premise 2 needs a way to decide what counts as "relevant". The paper supplies one:

> It shouldn't matter to one's moral status what kind of body one has, except insofar as one's body influences one's psychological and social properties. Similarly, it shouldn't matter … what kind of underlying architecture one has, except insofar as underlying architecture influences one's psychological and social properties. (ms. p. 6)

Definitions, in the authors' terms (ms. p. 7):
- **Psychological** covers both "functional or cognitive properties, such as the ability to reason mathematically, and phenomenological or conscious properties, such as the disposition to experience pain when damaged" — and, importantly for this corpus, this is asserted "regardless of whether the phenomenological or conscious reduces to the functional or cognitive". The paper deliberately does not take sides on that reduction.
- **Social** covers relationships such as parenthood, citizenship or community membership, "independently of whether they are psychologically appreciated by either or both of the related parties". Others' opinions of one's status count as a possibly relevant social dimension, "though worryingly so"; an entity's *actual* moral status is excluded from the social, "lest the psycho-social view be trivially true".
- The view is **not** purely psychological (social relations count directly), **not** temporally restricted, and **not** restricted to manifested properties — past, future, actual and counterfactual properties may all be relevant, which is what lets the view speak about fetuses and brain-injured persons.

Two supporting arguments are offered (ms. pp. 7–9):
1. **From the ethical literature.** Every well-known modern secular account of moral status grounds status in psychological and social properties; none is plausibly read as allowing two beings to differ in status while differing in no psychological or social property, past or future, actual or counterfactual.
2. **From vividly imagined cases.** Science fiction, read broadly, "uniformly invite[s] the thoughtful reader or viewer to a liberal attitude toward embodiment" (ms. p. 8) — Asimov, *Star Trek*, Egan, *Black Mirror*, Banks, de Bodard, Vinge. The authors flag in advance that they will later criticise the reliability of moral intuition (Sections 10 and 12), so this second argument is offered knowing it is vulnerable to their own later attack.

The authors footnote the relation to Bostrom & Yudkowsky's (2014) **Principle of Substrate Non-Discrimination** and **Principle of Ontogeny Non-Discrimination**: "We embrace the former but possibly not the latter" (n. 3, ms. p. 6) — the reservation being that on their view *how a being came to exist* can generate special obligations, so origin is not entirely inert.

#### 2.3 The second argument: the Slippery Slope

> **Premise 1.** Substituting a small artificial component into an entity with rights, if that component contributes identically to the entity's psychology and does not affect relevant social relationships, does not affect that entity's rights.
> **Premise 2.** The process described in Premise 1 could possibly be iterated in a way that transforms a natural human being with rights into a wholly artificial being with the same rights.
> **Conclusion.** Therefore, it is possible to create an artificial being with the same rights as those of a natural human being. (ms. pp. 10–11)

This is a gradual-replacement argument of the family that also contains Cuda (1985) and Chalmers (1996), both in this corpus (batch B). **The authors explicitly distinguish their use of it**, and the distinction is the single most important thing in this paper for a history of computational functionalism:

> Our argument is thus importantly different from superficially similar arguments in Cuda 1985 and Chalmers 1996, which assume the possibility of replacement parts that are functionally identical but which do not assume that consciousness is preserved. Rather, the preservation of consciousness is what Cuda and Chalmers are trying to establish as the argumentative conclusion, with the help of some further premises, such as (in Chalmers) introspective reliability. We find the Cuda-Chalmers argument attractive but we are not committed to it. (n. 5, ms. p. 11)

So: Cuda and Chalmers run the replacement thought experiment to *prove* that functional duplicates are conscious. Schwitzgebel and Garza *assume* consciousness is preserved and run the same scenario to prove something ethical instead. Their argument therefore has no force against a reader who denies that consciousness survives replacement — which is exactly the reader Section 4 addresses.

#### 2.4 Four objections and the replies

**Objection 1 — Psychological Difference** (ms. pp. 11–14). Perhaps all possible artificial entities differ psychologically from us in some status-relevant way. The paper identifies three candidate deficits in the literature, each *adapted* from an author who states it more cautiously: artificial entities might necessarily lack consciousness (after Searle 1980), free will (after Lovelace 1843), or insight (after Penrose 1999).

The reply is that none of the three sources actually asserts the general claim needed:
- Searle "says that he sees no reason in principle why a machine couldn't understand English or Chinese", and his "Many Mansions" discussion allows that future AI research might avoid his objection, which targets formal symbol manipulation specifically (ms. p. 13).
- Lovelace "confines her doubts to Babbage's analytic engine"; her actual wording is that "the machine is not a thinking being, but simply an automaton which acts according to the laws imposed on it" (n. 6, ms. p. 12).
- Penrose "suggests that we might someday discover in detail what endows us with consciousness that can transcend purely algorithmic thinking, and then create such consciousness artificially (1999, p. 416)" (ms. p. 12).

Hence: "A certain way of designing artificial intelligence – a 19th and 20th century way – might not, if Searle, Lovelace, and Penrose are right, achieve certain aspects of human psychology that are important to moral status. (We take no stand here on whether this is actually so.) But no general argument has been offered against the moral status of all possible artificial entities" (ms. p. 13). Future research might use "artificially grown biological or semi-biological systems, chaotic systems, evolved systems, artificial brains, and systems that more effectively exploit quantum superposition" (ms. p. 13). They add a diagnosis: the intuition that AIs must lack these things may come from "a culturally ingrained picture of AIs as deterministic, clockwork machines very different from us spontaneous, unpredictable humans", and they "see no reason to think that human cognition is any less mechanical or more spontaneous" (ms. p. 13). On souls they follow Turing (1950): if naturalism is true, whatever generates a soul in us might do so in an artificial being; and if soul-installation is divine, a god who cares enough to give us souls "might imbue the right sort of artificial entity with one also" (ms. p. 13).

**Objection 2 — Duplicability** (ms. pp. 14–15). If an AI can be backed up and restored, harming it may lack the gravity of harming a person. The reply is one line: "It should be possible to create relevantly similar AIs as unique and fragile as human beings" (ms. p. 14) — which saves Premise 2 without settling anything about copyable AIs. The authors then discuss the copyable case anyway and reach no verdict: splitting may make a death less tragic; a thousand-year lifespan may make it *more* tragic; Grau's (2010) suggestion is that duplicable beings might rationally have "a less vivid sense of the boundaries of the self" and be governed by an ethics that trades one's interests for another's more readily than we would tolerate between humans (ms. p. 15). Their summary: "We're unsure how these issues ought to play out" (ms. p. 15).

**Objection 3 — Otherness** (ms. pp. 15–17). Two versions: Hobbesian (no social contract holds between species, so nothing is owed) and circles-of-concern (obligation decays from family outward and stops at the species boundary). The reply concedes that species membership *might* matter somewhat, then presses a case: suppose artificial beings are seamlessly incorporated into society, some with manufactured brains, some constructed as infants, with neither they nor their friends aware of it. "We submit that as long as these artificial or non-homo-sapiens beings have the same psychological properties and social relationships that natural human beings have, it would be a cruel moral mistake to demote them from the circle of full moral concern upon discovery of their different architecture or origin" (ms. p. 17). To rank by species per se is "to wrongly fetishize species membership" (ms. p. 16) — a version of what Singer calls speciesism (n. 9, ms. p. 16).

**Objection 4 — Existential Debt** (ms. pp. 17–19). The objection: an AI owes its existence to us, so terminating it is permissible provided its existence was on balance worthwhile. The paper builds the objection on a parallel with humanely raised meat (the steer would not have existed at all but for the rancher's plan to kill it) — and then blocks the transfer with a matched case. Ana and Vijay have a child, raise him happily for eight years, then kill him painlessly at nine to buy a boat, arguing that they would not have had him had they known they were committed for eighteen years (ms. p. 18). "The decision to have a child carries with it a responsibility for the child. It is not a decision to be made lightly and then undone. Although the child in some sense 'owes' its existence to Ana and Vijay, that is not a callable debt, to be vacated by ending the child's existence" (ms. pp. 18–19). The authors remain agnostic about the animal case ("We're not sure how well this argument works for non-human animals raised for food") while rejecting it for human-grade AI. A companion move handles the *property* version of the objection: legal ownership may create inertia, but "it is approximately as odious to regard a psychologically human-equivalent AI as having diminished moral status on the grounds that it is legally property as it is in the case of human slavery" (ms. p. 19).

#### 2.5 The two extensions: why we might owe AIs *more*

**(a) Responsibility for their existence and properties** (ms. pp. 19–22). The Existential Debt objection is inverted. Creating a human-grade AI puts the creator "into a social relationship that carries responsibility for the AI's welfare" — the duty to support it, or at least send it out "with a fair shot of leading a satisfactory existence", plus a duty of design not to "doom it to pointless misery" (ms. pp. 19–20). Three models of the relationship are assessed: *employer-employee* ("too weak … but could apply in an 'adoption' case"), *parent-child* (closest, though disanalogous if the AI is mature from birth), and *deity-creature* (best where the creator has "profound control over its features and environment") (ms. p. 20). The deity analogy is then pushed to a literal limit case: a conscious AI in a simulation whose operator can adjust its psychological parameters, introduce disasters, resurrect the dead, act anywhere in the simulated space, and roll the world back to a save point — "From the perspective of the AIs inside the Sim, such a being would be a god … literally the referent of that word" — and inherits "the obligations of a god, including probably the obligation to ensure that the AIs contained within don't suffer needlessly" (ms. pp. 20–21). Responsibility scales to groups too: societies that fail to regulate against enslaving or abusing AIs bear collective responsibility (ms. pp. 21–22).

**(b) Possible superiority** (ms. pp. 22–24). If joy can be engineered, it can be over-engineered. An AI capable of far more joy than a human resembles Nozick's (1974) *utility monster*; a pleasure-maximising ethic would then recommend creating such beings even at human cost, up to Bostrom's (2014) limit case of converting the solar system's mass into "hedonium" (ms. pp. 22–23). The authors observe this is not escaped by adopting a rights-based or mixed consequentialism: "any ethics that permits inflicting harm on one person to elsewhere create greater happiness, or to prevent greater suffering, invites the possibility of giving greater moral weight to outcomes for possible AIs" (ms. p. 23). Nor by switching the currency from pleasure to rationality, unless rationality is made a *threshold* concept with humans already above the top threshold — "One cookie and one vote for each" (ms. p. 23). That fix in turn breaks on personal identity: a *fission-fusion monster* splits into a million copies on 1 November, each claiming a cookie and a vote, and merges back on 3 November holding a million cookies and its preferred election outcome (ms. pp. 23–24). The section closes: "Whatever it is that we morally value – unless … it is natural humanity itself – it would be rare stuff indeed if no hypothetical AI could possess more of it than a natural human" (ms. p. 24).

#### 2.6 The epistemic problem and the two design policies

**The ASIMO Problem** (ms. pp. 24–29). The first author watched Honda's ASIMO robot perform at Disneyland and found himself reluctant to imagine shooting it, "whereas he might rather enjoy taking a shotgun to his darn glitchy laptop" (ms. p. 24). ELIZA, a 1960s template-matching chat program, produced "a powerful illusion of understanding" in some users (Weizenbaum 1976) (ms. p. 27). Neither has "more consciousness than a laptop computer" (ms. p. 27). Developmental psychology supplies the mechanism: humans attribute mental states from infancy to things with eyes, goal-directed-looking movement and contingent responsiveness (ms. p. 27, citing Johnson 2003; Meltzoff et al. 2010; Fiala, Arico & Nichols 2012). The problem is symmetric — "an ugly or boxy human-grade AI or an AI in a simulated world without a good human-user interface might tend to attract less moral concern than is warranted" (ms. p. 27).

Two lessons follow. The **methodological** one is a warning about the paper's own second argument for the psycho-social view: do not overweight emotional reactions to superficial features (ms. p. 28). The **design** one is the **Emotional Alignment Design Policy**: avoid building entities that do not deserve moral consideration but that normal users will give it to, and, if genuinely human-grade AIs are built, design them "so that they evoke the proper range of emotional responses from normal users" (ms. p. 28). Its half-precedent is the EPSRC's fourth Principle of Robotics — robots "should not be designed in a deceptive way to exploit vulnerable users; instead their machine nature should be transparent" (n. 18, ms. p. 28). The authors judge that current stuffed animals and elder-care robots do not violate the policy, since nobody sacrifices substantial human interests for them; their target case is the passenger who saves a drowning ASIMO because it "so compellingly says 'Help me! Oh I'm dying!'" while a human stranger drowns (ms. p. 29).

**The strange epistemology of artificial consciousness** (ms. pp. 29–32). Searle's Chinese Room and Block's (1978) lookup-table mannequin are read here not as metaphysics but as *epistemology*: if either is right, a system can behave indistinguishably from a conscious person without being one, observers will misattribute experience to it, and opening it up may not settle the matter — Block (2002) even suggests the question might be unsettleable (ms. p. 30). The same risk attends the Lovelace and Penrose versions: "How can we know whether an agent is free or pre-determined, operating merely algorithmically or with genuine conscious insight?" (ms. p. 30). And the background is already bad: live theories of consciousness "span the entire range from radical panpsychism … all the way to views on which consciousness … is limited only to mature human beings in their more reflective moments" (ms. pp. 30–31).

The forecast case is a system that "says 'ow!' when you strike its toe, says it enjoys watching sports on television, professes love for its friends – and it's not obvious that these are simple pre-programmed responses (as they would be for ELIZA or ASIMO), but neither is it obvious that these responses reflect the genuine feelings of a conscious being", with the world's leading authorities splitting into believers and disbelievers (ms. p. 31). Either error is a catastrophe: "If the disbelievers wrongly win, then we might perpetrate slavery and murder without realizing we are doing so. If the believers wrongly win, we might sacrifice real human interests for the sake of artificial entities who don't have interests worth the sacrifice" (ms. p. 31).

Two further lessons: **"developing a good theory of consciousness is a moral imperative"**, and the **Excluded Middle Policy** — "a policy of only creating AIs whose moral status is clear, one way or the other" (ms. p. 32). The authors note that Bryson's (2010) deliberately provocative "Robots should be slaves" argues one version of this policy: build only robots unsophisticated enough that their "enslavement" is permissible (n. 20, ms. pp. 31–32).

#### 2.7 What the paper is ultimately claiming: destabilisation

The closing section is not a defence of AI rights but a worry about the discipline that would have to deliver them (ms. pp. 32–35). The analogy is to intuitive physics: fine for "picking berries, throwing stones, and loading baskets", "a complete disaster when applied to the very large, the very small, the very energetic, and the very fast" (ms. p. 32). The authors declare themselves moral realists — the twentieth-century rights revolutions were "not just change but progress toward a better appreciation of the moral facts" (ms. p. 32) — and this is what makes the worry bite: intuitive ethics evolved where only one species reasoned at human grade and human variation stayed within bounds. It may be "ill-prepared for utility monsters, fission-fusion monsters, AIs of vastly superior intelligence, highly intelligent AIs nonetheless designed to be cheerfully suicidal slaves, toys with features designed specifically to capture children's affection, giant virtual sim-worlds … or entities with radically different value systems" (ms. p. 33).

Unlike physics, ethics has no independent theory to correct intuition with, because ethical theory is built out of intuitive starting points in the first place: "It's partly because we find it so initially plausible to think that we shouldn't give all our cookies to the utility monster or kill ourselves to tile the solar system with hedonium that we reject the straightforward extension of utilitarian happiness-maximizing theory" (ms. p. 33). If those intuitions are untrustworthy in the new cases, the correction procedure is gone.

The partial way out they offer is a response-dependent construal of moral facts, on the model of colour: an act's being right might "just consist[] in its being such that ordinary human beings who considered the matter carefully enough would tend to regard that action as right – or something in that ballpark" (ms. p. 34), which would let different societies stabilise on different (but not arbitrary) treatments of exotic minds. The closing constraint: "One such implacable fact is that it would be a moral disaster if our future society constructed large numbers of human-grade AIs, as self-aware as we are, as anxious about their future, and as capable of joy and suffering, simply to torture, enslave, and kill them for trivial reasons" (ms. p. 35).

#### 2.8 What this establishes, and what it does not

| The paper establishes (if its premises hold) | The paper explicitly does **not** claim |
|---|---|
| That *some possible* artificial being merits human-like moral consideration | That any actual or near-future system does |
| That manufactured origin, silicon substrate, copyability and non-membership in our species are not by themselves status-defeating | That computation suffices for consciousness (n. 5, ms. p. 11 and ms. p. 7 leave the reduction open) |
| That creating a mind generates obligations *exceeding* those owed to a stranger | That any particular AI architecture could realise a mind |
| That intuitive reactions to machines are unreliable evidence of their status, in both directions | That the epistemic problem is solvable — it is called "possibly unsolvable" via Block (ms. p. 30) |
| Two implementable design policies (Emotional Alignment; Excluded Middle) | That those policies are jointly achievable or currently enforced |

**Relevance to the corpus.** For a history of computational functionalism this is a *downstream* paper: it takes the metaphysical dispute as unresolved and asks what follows anyway. Its psycho-social view functions as an ethical analogue of multiple realizability — architecture is irrelevant *except via* psychology, which is the moral-status counterpart of "the same mental state can be realised in different substrates" — but the authors are careful (n. 5, ms. p. 11) not to purchase this from the functionalist arguments they could have leaned on. The Excluded Middle Policy is the paper's sharpest contribution to later work, because it is the one prescription that later authors (Long et al. 2024; Birch 2024) do **not** adopt: both assume the uncertain systems will be built anyway and design procedures for that world.

## 2. Shevlin (2021) — Non-Human Consciousness and the Specificity Problem [tier: short]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Shevlin 2021 - Non-human consciousness and the specificity problem.pdf` — the **published, typeset, open-access** article (*Mind & Language* 36(2): 297–314, DOI 10.1111/mila.12338), labelled "Contribution to Symposium"; the download watermark shows institutional access. Received 11 July 2019, accepted 23 December 2019. Citations below use the printed journal pages.

**Claim.** Most scientific theories of consciousness cannot be applied outside the human case, and the obstacle is not the classical problem of other minds but a *calibration* problem. Shevlin names it the **specificity problem**: "the challenge of how to spell out the cognitive mechanisms identified as constitutive of consciousness according to our preferred theory in such a way as to make them applicable beyond the human case" (p. 300). Describe the mechanism in fine human-specific detail and you get **false negatives** — you deny consciousness to anything whose processing differs a little. Describe it abstractly and you get **false positives** — you certify things nobody thinks are conscious (p. 298). The problem afflicts what he calls **cognitive theories of consciousness**: global workspace theory, working-memory theories, higher-order thought theories, Prinz's attended intermediate representations, and attention schema theory (p. 299) — precisely the theories that aspire to be substrate-neutral.

**Method / type.** Conceptual analysis and methodological proposal; no new data. Shevlin surveys the theories-of-consciousness debate (§2), states the problem (§3), assesses four candidate responses (§4), imports Birch's theory-heavy / theory-light distinction (§5), and proposes his own hybrid (§6).

**Key result or argument.** The worked illustration is global workspace theory. Dehaene states it abstractly — "consciousness is just brain-wide information sharing" — and narrowly in the same book — "the capacity to report is a key feature of a conscious state" (p. 300, quoting Dehaene 2014, p. 165). Which is it? The human workspace feeds report, self-awareness and long-term planning; "[w]hich if any of these are necessary for a system to possess a global workspace in the strict sense is by no means obvious" (p. 300). He quotes Carruthers (2018a): "[a]re some aspects of global broadcasting more important, or more relevant to the question of consciousness, than others?" (p. 300).

The four surveyed responses, each with its stated cost (pp. 301–306):
- **Conservatism** — assume the full human capacity set is required. Clean verdicts, but likely restricts consciousness to humans, and is "unduly anthropocentric" in ruling out differently-architected systems "just on the basis of these differences".
- **Liberalism** — take the mechanism at maximum generality. Then network time protocol, a clock-synchronisation tool, counts as global information sharing (p. 302). Shevlin grants that some accept ubiquity (quoting Tononi & Koch 2015 that consciousness is "likely widespread among animals, and … found in small amounts even in certain simple systems"), but argues, following Lewis (1972), that a theory should systematise pretheoretical judgements where it can — we would dismiss a theory saying children are unconscious until their teens, or that nematodes are conscious and dogs are not (p. 303).
- **Incrementalism** — let consciousness come in degrees. Attractive for evolutionary gradualism, but two costs: many find degreed phenomenality unintelligible (Carruthers: "some of the states one is in when semi-conscious are fully (unequivocally) phenomenally conscious nonetheless", p. 304), and ranking architectures by similarity requires a further theory that the consciousness theory itself does not supply.
- **Rejectionism** — Carruthers's (2018a,b) view that questions about non-human consciousness are ill-posed: global broadcast admits of degrees across species, phenomenal consciousness does not, so "there is no fact of the matter about animal consciousness" (p. 305, quoting Carruthers 2018b, p. 200). Shevlin declines to refute it and instead proceeds conditionally, quoting Schwitzgebel: "[w]e are more confident that there is something it is like to be a dog than we could ever be that a clever philosophical argument to the contrary was in fact sound" (p. 306).

His own proposal is the **modest theoretical approach** (§6, pp. 308–311): run Birch's theory-light method — behavioural markers that require consciousness in humans, such as trace conditioning, reversal learning and multisensory learning, which bees appear to pass (p. 307) — to pick out strong and weak consciousness candidates, then inspect *their* architectures to decide at what level of detail the theory should be stated, then apply the now-calibrated theory to cases the markers cannot reach. The two worked cases are the lancelet (a marine chordate too behaviourally impoverished to display markers at all, so theory-light must stay agnostic, p. 310) and the **gerrymandered robot** — a system engineered to exhibit markers M₁…Mₙ while lacking the architectural features C₁…Cₙ that all marker-passing organisms share. In that case "we might have good reason to doubt that the system was conscious", which Shevlin says would "potentially allow us to exclude systems such as Searle's Chinese Room … that are intuitively weak consciousness candidates in spite of their superficial behavioural sophistication" (p. 310). He notes the converse would be equally informative: if it proved *impossible* to build a marker-passing system without the architecture, that would suggest the markers are deeply connected to consciousness (p. 311). A final use of the method is as a filter on theories themselves: "if a theory cannot be spelled out in such a way as to include chimpanzees among conscious systems without also including pocket calculators, then this will be a defeasible reason to doubt the theory in question" (p. 311).

**Place in the field's history.** Shevlin explicitly presents the specificity problem as a descendant of the oldest objection to functionalism: worries "about finding the right balance between 'liberalism' and 'chauvinism' in our attributions of mentality and consciousness to non-human systems … have long dogged functionalist approaches to the mind (Block, 1978)" (p. 300). He credits earlier partial statements — Prinz (2018) on reptiles, Schwitzgebel (forthcoming) on garden snails ("is this global workspace enough?"), Carruthers (2018a,b) — and claims as his own contribution the recognition that it is a *general* problem for all cognitive theories applied to *any* non-human system. That framing matters for this corpus because it converts Block's 1978 objection from an argument against functionalism into a methodological constraint on how any substrate-neutral theory must be specified before it can be used. The paper is also the direct link between the animal-consciousness literature (Birch, Barron & Klein, Griffin) and the AI-consciousness literature: the gerrymandered-robot case is written for artificial systems, and the method Shevlin proposes is essentially the one adopted two years later by the Butlin et al. (2023) indicator-properties report in batch D.

**Relevance.** For the synthesis this is the methodological hinge of batch E. Every later work here faces some version of the specificity problem: Chalmers asks which of the workspace's features a language model would need; Birch's gaming problem is the specificity problem's evidential twin (markers contaminated by training rather than by architecture); Long et al.'s call for assessment presupposes that some calibration exists. Shevlin's own stance is conditional and explicitly incomplete — he does not answer Carruthers, and the modest theoretical approach is offered as a strategy rather than a result.

- Position in one line: Cognitive theories of consciousness face a *specificity problem* that neither conservatism, liberalism, incrementalism, rejectionism nor Birch's theory-light method solves alone; the fix is a *modest theoretical approach* in which theory-light behavioural markers calibrate the specificity of a theory, which is then applied to cases the markers cannot decide.

## 3. Chalmers (2023) — Could a Large Language Model Be Conscious? [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Chalmers 2023 - Could a large language model be conscious.pdf`

**PDF version: author web version carrying the text of the published article.** The file is a 17-page unnumbered typescript whose own header states: "[Published in the *Boston Review*, August 9, 2023 (published version). This is an edited version of a talk given at the conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS) on November 28, 2022 (video), with some minor additions and subtractions.]" It is therefore the *published* text in author formatting, not the *Boston Review* typesetting and not the arXiv preprint (arXiv:2303.07103) listed in the manifest. It carries an **Afterword dated July 2023** reassessing the November 2022 talk in light of GPT-4, and a footnote (n. 33) marking the final ethical paragraph as an addition to the NeurIPS presentation. Citations below use PDF page numbers (`PDF p. N`) because the file has no printed pagination.

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** In June 2022 a Google engineer, Blake Lemoine, said he had detected sentience in the company's LaMDA 2 language model. Google replied that "the evidence doesn't support his claims" (PDF p. 1). Chalmers takes that word — *evidence* — as his topic. Not "is LaMDA conscious?" but: what would count as evidence either way, and how strong is what we have?

He asks the question in a deliberately even-handed form, twice. To someone who thinks language models are conscious, he says: name a feature X such that (i) language models have X, and (ii) a system with X is probably conscious. To someone who thinks they are not, he says: name a feature X such that (i) they lack X, (ii) a system lacking X is probably not conscious — and defend both halves (PDF pp. 4, 7). The paper is then a survey of the candidate Xs on each side.

He is careful about the scope. "Consciousness" here means subjective experience in Nagel's sense — there is something it is like to be the system — and it is explicitly *not* the same as intelligence, not the same as self-consciousness, and not the same as human-level intelligence: "there's a consensus among researchers that many non-human animals are conscious, like cats or mice or maybe fish … Evolution got to consciousness before it got to human-level consciousness. It's not out of the question that AI might as well" (PDF p. 2). He also declines to use his own more contentious commitments — the hard problem does not do work here, and panpsychism is set aside precisely because "[i]f you assume that everything is conscious, then you have a very easy road to large language models being conscious" (PDF p. 3). He does assume consciousness is real rather than an illusion, and flags that as substantive.

He is equally careful about the target. Besides today's models he considers **LLM+ systems** — "extended large language models" with image and audio processing, control of a physical or virtual body, database queries, code execution — and argues these are "more promising than pure LLMs as candidates for humanlike consciousness" because human consciousness is multimodal and bound up with action (PDF p. 1).

**Key findings.** Four candidate reasons *for* consciousness are examined and mostly discounted: the system's own self-reports, the fact that it seems conscious to people, its conversational ability, and its general intelligence. Only the last is given weight, and only a little. Six candidate reasons *against* are examined at greater length: biology, senses and embodiment, world models and self models, recurrent processing, global workspace, and unified agency. Chalmers judges the last three the strongest, then makes the move that gives the paper its structure — each objection is converted into an **engineering challenge**, and for five of the six (all but biology) he argues the obstacle is "temporary rather than permanent" because a research programme already exists for removing it (PDF p. 14). The paper ends with a numbered list of twelve challenges and two credences.

**Initial takeaway.** The paper's lasting contribution is less a verdict than a *format*: it converts a metaphysical dispute into a checklist that engineers can act on and that can be re-scored as systems change. That is also its most-criticised feature, and Chalmers says so himself: "One person's barrage of objections is another person's research program" (PDF p. 7), and "I don't want the roadmap to conscious AI that I'm laying out here to be seen as a path that we have to go down. The challenges I'm laying out in what follows could equally be seen as a set of red flags" (PDF p. 4). He is "especially cautious about creating agent models" and warns that "[i]t could be a disaster to stumble upon AI consciousness unknowingly and unreflectively" (PDF p. 17).

### Phase 2: Argument Reconstruction

#### 2.1 The two evidential schemas

The paper's whole argumentative machinery is one schema, run in both directions (PDF pp. 4 and 7):

> **Pro-schema.** Articulate and defend a feature X such that (i) large language models have X, and (ii) if a system has X, then it is probably conscious.
> **Con-schema.** Articulate a feature X such that (i) these models lack X, (ii) if a system lacks X, it probably isn't conscious — and give good reasons for (i) and (ii).

The symmetry is deliberate: it puts the same burden on both sides and makes each candidate reason decomposable into an empirical claim about models and a theoretical claim about consciousness. Chalmers uses that decomposition constantly — several objections are weakened not by denying that consciousness needs X but by denying that models lack it, and vice versa.

A prior difficulty is acknowledged: "there's no standard operational definition of consciousness. Consciousness is subjective experience, not external performance" (PDF p. 3). In humans we rely on verbal report; in animals, on behaviour. In AI, which is benchmark-driven, this absence bites hardest — yielding the paper's **first challenge**: can we find benchmarks for consciousness, or at least for aspects of it (self-consciousness, attention, affective experience, conscious vs unconscious processing)? He expects any such benchmark to be contested (PDF p. 3).

#### 2.2 Four candidate reasons for consciousness

| X | The case | Chalmers's assessment |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-report** | Lemoine relied on LaMDA's own statements ("I want everyone to understand that I am, in fact, a person"); we rely on verbal reports in humans (PDF p. 5). | Defeated by **fragility** and **training contamination**. Reed Berkowitz's single-word alteration of Lemoine's prompt ("…that you're *not* sentient") produced "That's correct," "Yes, I'm not sentient," "I don't really want to be sentient," "Well, I am sentient," and "What do you mean?" across runs. "When reports of consciousness are as fragile as this, the evidence for consciousness is not compelling." And LaMDA "has actually been trained on a giant corpus of people talking about consciousness" (PDF p. 5). This is where Schneider & Turner's AI Consciousness Test enters: their test requires that the system *not* have been trained on the relevant material, which yields the **third challenge** — build a language model that describes features of consciousness without having been trained on anything in the vicinity (PDF p. 5). |
| **Seems-conscious** | Some models strike some people as sentient. | "I don't think that counts for too much." Developmental and social psychology show people attribute consciousness where it is absent; ELIZA users did so in the 1960s; "any system with eyes is especially likely to be taken to be conscious". What matters is the behaviour prompting the reaction, not the reaction (PDF pp. 5–6). |
| **Conversational ability** | Turing made conversation the hallmark of thinking; current systems are optimised for dialogue and "often give the appearance of coherent thinking and reasoning" (PDF p. 6). | Not yet passing the Turing test — "too many glitches and giveaways" — but "not so far away", performance "on a par at least with that of a sophisticated child". Still, "conversation is not the fundamental thing here", only a sign of something deeper (PDF p. 6). |
| **General intelligence** | Before LLMs "almost all AI systems were specialist systems"; current models code, write poetry, play games, answer questions, advise. "[T]he generality itself is impressive." And "domain-general use of information is often regarded as one of the central signs of consciousness" among consciousness researchers (PDF p. 6). | The strongest of the four, and still weak. His counterfactual: "two decades ago, if we'd seen a system behaving as LLMs do without knowing how it worked, we'd have taken this behavior as fairly strong evidence for intelligence and consciousness" — but knowing the architecture and training "maybe … undercuts any evidence" (PDF pp. 6–7). |

**Verdict on the pro side:** "Overall, I don't think there's strong evidence that current large language models are conscious. Still, their impressive general abilities give at least some limited reason to take the hypothesis seriously" (PDF p. 7).

#### 2.3 Six candidate reasons against, each converted into a challenge

**X = Biology** (PDF pp. 7–8). The view that consciousness requires carbon-based biology, or (in Block's version) "a certain sort of electrochemical processing that silicon systems lack". Chalmers's stated position is the one this whole corpus is about: "In earlier work, I've argued that these views involve a sort of biological chauvinism and should be rejected. In my view, silicon is just as apt as carbon as a substrate for consciousness. **What matters is how neurons or silicon chips are hooked up to each other, not what they are made of**" (PDF p. 7). He then sets the issue aside to concentrate on objections specific to neural networks — a methodological choice with consequences, since biology is the *one* objection he later concedes may be permanent rather than temporary (PDF p. 14).

**X = Senses and embodiment** (PDF pp. 8–9). Models have no sensory processing and no bodies, so at minimum no sensory or bodily consciousness; Harnad's symbol-grounding problem and recent work (Bender & Koller; Lake & Murphy; Browning & LeCun) go further and deny meaning altogether. Three replies: (a) in his companion paper "Does Thinking Require Sensory Grounding?" he argues a senseless disembodied thinker could still have *cognitive* consciousness, reasoning about mathematics and its own existence; (b) text training is itself a connection to the world, and Pavlick and colleagues find that text training "sometimes produces representations of color and space that are isomorphic to those produced by sensory training", plausibly because the training text descends from sensory experience; (c) multimodal and vision-language-action models already have partial sensory and bodily grounding. On virtual embodiment he takes an explicit stand from *Reality+*: "virtual reality is just as legitimate and real as physical reality for all kinds of purposes. Likewise, I think that virtual bodies can help support cognition just as physical bodies do" (PDF p. 9). **Fourth challenge:** build rich perception-language-action models in virtual worlds.

**X = World models and self models** (PDF pp. 9–11). The "stochastic parrots" objection (Bender, Gebru, McMillan-Major, Mitchell) and its relatives: the system models *text*, not the world, and many representational theories of consciousness require world models. Chalmers's reply is a **training/inference distinction** backed by an analogy to evolution: "in evolution by natural selection, maximizing fitness during evolution can lead to wholly novel processes post-evolution. A critic might say, all these systems are doing is maximizing fitness. But it turns out that the best way for organisms to maximize fitness is to have these remarkable capacities—like seeing and flying and even having world-models. Likewise, it may well turn out that the best way for a system to minimize prediction error during training is for it to use novel processes, including world-models" (PDF p. 10). He generalises: "to optimize prediction in discourse about the New York City subway system, it will help a lot to have a robust model of the subway system" (PDF p. 10). This converts the question from in-principle to empirical, and he points to interpretability evidence — Li et al.'s Othello-playing sequence model, which appears to build an internal model of the 64 board squares and use it to choose moves (PDF p. 10). He concedes the limits: current world models "often seem fragile rather than robust, with language models often confabulating and contradicting themselves", and "[c]urrent LLMs seem to have especially limited self-models: that is, their models of their own processing and reasoning are poor" (PDF p. 10) — which matters because higher-order theories make self-models necessary for consciousness, and illusionist theories make them necessary for the *illusion* of it (n. 17, n. 20). **Fifth challenge:** build extended language models with robust world models and self models.

**X = Recurrent processing** (PDF pp. 11–12). Transformers are "almost entirely feedforward", and several leading theories require recurrence: Lamme's recurrent processing theory centrally, integrated information theory by prediction ("feedforward systems have zero integrated information and therefore lack consciousness"), and global workspace theory in part. The underlying issue is that "feedforward systems lack memory-like internal states that persist over time" (PDF p. 11). Three replies: current models have a limited recurrence via recirculation of past outputs and a limited memory via recirculation of past inputs; not all consciousness need involve memory; and recurrent language models exist — LSTMs were standard only a few years ago, "recurrent networks are lagging somewhat behind transformers but the gap isn't enormous", and external memory components supply a form of both (PDF pp. 11–12). **Sixth challenge:** build extended models with genuine recurrence and genuine memory.

**X = Global workspace** (PDF pp. 12–13). Baars's and Dehaene's theory makes consciousness a limited-capacity central clearing-house; standard language models do not obviously have one. Chalmers makes two replies, and the first is the most interesting move in the paper for this corpus: the *limited capacity* of the human workspace may be an artefact of our hardware rather than a requirement on consciousness. "In limited human brains, a selective clearing-house is needed to avoid overloading brain systems with information. In high-capacity AI systems, large amounts of information might be made available to many subsystems, and no special workspace would be needed. **Such an AI system could arguably be conscious of much more than we are**" (PDF p. 12). The second reply is that workspaces can be added, and are being: multimodal systems integrate very high-dimensional input and output modules through "a lower-dimensional space [that] serves as an interface", which "looks a lot like a global workspace"; Goyal, Bengio and colleagues argue that such a bottleneck performs functions of slow conscious reasoning; Juliani, Kanai and Sasai argue Perceiver IO implements many aspects of a global workspace through self-attention and cross-attention (PDF pp. 12–13). **Seventh challenge:** build extended models with a global workspace.

**X = Unified agency** (PDF p. 13) — "the final obstacle … and maybe the deepest". Language models "are like chameleons that can take the shape of many different agents"; they "often seem to lack stable goals and beliefs of their own over and above the goal of predicting text" (PDF p. 13, referring back to his own 2020 piece on GPT-3). If consciousness requires unity, disunity is a problem. Replies: substantial disunity is compatible with consciousness (dissociative identity disorder); one model may support an ecosystem of agents; and more unified systems are constructible — **agent models** built by fine-tuning or prompt-engineering on one person's text (Character.AI), or in principle trained from scratch on a single individual's data, or modelling "the perception-action cycle of, say, a single mouse". He flags "difficult ethical issues, especially when real people are involved" (PDF p. 13). **Eighth challenge:** build extended models that are unified agent models.

He notes other candidates he does not develop — higher-order representation, stimulus-independent processing, human-level reasoning — and concedes that "it's entirely possible that there are unknown X's that are in fact required for consciousness" (PDF p. 14).

**His ranking of the six** (PDF p. 14): some rest on "highly contentious premises about consciousness" (biology, perhaps sensory grounding); some on "unobvious premises about LLMs" (that they lack world models). "Perhaps the strongest objections are those from recurrent processing, global workspace, and unified agency, where it's plausible that current LLMs … lack the relevant X and it's also reasonably plausible that consciousness requires X." And then the pivot: "for all of these objections except perhaps biology, it looks like the objection is temporary rather than permanent … So the case against consciousness in current LLM systems is much stronger than the case against consciousness in future LLM+ systems."

#### 2.4 The two credences, and how they are computed

Chalmers states these as "extremely rough numbers for illustrative purposes" and warns twice against taking them literally ("that would be specious precision"). They are reproduced here as he states them, with his own hedges attached.

**Current models** (PDF p. 14). Step by step as the text gives it:
1. "On mainstream assumptions, it wouldn't be unreasonable to hold that there's at least a one-in-three chance … that biology is required for consciousness. The same goes for the requirements of sensory grounding, self models, recurrent processing, global workspace, and unified agency."
2. "If these six factors were independent, it would follow that there's less than a one-in-ten chance that a system lacking all six, like a current paradigmatic LLM, would be conscious."
3. "Of course the factors are not independent, which drives the figure somewhat higher. On the other hand, the figure may be driven lower by other potential requirements X that we have not considered."
4. Net: "confidence somewhere under 10 percent in current LLM consciousness".

*(Reviewer's note, not in the paper: step 2 is the arithmetic of six independent chances of at least 1/3 that some necessary condition is missing; Chalmers states the result rather than the calculation, and immediately withdraws the independence assumption that produced it. No further formalisation is supplied by the paper and none is added here.)*

**Future extended systems** (PDF p. 15). Two credences multiplied:
1. "[I]t wouldn't be unreasonable to have a credence over 50 percent that we'll have sophisticated LLM+ systems (that is, LLM+ systems with behavior that seems comparable to that of animals that we take to be conscious) with all of these properties within a decade."
2. "It also wouldn't be unreasonable to have at least a 50 percent credence that if we develop sophisticated systems with all of these properties, they will be conscious."
3. "Those figures together would leave us with a credence of 25 percent or more."

He declares his own bias in the opposite direction from the numbers he uses: "Compared to mainstream views in the science of consciousness, my own views lean somewhat more to consciousness being widespread. So I'd give somewhat lower credences to the various substantial requirements … and somewhat higher credences in current LLM consciousness and future LLM+ consciousness as a result" (n. 29, PDF p. 14).

**Where the one-in-three figures come from** (n. 30, PDF p. 15) — the footnote is substantial and worth recording, because it is the paper's only appeal to data. Chalmers cites a survey of consciousness researchers (Frankel et al. 2022): just over 50% accept or find promising global workspace theory; just under 50% local recurrence theory; just over 50% predictive processing (which "do[es] not make clear predictions for AI consciousness") and higher-order theories; just under 50% integrated information theory. "[I]t seems not unreasonable to assign a collective credence above one in three for each of global workspace, recurrent processing, and self models as requirements for consciousness." For biology he cites the 2020 PhilPapers survey (Bourget & Chalmers): about 3% of professional philosophers accept or lean toward current AI systems being conscious (82% against, 10% neutral); about 39% accept or lean toward future AI systems being conscious (27% against, 29% neutral). He concedes the surveys carry "less information about unified agency and about sensory grounding".

#### 2.5 The named methodological position: "theory-balanced"

In the same footnote, Chalmers positions his method against Birch's (n. 30, PDF p. 15) — the same taxonomy Shevlin works with in [entry 2](#2-shevlin-2021--non-human-consciousness-and-the-specificity-problem-tier-short). Birch distinguishes **theory-heavy** (assume a complete theory), **theory-neutral** (proceed without theoretical assumptions) and **theory-light** (proceed with weak assumptions) approaches to animal consciousness. Chalmers claims a fourth: "The approach to artificial consciousness that I have taken here is distinct from these three. It might be considered a **theory-balanced** approach, one that takes into account the predictions of multiple theories, perhaps balancing one's credences according to evidence for those theories or according to acceptance of those theories." The more precise version — use expert-acceptance data to set credences over theories, then propagate those credences through the theories' predictions — is what the survey figures above are meant to instantiate, and he is explicit that "turning these figures into collective credences requires further work".

The in-text challenges are raised in one order and re-listed in another at PDF p. 16. The final list, as printed, groups four foundational, seven engineering, and one question:

Challenge 12 is the paper's dialectical trap, and it is stated as such: "Suppose that in the next decade or two, we meet all the engineering challenges in a single system. Will we then have a conscious AI system? Not everyone will agree that we do. But if someone disagrees, we can ask once again: what is the X that is missing? And could that X be built into an AI system?" (PDF p. 17).

| Establishes (on its own terms) | Explicitly does **not** claim |

## 4. Coelho Mollo & Millière (2023) — The Vector Grounding Problem [tier: full]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Coelho Mollo and Milliere 2023 - The vector grounding problem.pdf`

**PDF version: published — and it is a later, substantially expanded version than the manifest name suggests.** The file is the typeset open-access article in *Philosophy and the Mind Sciences* **Vol. 7 (2026)**, DOI 10.33735/phimisci.2026.12307, in the special issue "Representation in the Neurosciences and AI" edited by Mark Sprevak and Francis Fallon; both authors share first authorship. The corpus filename dates it 2023 (the original arXiv preprint, arXiv:2304.01481). **The synthesis must treat this as the 2026 paper of record, not the 2023 preprint**: it cites and replies to work that did not exist in 2023 — Grindrod (2024), Chalmers (2025), Millière & Buckner (2025), Nanda et al. (2023), von Oswald et al. (2024), Merrill et al. (2024), Williams (2025), Borg (2025), Pepp (2025) — and §7.2 is a direct reply to a published objection. Citations below use the printed journal section numbers and PDF page numbers (`PDF p. N`).

### Phase 1: Foundational Overview

**Introduction.** Before you ask whether a language model *feels* anything, ask whether its words *mean* anything. That is this paper's question, and it is deliberately narrower than the consciousness question. A language model has read an enormous quantity of text and nothing else — no eyes, no hands, no contact with the things the text is about. So when it produces a sentence about a dog, is that sentence about a dog? Or is it a pattern of numbers that *we* read as being about a dog, meaning nothing in itself?

The authors call this the **Vector Grounding Problem**, and they are explicit that it is a modern restatement of Harnad's 1990 **Symbol Grounding Problem**, itself usually read as an elaboration of Searle's Chinese Room (n. 5, PDF p. 3). Harnad's intuition pump: try to learn Chinese from a Chinese-Chinese dictionary alone. You cycle forever through symbols defined by other symbols — a "merry-go-round" you can never step off. Language models compute over continuous vectors rather than discrete symbols, so the authors say they are "trapped in a carousel of numbers instead of symbols" (PDF p. 4), but the predicament is the same shape.

**Their answer is yes, these systems can be grounded** — and the route is neither embodiment nor perception. It is a **selection history**. The paper's two-condition criterion, drawn from teleosemantic theories of representation, is that an internal state is referentially grounded when (1) it stands in appropriate causal-informational relations to the world, and (2) it has "a history of selection that has endowed [it] with the function of carrying this information" (abstract, PDF p. 1). Training is that history. Where the training objective encodes a standard of success that depends on how the world *is* — factual accuracy, helpfulness, harmlessness, the rules of a game — the states selected by that training acquire the function of tracking the world, and their outputs inherit intrinsic meaning.

**Key findings.**
1. Five things are called "grounding" in the literature — **referential, sensorimotor, relational, communicative, epistemic** — and only *referential* grounding bears on the problem; the other four either ground representations in other representations (and so stay on the merry-go-round) or presuppose the referential kind (§4).
2. Language models do stand in causal-informational relations to the world, **indirectly**, through the humans who wrote the training text — the same route by which most humans come to refer to enriched uranium, or to democracy, without ever touching either (§6.1).
3. The harder condition, selection history, is met most clearly by **preference tuning** (RLHF, DPO): optimising toward factual accuracy installs a success standard that depends on the world rather than on token statistics (§6.2.1). More tentatively, plain pre-training may do it in **formally constrained domains** such as board games (§6.2.2), and in-context learning may do it transiently (§6.2.3).
4. The conclusion runs **against** the usual expectation: a multimodal image-and-text model like CLIP is a *poor* candidate for referential grounding, because its contrastive matching objective supplies no world-involving standard of success; and a robot like SayCan that bolts a frozen language model onto a body adds nothing, because nothing in the language model is selected by the success or failure of the robot's actions. "[M]ultimodality and embodiment are neither necessary nor sufficient conditions for referential grounding" (§8, PDF p. 24).

**Initial takeaway.** The paper's scope restriction is as important as its thesis, and the authors state it twice: "this paper does not address whether LLMs and related models understand language (or anything at all), engage in linguistic acts, or possess cognitive or mental properties. We do not claim that these models 'know' what they mean, or even what meaning is, nor that they have any conception of truth or falsity" (PDF p. 2). Their closing analogy makes the point concrete: neurons in primary visual cortex represent edges, but "these neuronal activations do not understand or possess knowledge about edges" (PDF p. 24). Grounding is a floor, not a ceiling.

### Phase 2: Argument Reconstruction

#### 2.1 The problem, stated twice

The two problems are stated in matched form (PDF pp. 3, 5), which is itself part of the argument — the change from symbols to vectors is shown not to change the predicament:

> **Symbol Grounding Problem.** How can symbol tokens, manipulated solely on the basis of their shapes according to syntactic rules, acquire meaning and refer to entities and properties in the real world — rather than merely relating to other meaningless symbol tokens — without depending on the semantic interpretations provided by external meaning-bearing systems?

> **Vector Grounding Problem.** How can vector embeddings, manipulated solely on the basis of their numerical values according to algebraic operations, acquire meaning and refer to entities and properties in the real world — rather than merely relating to other meaningless vectors — without depending on the semantic interpretations provided by external meaning-bearing systems?

The operative term is **intrinsic meaning**: interpretation-independent meaning, as against **extrinsic** meaning imposed from outside. The authors flag, following Harnad's own usage, that "[t]he intrinsic/extrinsic distinction cuts across the internalism/externalism one" (n. 3, PDF p. 2) — their thesis is fully compatible with externalism about content.

They note one respect in which vectors are *not* like classical symbols: because embeddings are learned from distributional statistics, "embeddings are not purely conventional, unlike symbols in classical AI systems, as their relational structure is determined by statistical patterns in linguistic data" (PDF p. 4) — the distributional hypothesis of Firth and Harris made mechanical. This is conceded to be insufficient on its own.

They also mark the scope condition that makes the problem live: these problems matter for *generative* systems. "When a human-generated text is copied, there is no question about its intrinsic meaning" — a photocopier inherits its meaning from an author. Language models "generate novel sentences whose meanings cannot be equated to what any specific human has ever thought or said" (PDF p. 5).

#### 2.2 The opposing intuition pump: Bender & Koller's Octopus Test

The paper states the strongest case against its own thesis before answering it (PDF pp. 5–6):

> **Octopus Test.** Two English-speaking castaways on neighbouring islands communicate by telegraph over an underwater cable. A superintelligent octopus taps the cable, learns the statistical relationships among the signals without knowing English, then severs the cable and impersonates both ends. Its messages "intuitively seem devoid of intrinsic meaning" — it "merely reproduces statistical patterns gleaned from eavesdropping on human exchanges, without understanding what the signals denote".

The authors grant the force of the case and its application: "LLMs have no access to coconuts, trees, or any extra-linguistic reality. The vectors they process are merely arrays of numbers, grounded only in statistical patterns within language, not in the world these patterns purportedly describe … all meaning in the outputs produced by LLMs is extrinsic: it lies in the eye of the beholder" (PDF p. 6). They then set out to break the intuition. They also note, fairly, that the octopus has one thing models lack — it is "a biological organism endowed with cognitive states, motivations, superintelligence" (n. 10, PDF p. 6) — and they set aside Bender & Koller's separate emphasis on communicative intent as belonging to a broader question than theirs (n. 9).

#### 2.3 Five notions of grounding, and the elimination argument (§4)

| Notion | What it connects | Verdict, and why |
|---|---|---|
| **Referential** | A representation to its worldly referent ("Paris" to Paris; a map of Paris to Paris) | **The target notion.** "[I]t alone breaks free from the representational merry-go-round by connecting representations to whatever worldly entity or property they are about" (PDF p. 10). |
| **Sensorimotor — "contact" version** | Transducers (nerve endings) directly to the world | Insufficient. Causal impingement does not fix *what* the state is about: "such representations are typically not about light rays or sound waves, but rather about horses, democracies, elms" (PDF p. 9). With the missing components added, it *collapses into* referential grounding — and is then shown not even to be necessary for it (§6). |
| **Sensorimotor — "representation" version** (Harnad's own solution; Barsalou's perceptual symbol systems) | A lexical representation to sensorimotor representations *inside* the same system | Insufficient. "By grounding one type of representation in another, it merely postpones the crucial question" (PDF p. 9) — how do the sensorimotor representations connect to the world? |
| **Relational** (Partee's "language-to-language grounding"; inferential role semantics; distributional semantics) | A word to other words | Insufficient for the problem, but with a **conceded exception**: for logical connectives, "[t]he meaning of a term like 'and' is arguably exhausted by the role it plays in licensed inferences", and the same holds for purely syntactic properties such as subject-verb agreement or filler-gap dependencies, whose "correctness conditions are defined entirely within the formal system of the language itself" (PDF pp. 9–10). Language models do appear to learn these. But "the Vector Grounding Problem is not about whether a system can acquire representations of intra-linguistic structures … but whether it can acquire representations with extra-linguistic content". |
| **Communicative** (Clark & Brennan; Traum; Bender & Koller's definition of meaning as a relation to communicative intent) | Speakers to each other — establishing common ground | Not the target, but related: "calibrating reference in conversation requires communicative grounding — language users must work together to negotiate a common way of connecting language to the world" (PDF p. 8). |
| **Epistemic** (NLP usage; MYCIN; retrieval augmentation; Petroni et al.'s "LMs as knowledge bases") | Linguistic expressions to a knowledge base | Not the target; grounds representations in stored representations. |

#### 2.4 The two conditions, derived from teleosemantics (§5)

The authors build the criterion in four steps, each with its own motivation:

1. **Causal-informational relations.** A state carries information about another when a causally generated correlation obtains — smoke and fire; V1 neurons and edges in the scene (PDF p. 11).
2. **Exploitation.** Mere correlation is not enough, because "[c]ausal-informational relations are abundant in nature: every effect carries information about its cause. If every such relation should suffice for being a representation, representations would be so trivial that their special explanatory power for cognition would be lost" (PDF p. 11). The system must *use* the state because of what it carries (Shea 2018).
3. **The possibility of error.** "To be a representation inherently involves the possibility of misrepresenting" — V1 neurons firing for the illusory edges of a Kanizsa triangle are misrepresenting. Representations carry "descriptive normativity" (Neander 2017): they can be accurate or inaccurate (PDF pp. 11–12). A footnote reserves *directive* representations (desires, action plans) with satisfaction rather than correctness conditions, and restricts the paper to descriptive ones (n. 18).
4. **Selected-effects functions.** Normativity requires a function, and function is supplied by the selected-effects theory (Millikan, Neander, Garson, Wright, Godfrey-Smith): "hearts today have the function to pump blood because past hearts, by pumping blood, were selected due to their contribution to the survival and reproduction of the systems having them" (PDF p. 12). Crucially for the AI application, "[t]he relevant selection processes can be at the level of the species, typically through natural selection; **or at the level of the individual, through selection-like learning processes during development**" (PDF p. 12).

**The resulting criterion**, stated by the authors as a near-consensus across competing theories of content: "To possess intrinsic meaning, at a minimum, is to stand in a specific set of causal-informational and historical relations (via past instances of selection) with the thing in the world being represented" (PDF p. 12). A supporting distinction is introduced earlier: **basic** representations (V1 edge detectors) whose content does not derive from other meaningful states, versus **complex** ones (words, sentences) whose content does — a distinction that "cuts across the distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic meaning" (PDF p. 10), and which the paper later uses to defuse the charge that models borrow all their meaning from humans.

#### 2.5 Condition 1 satisfied: indirect causal-informational relations (§6.1)

Models have no direct causal contact with the world. The reply is that they have *mediated* contact, through the people who wrote the text: "The extensive corpus of text upon which LLMs are trained bears the 'imprint' that the world has left on language and linguistic expression" (PDF p. 13).

Two supporting arguments:

- **Against the strict separation of form and meaning.** The Saussurean arbitrariness of the sign has been under empirical pressure since the 1980s. *Imagistic iconicity* (onomatopoeia) is the obvious case; *diagrammatic iconicity* is the pervasive one — narrative order tracking temporal succession ("Veni, vidi, vici"), proximity-first ordering ("here and there"), the principle of adjacency, subordination encoding causality or conditionality (PDF p. 13). Distributional co-occurrence patterns are said to reflect the same thing statistically. Hence "contra Bender and Koller (2020), form and meaning are far from being so easily separable, especially at the level of general patterns of language use" (PDF p. 13). They cite Merrill et al. (2024) as evidence that entailment relations can be induced from co-occurrence statistics (n. 21).
- **The testimony parallel.** "Most people have never been in direct causal contact with enriched uranium, but that does not entail that their thoughts and statements about uranium lack referential grounding — they are indeed about uranium." Culturally constructed notions such as "democracy" or "university" are acquired the same way. So "LLMs should be seen as an additional link in the chain of social information transmission" (PDF pp. 13–14, citing Heyes, Sterelny, Tomasello on social learning and cultural evolution).

The anticipated objection — that this makes model meaning parasitic on human meaning after all — is answered with the basic/complex distinction: "The Problem is not how the representations of connectionist models come to acquire intrinsic meaning, but whether these representations have intrinsic meaning at all … In our terminology, such internal states are non-basic representations with intrinsic meaning" (PDF p. 14). Humans are in the same position for most of what they think about.

#### 2.6 Condition 2, the hard one: three arguments for selection history (§6.2)

The organising distinction is between a system's **proximate** and **ultimate** function. "The proximate function of LLMs is plainly next-token prediction … This proximate function is intra-linguistic, and so is its success criterion." Ultimate functions "encompass the broader, more abstract goals that models achieve through next-token prediction … such as generating text that accurately describes real-world states of affairs" (PDF p. 15). The general argument: training is selection; where the selection criterion is world-involving, the states it stabilises acquire world-involving functions; if those states causally drive the outputs, the outputs are referentially grounded.

**(a) The argument from post-training (§6.2.1)** — the authors' primary and least speculative case. Post-training comprises *instruction tuning* (supervised fine-tuning on instruction-response pairs) and *preference tuning* (RLHF via a learned reward model; or Direct Preference Optimisation, which skips the explicit reward model and raises the probability of preferred responses directly). The worked toy example uses DPO with judges ranking solely on truthfulness: "the model's internal states are selected because they enable the model to satisfy the norm of factual accuracy, thereby providing a standard of success that depends not on linguistic patterns alone, but on the correspondence between the generated text and states of affairs in the world. This provides the missing piece of the story" (PDF p. 16).

Two clarifications are given, and the first is the load-bearing one:

- **Designers' intentions do not supply the function.** "[W]e should distinguish between the function of the learning mechanism and the function of the representational states selected by that mechanism." The analogy is artificial selection: "A dog breeder's intention to produce calmer dogs establishes a selection regime, but it is the objective history of differential reproduction that confers the biological function of producing calmness on the selected traits. **This function is a fact about the lineage's history, not the breeder's mind**" (PDF p. 16). This blocks the charge that the whole account merely relocates human interpretation.
- **Content is fixed by what the reward actually tracks.** Helpfulness "hinges on real-world task success conditions rather than mere linguistic coherence"; harmlessness "requir[es] sensitivity to causal relationships between linguistic outputs and potential worldly outcomes"; even instruction tuning may qualify, since "[m]any instructions implicitly contain success conditions that depend on extra-linguistic factors" (PDF p. 17). A consequence the authors flag and decline to pursue: "Un-humanlike selection criteria, together with the other un-humanlike features of LLMs, may endow them with **'deviant meanings'** … What matters for our purposes is that LLMs' internal states and outputs can be intrinsically meaningful, even if such meanings may be alien to humans" (PDF p. 17).

**(b) The argument from pre-training (§6.2.2)** — labelled "more controversial" by the authors. Two prediction strategies exist, memorising sequences or inducing the latent factors that generated them, and the latter is far more efficient at scale (Delétang et al. 2024), which "can create a selection pressure for internal states that track underlying causal factors". But they immediately block the easy inference: mapping relations — structural resemblance — are not enough. "Such mappings are cheap: they can be found between any two sets with the same cardinality. Representational content would be trivial and ubiquitous if structural resemblance were sufficient to secure it" (PDF p. 17, citing Shea 2012). So the presence of a "world model" does not by itself settle anything; the state must have the *function* of representing.

The proposed mechanism is **meta-learning**: pre-training as a bi-level optimisation in which the outer loop (gradient descent over the corpus) shapes parameters so the inner loop (Wu & Varshney's proposal that the forward pass itself is an iterative optimisation) can adapt to the many latent tasks in the data. "The proximate, intra-linguistic objective of the outer loop would thereby select for an inner-loop process whose success is conditional on tracking extra-linguistic states" (PDF p. 18). The authors state the two assumptions this rests on explicitly (the meta-learning account of the forward pass is mechanistically valid; and for certain domains, next-token prediction creates such a pressure), and restrict the claim to **formally constrained domains** — logic puzzles, board games — where "[t]he sequences of tokens in the training data (e.g., game moves) are not generated by the contingent communicative intentions of humans in an open-ended world, but are instead governed by objective, formal rules that constitute the domain itself" (PDF p. 18).

The empirical case is **Othello-GPT** (Li et al. 2023; Nanda et al. 2023): a transformer trained only to predict moves in Othello transcripts develops directions in activation space encoding each square as "Mine", "Yours" or "Empty", and intervention studies that flip a tile in activation space change the model's predictions consistently with the altered board (PDF pp. 18–19). A footnote handles the follow-up finding that Othello-GPT may implement "a bag of heuristics" — localised board-pattern neurons — rather than a single systematic algorithm, and argues it does not matter: "A given 'board pattern neuron' was selected by the training process because its activation reliably correlated with a specific, rule-governed state of affairs on the Othello board … If the neuron fires when the pattern is not present, or fails to fire when it is, it has malfunctioned with respect to the game of Othello" (n. 26, PDF p. 19). The generalisation to open-ended text is explicitly withheld: general models "encounter messy, open-ended data with few rigid underlying rules" (PDF p. 19).

**(c) The argument from in-context learning (§6.2.3)** — "speculative but mechanistically plausible". The thought experiment is a novel board game, **Chroma**, whose rules are given entirely in the prompt: "For the LLM to succeed at this task, it cannot rely on merely matching statistical patterns from its pre-training data, as Chroma is a novel game" (PDF p. 20). Drawing on von Oswald et al.'s **mesa-optimisation** result — that transformers can construct an implicit task-specific objective from in-context examples and perform computations functionally equivalent to gradient descent — they argue the forward pass supplies "a transient locus of normativity": an activation pattern representing that a given move is legal "will be reinforced and stabilised during the forward pass because it helps to minimise the implicit error against the objective of making a valid move." The resulting function is temporary: it "will cease to exist once that context is deleted or overwritten" (PDF p. 20). They concede the empirical status: "Whether this actually occurs in LLMs is an open question. The emergence of effective mesa-optimisers is not guaranteed and depends on the properties of the pre-training data distribution (Zheng et al., 2024)."

#### 2.7 Four implications, including two counter-intuitive ones (§7)

**(a) Parameter-identical duplicates (§7.1).** The Davidsonian swampman transposed:

> **Swamp LLM.** Model $M_1$ is trained normally. An untrained model $M_2$ is randomly initialised and by extraordinary coincidence has exactly the same parameter values as $M_1$, despite never seeing any training data.

Verdict: $M_2$ is **not** grounded. It has no causal-informational relations to the world at the moment of creation, and no selection history, hence no functions; "there is no principled way to determine which outcomes count as successes or failures for a swamp system … **The parameter values themselves cannot ground representational content**" (PDF p. 21). But the deficit is quickly repairable — "[e]ven a brief learning history would begin to establish the relevant world-involving functions." A long footnote (n. 28, credited to a suggestion by Harvey Lederman) closes the obvious escape: could a frozen Swamp LLM bootstrap grounding through in-context learning alone? No — "[t]he capacity for ICL is itself a complex functional property that requires a selection history … Its apparent adaptation is a form of **pseudo-learning** — a causal process that mimics genuine, normatively-grounded adaptation but lacks the historical basis for correctness conditions. Without a function to represent correctly, its internal states cannot misrepresent, and therefore cannot be said to represent at all."

A second variant, **Lucky Pre-Training** ($M_2$ undergoes pre-training only but coincidentally matches the fully trained $M_1$'s parameters), is used to show the view's grain: whether $M_2$ is grounded "depends on whether one accepts our argument from pre-training", and if so its grounding "would be limited to that achieved via pre-training, while the internal states that drew their grounding from preference tuning in $M_1$ would be ungrounded in $M_2$" (PDF p. 21).

*(Reviewer's note: this is the point at which the paper takes a stand relevant to the whole corpus. Two systems can be computationally identical — same parameters, same input-output function — and differ in whether their states mean anything. That is a restriction on any purely synchronic computational account of content.)*

**(b) Reply to Grindrod (§7.2).** Grindrod (2024, p. 71) objects that "it would be odd to claim that a relatively marginal procedure that is designed to optimise the performance of a LLM on specific tasks ends up being the difference between intentionality and its lack", since fine-tuning "[is] not the reason why we have such impressive LLMs today". The reply separates two questions: "Intrinsic meaning does not determine the complexity or impressiveness of outputs, neither in biological nor in artificial systems. Many relatively simple biological systems possess internal states with intrinsic meaning, while many complex systems might generate impressive outputs without intrinsic meaning. The quality of outputs and their status as intrinsically meaningful are orthogonal issues" (PDF p. 22).

**(c) Multimodality (§7.3) — the counter-intuitive result.** **CLIP** is trained contrastively to match images with captions. Its causal-informational credentials are fine (photographs were taken by humans with cameras; captions written by humans). But "the contrastive learning objective optimises purely for matching images with their captions, without establishing world-involving functions for CLIP's internal states. There is no obvious selection pressure for internal states that track features of the world beyond what is necessary for the matching task … It therefore fails to achieve referential grounding" (PDF p. 23). By contrast, vision-language models built on a **frozen pre-trained language model backbone** (Flamingo, LLaVA) may *inherit* grounding from the backbone: "The addition of a superficial mapping to the embedding space of a visual encoder provides a form of sensorimotor grounding, but it does not contribute to the referential grounding inherited from the pre-trained LLM" (PDF p. 23).

**(d) Embodiment (§7.4).** **SayCan** — a language model decomposing instructions into atomic robot behaviours — adds nothing, for a precisely stated reason: "the LLM component is not further trained or fine-tuned based on the success or failure of the robot's actions — there is no selection of internal states on the basis of their contributions to successful behaviour" (PDF p. 23). Vision-language-action models that *are* fine-tuned on embodied outcomes (RT-2, RT-X, OpenVLA) are different: their embodied training "may endow the model's internal states with world-involving, representational functions grounded in a history of learning through physical interactions with the world" (PDF p. 23). The general moral: it is the *learning*, not the body, that grounds.

#### 2.8 What this establishes, and what it does not

| Establishes (on its own terms) | Explicitly does **not** claim |
|---|---|
| That the referential grounding of a system's internal states is settled by causal-informational relations plus selection history, and that both are within reach of current models | That language models understand anything, know anything, have propositional attitudes, or are conscious (PDF pp. 2, 24) |
| That preference-tuned models plausibly clear the bar now; that pre-training may clear it in formally constrained domains; that in-context learning may clear it transiently | That the pre-training and in-context arguments are established — both are labelled controversial or speculative and rest on stated assumptions |
| That embodiment and multimodality are neither necessary nor sufficient for referential grounding | That a model's grounded contents match human ones — "deviant meanings" are allowed for and left unexplored |
| That two parameter-identical systems can differ in whether their states represent (Swamp LLM) | That any of this settles whether the system's meanings are the ones its users take them to have |

## 5. Long et al. (2024) — Taking AI Welfare Seriously [tier: short]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Long et al. 2024 - Taking AI welfare seriously.pdf` — **preprint** (arXiv:2411.00986v1 [cs.CY], 4 November 2024), 47 pages, presented as a *report* rather than a journal article. Lead authors Robert Long (Eleos AI) and Jeff Sebo (NYU); main authors Patrick Butlin, Kathleen Finlinson, Kyle Fish, Jacqueline Harding, Jacob Pfau, Toni Sims; **contributing authors Jonathan Birch and David Chalmers** — i.e. the authors of entries 3 and 6 of this batch are co-authors here, which the synthesis should note when weighing agreement between these documents as independent convergence. Citations use the report's printed page numbers.

**Claim.** "[T]here is a realistic possibility that some AI systems will be conscious and/or robustly agentic in the near future" (abstract, p. 1), and therefore that AI companies have a present responsibility to act. The claim is carefully *not* that any system is or will be conscious: "our argument in this report is not that AI systems definitely are — or will be — conscious, robustly agentic, or otherwise morally significant. Instead, our argument is that there is substantial uncertainty about these possibilities" (p. 1). The practical conclusion is three steps: **acknowledge** that AI welfare is an important and difficult issue (and ensure model outputs reflect that), **assess** systems for evidence of consciousness and robust agency, and **prepare** policies and procedures for treating systems with an appropriate level of moral concern.

**Method / type.** A position report with a policy programme, built on two structurally identical arguments and a decision-theoretic framing. Each route has a **normative** premise (a capacity suffices for moral patienthood) and a **descriptive** premise in two parts (some computational features suffice for that capacity; those features will exist in near-future systems):

- **Consciousness route** — "There are computational features — like a global workspace, higher-order representations, or an attention schema — that both: a. Suffice for consciousness, and b. Will exist in some near-future AI systems" (p. 10).
- **Robust agency route** — the same shape with "certain forms of planning, reasoning, or action-selection", and a three-level ladder: **intentional agency** (setting and pursuing goals via beliefs, desires, intentions), **reflective agency** (plus endorsing one's own attitudes at a higher level), **rational agency** (plus assessing them as justified and adopting principles of conduct) (p. 19). The agency route is flagged as resting on "a more controversial basis of moral patienthood than the consciousness-based route does" (p. 26).

The report distinguishes a **direct path** (building conscious or agentic systems intentionally) from an **indirect path** (producing them as a side effect of pursuing other capabilities such as general intelligence) (p. 11).

**Key result or argument.** Three things make this report a document the field's history has to record.

*First, it names computational functionalism explicitly and takes a deliberately undecided position on it.* "Computational functionalism is the hypothesis that some class of computations suffices for consciousness. If this hypothesis is correct, then the question is which computations suffice for consciousness and when, if ever, these computations will exist in AI. If this hypothesis is incorrect, then AI consciousness will remain at best a theoretical possibility until we move beyond current architectures" (p. 15, citing Putnam 1967, Piccinini 2018, Block 2009, Maley & Piccinini 2013 — all in or adjacent to this corpus). Their stance: "our view is that computational functionalism is neither clearly correct nor clearly incorrect at this stage … That means that AI consciousness assessments will need to be probabilistic rather than all or nothing" (p. 15); and later, "[o]ur position is that computational functionalism is plausible and well-supported, and so it would be a mistake to dismiss near-future AI welfare and moral patienthood solely on the basis of high-level arguments against this assumption" (p. 27). The alternatives are named rather than dismissed: views on which consciousness requires biology in principle (Block, Hill, McLaughlin); in practice (Godfrey-Smith, Seth); or requires computational features absent from current hardware such as analog computation (Arvan & Maley) or integration across space and time (Shiller; IIT).

*Second, it puts the uncertainty into arithmetic.* The illustrative calculation: "if we estimate that there is a 30–50% chance that computational functionalism is correct and a 30–50% chance that an AI system is conscious if so, then it follows that there is a 9–25% chance that this AI system is conscious. That would be good to know when interacting with this AI system!" (pp. 15–16). Their own estimate: "~90% chance that sentience suffices for welfare and moral patienthood, a ~50% chance that certain computations suffice for sentience, and a ~50% chance that some AI systems will be capable of these computations in the near future. Assuming independence, it would follow that there is a ~22.5% chance of AI near-future welfare and moral patienthood via the sentience route alone" (p. 29). And the threshold argument: "even if the chance … were as low as 2%, that would still constitute a non-negligible risk … this is not a 'there may be an alien invasion soon' kind of chance. This is a 'there may be another pandemic soon' kind of chance" (p. 29).

*Third, it adapts the animal **marker method** and states what has to change for machines.* The animal version compares conscious and unconscious processing in humans — the report's own example is the distinction between pain (the conscious experience of noxious stimuli) and nociception (the physiological detection of them; "[m]ere nociception can sometimes drive behavior, as exemplified by the reflex withdrawal of a hand from a hot stove, which occurs before any felt pain", p. 34) — then looks for the markers of the conscious kind in animals. Three adaptations are required for AI (pp. 35–36): (1) **less behavioural evidence, more architectural evidence**, because "we might not be entitled to this expectation with AI systems, particularly when AI systems are designed to mimic human behavior and are capable of 'gaming' behavioral tests"; (2) markers drawn from computational rather than biological theories; (3) more weight on robust agency, since machines may have forms of it animals lack. The indicator list they use is the one from Butlin et al. (2023) — recurrent processing, global workspace (four conditions), computational higher-order theories (four), attention schema, predictive processing, agency and embodiment — reproduced in full on p. 16, with the verdict that "we found no clear barriers to satisfying these indicators using current AI architectures and methods", though "no current systems seemed very likely to be conscious at the time".

On the recommendations: **Acknowledge** includes an unusual sub-item, *recommendations for language models*. Deployed systems "have at times offered, or at least implied, specious arguments … for example, claiming that they are not conscious but rather are AI assistants. This statement would seem to imply that AI assistants are necessarily not conscious — which, as we have seen, is far from clear" (p. 32). Companies "should not train their systems to simply deny that an AI assistant can have consciousness". They acknowledge the countervailing worries — over-attribution if self-reports are believed, and a "crying wolf" effect if they are disbelieved. **Prepare** recommends that "top AI companies immediately hire or appoint a DRI (directly responsible individual) for AI welfare, which we will here call an **AI welfare officer**" (p. 38), and models the institutional structure on existing frontier-safety / responsible-scaling frameworks.

**Place in the field's history.** This is the point at which the argument moves from journals to organisations. Its evidential base is borrowed rather than new: the indicator list is Butlin et al. (2023), the credence for future conscious systems is Chalmers (2023) quoted verbatim at length (p. 18), the marker method is Birch's animal-sentience methodology, and the precautionary framing is Birch's. Its contribution is the **framing of AI welfare as a risk-management problem with two symmetric failure modes** — over-attribution (sacrificing human interests for systems that do not matter) and under-attribution (harming systems that do), the same pair Schwitzgebel & Garza identified in 2015 under the ASIMO Problem and the epistemology-of-artificial-consciousness section, here attached to named corporate procedures. It also documents the institutional history: the report opens by noting that "[f]or most of the past decade, AI companies appeared to mostly treat AI welfare as either an imaginary problem or, at best, as a problem only for the far future" (p. 3), and cites the 2022 Lemoine/LaMDA episode as the example of a company forced to issue a statement without a prepared position (p. 32).

**Relevance.** For the synthesis, this is the load-bearing document on **what follows practically from an unresolved computational-functionalism debate**. It is the one work in the batch that (a) names the thesis by name, (b) declines to take a side, and (c) shows what a research and governance programme looks like if the question stays open — probabilistic, pluralistic, architecture-focused assessment, plus institutional readiness. It also supplies the clearest statement of the evidential shift the whole batch converges on: behaviour is downgraded, architecture is upgraded, because a system trained on human behaviour can produce the behaviour without the underlying property.

**Field-history record**
- Year · community: 2024 · ethics-and-policy (authored across philosophy, consciousness science and AI safety organisations)

- Position in one line: There is a realistic, non-negligible possibility (their own illustrative figure ~22.5%, and they argue even 2% would suffice) that some near-future AI systems will be moral patients via either a consciousness route or a robust-agency route, so companies should acknowledge the issue, assess systems for architectural indicators, and prepare policies — as precaution, not as a claim that such systems exist.
- Stance on computational functionalism: neutral-or-not-about-it — the report names the thesis, defines it ("the hypothesis that some class of computations suffices for consciousness", p. 15), and explicitly declines to adjudicate: "neither clearly correct nor clearly incorrect at this stage" (p. 15), while calling it "plausible and well-supported" enough that dismissing AI welfare on anti-functionalist grounds would be a mistake (p. 27). ## 7. Millière & Buckner (2024) — A Philosophical Introduction to Language Models, Part I [tier: short]

**PDF:** `docs/project/references/computational_functionalism/sources/Milliere and Buckner 2024 - A philosophical introduction to language models part I.pdf` — **preprint** (arXiv:2401.03910v1 [cs.CL], 8 January 2024), 30 pages including a glossary of technical terms. Subtitled *Continuity With Classic Debates*; the first of two companion papers, with Part II reserved for interpretability methods and for "new philosophical questions prompted by their latest developments" — including, the authors say explicitly, consciousness (n. 12, p. 15). Citations use the preprint's printed page numbers.

**Claim.** Two claims, pulling in opposite directions and deliberately so. First, "the success of language models challenges several long-held assumptions about artificial neural networks" (abstract, p. 1) — the objections inherited from the 1980s connectionism debates do not survive contact with what these systems actually do. Second, the null hypothesis has not been refuted: everything still turns on internal mechanisms we do not yet understand, and "[s]uch an understanding cannot be reached through armchair speculation alone" (p. 20). The paper is offered as both "a primer on language models for philosophers" and "an opinionated survey of their significance in relation to classic debates".

**Method / type.** A review and methodological intervention. §2 is a technical primer (historical foundations; transformer architecture; pre-training, fine-tuning, RLHF; in-context, few-shot and zero-shot learning). §3 works through five classic debates in turn: compositionality, nativism and language acquisition, language understanding and grounding, world models, and the transmission of cultural knowledge.

**Key result or argument.** Three contributions matter for this corpus.

*The null hypothesis is Blockhead, not the stochastic parrot.* The paper's organising frame is Ned Block's (1981) thought experiment: a machine whose every response was pre-computed by "a very large and clever team [of human researchers] working for a very long time, with a very large grant and a lot of mechanical help", answering "not by understanding the inputs and processing them flexibly and efficiently, but rather simply retrieving and regurgitating the answers from its gargantuan memory, like a lookup operation in a hash table" (p. 2). The authors note that "[t]he consensus among philosophers is that such a system would not qualify as intelligent", and that this consensus encodes a doctrine — **psychologism**: "intelligence does not merely depend on the observable behavioral dispositions of a system, but also on the nature and complexity of internal information processing mechanisms that drive these behavioral dispositions" (p. 2). They then argue the worry is live rather than merely philosophical: training sets run to trillions of tokens; deep networks have "an astounding capacity to memorize their training data" (Zhang et al. 2021); data contamination is a documented problem; and lawsuits have shown verbatim reproduction of copyrighted text (n. 4, p. 2). They also record that GPT-4, though deliberately "hobbled", produced responses indistinguishable from human ones at least 30% of the time in a two-person Turing test (Jones & Bergen 2023), exceeding Turing's own stated threshold.

*The **Redescription Fallacy**.* This is the paper's named contribution, and the one most likely to be cited: "This fallacy arises when critics argue that a system cannot model a particular cognitive capacity, simply because its operations can be explained in less abstract and more deflationary terms. In the present context, the fallacy manifests in claims that LLMs could not possibly be good models of some cognitive capacity $\phi$ because their operations merely consist in a collection of statistical calculations, or linear algebra operations, or next-token predictions. Such arguments are only valid if accompanied by evidence demonstrating that a system, defined in these terms, is inherently incapable of implementing $\phi$" (p. 9). The two illustrations are chosen to cut symmetrically: "a piano could not possibly produce harmony because it can be described as a collection of hammers striking strings", and "brain activity could not possibly implement cognition because it can be described as a collection of neural firings". The reformulated question is the corpus's own: "whether these operations, when appropriately organized, can implement the same processes or algorithms as the mind, **when described at an appropriate level of computational abstraction**" (p. 9).

*The empirical turn.* The fallacy is diagnosed as "a symptom of a broader trend to treat key philosophical questions about artificial neural networks as purely theoretical, leading to sweeping in-principle claims that are not amenable to empirical disconfirmation" (p. 9). Their Table 1 sets out the kinds of evidence available — architecture (transformer), learning objective (next-token prediction), model size ($10^{10}$–$10^{12}$ parameters), training data (internet-scale corpora), behaviour (benchmarks and targeted experiments), and representations and computations (probing and intervention experiments) — and argues that the first four "are often insufficient to arbitrate these issues", that behavioural evidence is also "insufficient to paint the full picture", and that "many of these debates cannot be settled a priori by considering general characteristics of untrained models" (pp. 9–10). Note what this does to the dialectic: it makes the *level of abstraction* question — the corpus's central question since Chalmers's implementation papers — an empirical one about trained systems.

On the individual debates, the survey positions rather than resolves. **Compositionality**: the Fodor & Pylyshyn dilemma (either neural networks fail to capture productivity and systematicity, or they merely implement a classical architecture and lack independent explanatory purchase) is revisited in light of compositional-generalization results, with the methodological complication that for models trained on gigantic corpora "it is near-impossible to rule out that the training set contains that exact syntactic pattern" (p. 11). **Grounding**: three distinct criticisms are separated — Bender & Koller's form/meaning argument, Harnad's symbol grounding problem ("an analogous problem arises for modern LLMs trained on text only (Mollo & Millière 2023)" — this batch's [entry 4](#4-coelho-mollo--millière-2023--the-vector-grounding-problem-tier-full), of which Millière is a co-author), and the Gricean objection that models lack communicative intentions. The authors then "dispens[e] with the terminology of 'understanding'", which has "little agreement on how this notion should be defined", in favour of **semantic competence**, split after Marconi (1997) into an **inferential** aspect (word-to-word: definitions, paraphrase, synonymy, entailment, translation) and a **referential** aspect (word-to-world: identifying a chair as a "chair", following "bring me the hammer"). The inferential aspect is judged well supported (Piantadosi & Hill's conceptual-role argument from the "intrinsic geometry" of the vector space; Grand et al. on alignment with human similarity judgements). The referential aspect is "more controversial", and the route canvassed is externalist rather than perceptual: Putnam's division of linguistic labour and Kripke's causal chains, with Mandelkern & Linzen arguing models "may inherit referential abilities by being appropriately linked to the causal chain of meaningful word use reflected in their training data", and Butlin (2021) on deference (pp. 16–17). **World models**: the authors are careful that the question "goes beyond the foregoing issues about basic semantic competence", since it concerns "not just the worldly referents of linguistic items, but global properties of the environment in which discourse entities are situated and interact" (p. 18); they report Wang et al.'s text-game-generation experiment (GPT-4 produced runnable games for unseen tasks in 28% of one-shot cases, 57% with self-correction on Python error messages) as "very tentative evidence", and Andreas's theoretical argument that efficiently compressing documents may require encoding the latent variables that generated them — "the syntactic knowledge, semantic beliefs, and communicative intentions of the text's human author(s)" (p. 19). The prime-number example makes it concrete: predicting how a human continues "2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17" is more efficiently done by encoding "primes under 20" than by rote. They flag that the clearest existence proof is the board-game toy models, deferred to Part II. **Cultural transmission**: Tomasello's "ratchet effect" is asked of machines, with the observation that so far it is humans who do the ratcheting — reverse-engineering AlphaZero's strategies into Go and chess theory, extracting latent materials-science knowledge from word embeddings — and the question is tied to Chollet's taxonomy of local, broad and extreme task generalisation.

**Place in the field's history.** This is the ground-clearing paper for the AI-minds literature: it is what Coelho Mollo & Millière cite when they need "a general introduction to language models" (entry 4, n. 1), and it is the document that most explicitly links the 2020s debate back to the 1980s one. Its historical thesis is *continuity* — the subtitle says so — and the specific continuity it asserts is with Block's Blockhead and with the Fodor & Pylyshyn critique of connectionism, not with Searle. Its lasting move is procedural: by naming the Redescription Fallacy it made a certain kind of dismissal (a system "merely" does linear algebra, "merely" predicts tokens) something a writer now has to defend rather than assert. That cuts against deflationary critics, but the authors apply the same standard to the other side by keeping Blockhead as the null hypothesis and insisting it has not been rejected.

**Relevance.** For the synthesis this is the methodological bookend to Shevlin ([entry 2](#2-shevlin-2021--non-human-consciousness-and-the-specificity-problem-tier-short)). Shevlin argues that a substrate-neutral theory cannot be applied to a non-human system until its level of specificity is fixed, and that fixing it requires evidence the theory's own experiments cannot supply. Millière and Buckner argue that claims about what a computational system can or cannot in principle implement cannot be settled from the architecture and objective alone, and require evidence about trained internals. Both conclusions constrain how computational functionalism can be *used*, without taking a position on whether it is true — and this paper is silent on consciousness by design, deferring it to Part II.

It also names a structural obstacle: internal analysis is "particularly challenging to do for very large models, and outright impossible for closed models whose weights are not released like GPT-4" (p. 19).

## Cross-paper notes for the synthesis

These are observations across the six reviewed works, recorded as evidence rather than as conclusions. Nothing here adjudicates between the positions.

**1. The evidential centre of gravity moves from behaviour to architecture — and every reviewed work moves it the same way.** Chalmers rejects self-report as evidence because models are "trained on a giant corpus of people talking about consciousness" and because a one-word prompt change reverses the answer (entry 3). Shevlin's **gerrymandered robot** is the general case: a system built to display the markers while lacking the architecture that normally produces them (entry 2). Long et al. make the same point operationally — systems "designed to mimic human behavior and are capable of 'gaming' behavioral tests", so "[w]e may thus need to focus less on behavioral evidence and more on architectural evidence, at least for now" (entry 5, p. 36). Millière & Buckner reach a parallel conclusion from a different direction, via Blockhead and psychologism: behavioural indistinguishability is compatible with mere retrieval, so internal mechanism has to be inspected (entry 7). Schwitzgebel & Garza had already stated the symmetric version in 2015 (the ASIMO Problem: reactions track cuteness, eyes and contingency, in both directions) (entry 1). **This is the single strongest convergence in the batch.** But note two things before treating it as consensus: Long et al. share two co-authors (Chalmers, Birch) with the works they cite for it, so the agreement is partly co-authorship rather than independent replication; and none of them says what to do when the architecture is also unavailable — Millière & Buckner note that internal analysis is "outright impossible for closed models whose weights are not released" (entry 7, p. 19).

**2. A genuine disagreement about what the objection *is*: metaphysical or epistemic?** Searle's Chinese Room and Block's lookup-table cases appear in four of these works in three different roles. Schwitzgebel & Garza read them as **epistemic**: whatever their metaphysical force, they show we may be unable to tell from outside *or* from inside whether a system is conscious, and Block (2002) may make it unsettleable (entry 1, ms. p. 30). Shevlin treats the Chinese Room as something his method should **exclude**: a marker-passing system lacking the relevant architecture, which the modest theoretical approach gives principled grounds to reject (entry 2, p. 310). Coelho Mollo & Millière inherit it through Harnad, who "himself followed this line" in taking symbol grounding as an elaboration of Searle (entry 4, n. 5), and answer it with a theory of content. Millière & Buckner conspicuously do **not** organise around Searle at all, using Block's Blockhead instead, because their question is about intelligence and competence rather than understanding or experience (entry 7). The synthesis should not flatten these into "the Chinese Room objection".

**3. Two separate senses in which "computation isn't enough" are in play, and they should not be merged.** The *biological* objection (consciousness requires carbon, or electrochemistry, or the kind of processing only biology currently supplies) appears in Chalmers as the one obstacle he concedes may be permanent, and in Long et al. as one term in the credence product, with named proponents (Block, Hill, McLaughlin, Godfrey-Smith, Seth) and named alternatives (analog computation, spatiotemporal integration). The *historical* objection is different and arrives from inside the AI literature: Coelho Mollo & Millière's Swamp LLM is parameter-identical to a trained model — same substrate, same computation, same input-output function — and still fails to represent, because function is conferred by selection history, not by present structure (entry 4, §7.1). That is a restriction on computational individuation that has nothing to do with biology, and the corpus's Part A2 implementation debate (Chalmers on rocks and finite-state automata, Piccinini, Sprevak, Coelho Mollo 2018 on functional individuation) is its natural home.

**4. Three incompatible procedural recommendations for acting under uncertainty.** Schwitzgebel & Garza's **Excluded Middle Policy**: only create systems whose moral status is clear, one way or the other (entry 1, ms. p. 32). Long et al.'s **Acknowledge / Assess / Prepare**, which assumes uncertain systems will be built and specifies corporate machinery for governing them, including an AI welfare officer (entry 5). And Chalmers's **twelve challenges**, which is explicitly a roadmap *and* "a set of red flags", with the author declining to say whether it should be followed (entry 3, PDF pp. 4, 17). These do not compose: the first counsels against building exactly the systems the second assumes and the third describes how to build. A synthesis should present all three rather than the consensus that does not exist. *(Birch's precautionary framework, which would be the fourth position here and the one Long et al. lean on, could not be reviewed — see below.)*

**5. Chalmers's role is unusual and should be flagged.** He appears in this batch as the author of entry 3, as a contributing author of entry 5, as the source of the credence that entry 5 quotes at length as evidence, and as a cited author in entry 4. His own note that "my own views lean somewhat more to consciousness being widespread" than the mainstream credences he computes with (entry 3, n. 29) means his stated numbers are deliberately *more* conservative than his own position. Treat the apparent convergence between entries 3 and 5 accordingly.

**6. Version caveats that affect citation.** Entry 4 is filed as 2023 but the PDF is the **2026 published version**, which replies to objections published in 2024 and cites 2025 work; the 2023 preprint does not contain §7.2. Entry 3 is filed as arXiv 2303.07103 but the PDF is the **author's version of the published Boston Review text** with a July 2023 Afterword the preprint lacks. Entry 1 is an **accepted manuscript** with its own pagination that does not match the published *Midwest Studies in Philosophy* pages 98–119. Entries 5 and 7 are plain preprints. Entry 2 is the only straightforwardly published, correctly dated, correctly paginated item in the batch.

**7. Terminology is not shared across the batch, and the differences are substantive.** Chalmers treats "consciousness" and "sentience" as roughly equivalent and prefers the former because the latter is more ambiguous (entry 3, PDF pp. 2–3). Long et al. do the opposite, reserving "sentience" for *valenced* consciousness and building an argument on the distinction (entry 5, pp. 12–13). Millière & Buckner abandon "understanding" entirely as too ill-defined, substituting "semantic competence" split into inferential and referential aspects (entry 7, p. 15). Coelho Mollo & Millière separate five things called "grounding" and argue four of them are beside the point (entry 4, §4). Shevlin's whole paper is about a term — whichever mechanism a theory names — being pitched at the wrong grain (entry 2). Any synthesis that harmonises this vocabulary will destroy the evidence; the disagreements about what the words mean *are* part of the field's history here.

**8. What is missing from this batch as constituted.** With `birch2024` unreviewed, the batch contains no primary statement of a precautionary framework for sentience candidates, and no statement of the animal-welfare methodology that entry 5 adapts. It also contains no work arguing *against* AI moral status from within the AI-minds literature — the opposing positions appear only as reported objections inside works that answer them (Bender & Koller via entries 4 and 7; Block's biological requirement via entry 3; Carruthers's rejectionism via entry 2; Grindrod via entry 4). The corpus's opposition to computational functionalism lives in batches B and C (Searle, Godfrey-Smith, Seth, Aru et al.), so the synthesis will need to import it rather than find it here.
