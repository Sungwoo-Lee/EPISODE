Predator, Food and Hazard Settings Screen

Agents were trained in eight versions of the lookalike-animals world, each changing a different mix of four settings: how the predator threatens, how food comes, whether hazards can be seen, and how many hazards there are and how hard they hit. Every agent was then put through the same fixed test scenes from the original world, injured or unhurt, with a harmless rabbit or no animal. The question is which settings make an injured agent hide more when the rabbit is around.

01 What was tested

What we are looking for. Hypervigilance here means: an injured agent hides more when a harmless rabbit is around than when no animal is around, by more than an unhurt agent does. Hiding is measured as bush dwell, the share of a 100-step test episode the agent spends on a bush. The rabbit on its own should barely move an unhurt agent.

The starting world (world 1). The lookalike-animals world: level 05 of the training curriculum (a cold 10×10 map with campfires, bushes, predators and rabbits) where the predator and the harmless rabbit smell the same, the predator gives no warning before it pounces, and food items are small. Earlier analysis found these agents die mostly while out foraging, which leaves little room for caution.

The four setting changes. Each is either kept as in world 1 or changed:

  • A, predator threat: always exactly one predator (instead of 0–2), which notices the agent and pounces from 2–3 cells instead of always 3.
  • B, food economy: fewer but much bigger food items, so a meal takes about 7 eating steps instead of about 25.
  • C, hazard concealment: rocks and the hidden trap become invisible, so a hit is a true surprise (and the map looks less cluttered).
  • D, hazard load: fewer rocks and traps, but traps hurt more.

Eight worlds instead of sixteen. Trying every combination would take 16 worlds. A standard half factorial uses 8, chosen so that each change is present in exactly 4 of them; each change's effect is the average of its 4 worlds minus the average of the other 4. The price is that the effects of two-way combinations come in pairs that cannot be told apart (for example, “A with B” and “C with D” are measured as one sum). The worlds: 1 (nothing changed), 2 (A and D), 3 (B and D), 4 (A and B), 5 (C and D), 6 (A and C), 7 (B and C), 8 (all four).

Agents. In each world an ordinary agent and a modulated agent (the same network plus a small neuromodulator network that rescales its activity), seed 42, trained for 10 million steps. World 1 also has a second seed (43) of each agent, shown as a guide to how much two seeds of the same agent differ.

Tests. At every saved checkpoint, 30 episodes of 100 steps in world 1's fixed scenes: the agent in the middle of the map, one bush three cells away, no campfire, so only injury and animals can drive hiding. The agent starts at injury 0, 10, 20 … 90, with no animal, a wandering rabbit, a chasing rabbit or a predator. Each scene is run with healing off (the healing rate set to zero in the test scene only, so the starting injury lasts the whole episode) and with healing on, as in training. Agents of worlds 2–8 meet world 1's food and visible obstacles here, not their own world's.

The number that matters. The injury contrast is the rabbit's extra bush dwell (with rabbit minus with no animal) at a given injury, minus the same for an unhurt agent. Above zero means injury makes the rabbit matter more. Injury 70 is the level fixed in the design and is the headline; injury 10 is shown beside it.

This page decides nothing. The design tests each world's agents in scenes built from their own world and uses those results for the decision. Testing every agent in world 1's scenes, as here, is the design's secondary cross-test: it can flag a change as interesting but cannot make it “worth replicating”. The own-world scenes for worlds 3, 4, 7 and 8 (the ones with bigger food) are not built yet; worlds 2, 5 and 6 share world 1's scenes, so their results here are already their own-world results.

02 Each setting change against the bar

For each setting change, the injury contrast averaged over the 4 worlds with the change minus the 4 without it, then averaged over the two agents. The bar is the size such a difference would reach by chance about one time in twenty from seed-to-seed variation and the run's own checkpoint-to-checkpoint noise (section 08). A change “clears the bar” when its effect is larger than the bar and has the same sign at the 6, 8 and 10 million-step checkpoints.

07 What these results do not show

  • One seed per world. Each of the 16 screen runs is a single training run. The bar allows for seed-to-seed variation as measured in earlier runs, but a world can still look different only because of its seed.
  • Unfamiliar scenes for some agents. Agents from worlds with big food (3, 4, 7, 8) or invisible hazards (5, 6, 7, 8) meet world 1's small food and visible obstacles in these tests. A difference between worlds may partly reflect how each agent copes with a world it was not trained in.
  • A and C are not the changes this measure was assigned to. The design gives each change its own deciding measure; this injury contrast belongs to D. A and C clearing the bar here is a secondary result.
  • A large contrast can come from an unusual unhurt baseline. The contrast subtracts the unhurt values. World 2's modulated agent hides about 23 % of the time with no animal even when unhurt, and that value swings widely from checkpoint to checkpoint; much of its large contrast comes from this baseline rather than from the rabbit adding hiding when injured (Figure 2, world 2 tab).
  • The rabbit alone moves some agents a lot. In two modulated runs (worlds 2 and 4) the rabbit lowers an unhurt agent's hiding by about 20 points, and in world 5's ordinary run it raises it by about 20. The pattern check only asks that this is smaller than the injury contrast.
  • Healing off is a test-only change. The agents were trained with healing; an injury that never heals is a situation they never met in training.
  • Pairs of changes cannot be separated. A pair such as “AB+CD” is the sum of two two-way interactions; the design cannot say which one carries it.

08 How it is computed

Every saved checkpoint of each run (one every 0.2 million training steps) was replayed in world 1's fixed test scenes, 30 episodes per checkpoint, scene and starting injury; bush dwell is the share of the 100 steps spent on the bush. A run stopped and resumed partway writes its checkpoints into two or three folders; these are joined into one series. For each checkpoint, the injury contrast is (bush dwell with the rabbit minus with no animal) at the chosen injury, minus the same at injury 0. Each run is summarised by the mean over its newest 20 tested checkpoints (about the last 4 million steps), with a 95 % interval that allows for neighbouring checkpoints being alike: it uses an effective number of checkpoints, reduced according to the lag-1 autocorrelation (how strongly each checkpoint's value predicts the next one's). For a setting change, each agent's mean over the 4 worlds with the change minus the 4 without is computed, and the two agents are averaged. The bar is twice the expected standard deviation of that difference: the square root of (σ²/4 + the sum of the 16 runs' squared standard errors / 64), where σ = 2.67 points is the seed-to-seed spread of this test measure recorded in the project's critical-settings registry, and a run's standard error is the uncertainty of its 20-checkpoint mean. The sign check repeats the difference using the single tested checkpoint nearest 6, 8 and 10 million steps.
