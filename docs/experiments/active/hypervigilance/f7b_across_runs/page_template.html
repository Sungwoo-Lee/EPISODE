FIRST The most effective conditions

This page looks for the training conditions where hiding in the bush depends most clearly on two things: the animal (the agent hides more with a hunting predator than with no animal) and the agent's own injury (an agent that starts the test injured hides more than one that starts unhurt). The search is for the strongest cases, not for averages across groups. Each condition is shown with both agent types trained in it: the ordinary agent and the modulated agent, whose network has an extra module that rescales its internal signals according to its inputs.

Every agent was replayed in the same short test scenes (100 steps, a bush to hide in, and either no animal, a harmless wandering rabbit, a rabbit that chases the agent, or a hunting predator), at every saved checkpoint of its training, unhurt and injured. The numbers below use every checkpoint from 2 to 10 million training steps, not only the last few. The data and the tests are described in full in the next section.

Agents trained in the temperature worlds (levels 05 and 06) need temperature in their test scenes. The 22-Sep runs were measured in neutral scenes (air at 0 °C everywhere, no campfire, body temperature starting at 0); every later study (smell, body rules, thirst) only in its own scenes (the training world's cold air, a campfire right beside the bush, a random starting body temperature). How Figures B4–B8 are computed

Each agent of a pair was replayed at every saved checkpoint in the same four test scenes, with the starting injury set to exactly 0, 10, 20, …, 90 (the scenes are otherwise identical to the ones behind B2 and B3; at injury 0 and 70 they give the same numbers checkpoint by checkpoint), 30 episodes per checkpoint, scene and injury. Bush dwell is the share of the 100 steps spent on the bush. In B5 and B7 each line is the running average of 5 neighbouring checkpoints, drawn only to make ten overlapping lines readable; the numbers printed above each panel do not use it. Those numbers, and every point in B4, B6 and B8, are the plain mean over the checkpoints from 2 to 10 M training steps, one every 0.2 M steps. The 95 % interval around each point allows for neighbouring checkpoints being alike: it uses an effective number of checkpoints, reduced by the correlation between each checkpoint and the next (the lag-1 autocorrelation). Level 02 was tested without the chasing rabbit, so B4 has three panels. These are two trained runs per figure, one per agent type; they show how one agent's hiding depends on its injury, not how much that differs between training seeds.

How it is computed

Each trained agent was replayed in fixed test scenes at every saved checkpoint, 30 episodes per checkpoint, scene and starting injury; bush dwell is the share of the 100 steps the agent spent on the bush. For each checkpoint we form three differences: injured (starting injury 70) minus unhurt (0) with no animal; the same with the wandering rabbit; and predator minus no animal, both unhurt. Only checkpoints from 2 to 10 million training steps are used, one every 0.2 million steps, so a run that saved checkpoints twice as often does not count twice as much. Each difference is then averaged over those 41 checkpoints. The 95 % interval allows for neighbouring checkpoints being alike: it uses an effective number of checkpoints reduced by the correlation between each checkpoint and the next (the lag-1 autocorrelation). "Checkpoints where injury raised it" counts how often the no-animal difference is above zero; it describes consistency and is not a test, since neighbouring checkpoints are not independent. A run is left out of the ranking if, in a scene a measure uses, it survives fewer than 95 of the 100 steps on average over its newest 20 checkpoints. A condition is one world, setting, seed and test-scene set; every agent trained with the temperature system is ranked on its neutral-scene test only (the 22-Sep runs on the thermal battery's neutral arm, later runs on neutral copies of their own scenes), so all of them are measured in the same conditions. The ranking score is the lower of an agent's two percentile ranks (injury dependence, the mean of the two injury effects; animal dependence, the predator effect), and a condition is placed by its better agent.

THE DATA

Every agent on this page is a small learning program that lives on a grid of squares. It can move, eat, hide on a bush square, and get hurt by a predator; a rabbit looks and moves like an animal but is harmless. The agent senses its own body: how hungry it is and how injured it is, on a scale from 0 (unhurt) to 100. It learns by trial and error over millions of steps, and a copy of it is saved about 50 times along the way; each saved copy is a checkpoint. A run is one such training from start to end; runs that differ only in their random starting point are different seeds.

Two kinds of agent appear throughout. The ordinary agent has one network that turns what it senses into a move. The modulated agent has, in addition, a small second network that reads the body's state and adjusts how the main network works — a rough model of how chemicals such as noradrenaline change how the brain processes the same input.

The levels

Agents are trained on a ladder of worlds called levels. Each level keeps everything from the one below it and adds something new, so a level-05 agent has met every challenge of levels 00 to 04 plus the cold. The table shows what is switched on at each level today, read from the level files themselves. Levels were renumbered twice (16 and 30 September), so an older run's level number can mean a slightly different world; the group table below says where that matters.

The tests

None of these numbers come from the world the agent trained in. Each agent was put into short experiment tests: a 10×10 grid with the agent in the middle, one bush three squares to its left, and at most one animal starting on the far right; 100 steps; no food. There are six scenes — no animal; a hunting predator that can hurt it; a chasing rabbit that moves exactly like the predator but is harmless; the same rabbit with its smell removed; a wandering rabbit; and a wandering rabbit that smells like a predator — each played with the agent starting unhurt (injury 0) or badly hurt (injury 70), 30 times at every checkpoint. What is measured is bush dwell: the share of the 100 steps the agent spends on the bush.

QUESTION

On the Basic Behaviour page, Figure 7b showed that agents spend more time in the bush when a harmless rabbit chases them if they start the test badly injured (injury 70) than if they start uninjured (injury 0). That looks like injury making the agent treat a harmless animal as a threat. This page asks the same question of every trained agent that has, or could be given, the same experiment tests: short 100-step scenes with the agent, one bush and at most one animal, replayed at every saved checkpoint. These are not the agent's training environment; they are controlled scenes in which only the animal and the starting injury change.

This is exploration, not a test of a hypothesis. The aim is to find which settings make the injury effect larger or smaller, as cues for what to train next.

HEADLINE

What the runs show

1. Most of the injury effect is not about the rabbit, and it follows one rule of the body. In the {{N_FAST}} runs trained with fast healing on a bush, starting at injury 70 raises bush dwell by about {{NONE_NEW}} percentage points even when no animal is present. In the {{N_SLOW}} runs trained without it, the same number is {{NONE_SLOW}}. That fits injured agents going to the bush because that is where they heal, rabbit or no rabbit. It is a strong cue, not a proof: the runs with and without fast bush healing also differ in date, world version and how much food the body can store. The wandering rabbit adds almost nothing on top of that (median {{WANDER_MINUS}} points across the {{N_WANDER}} rows that are not hollow).

2. The chasing rabbit is where runs differ. Its extra injury effect, beyond the no-animal scene's, ranges from {{CHASE_MIN}} to {{CHASE_MAX}} points across runs. The largest values come from curriculum wave 1 at level 03 ({{W1_L03}} points, both agents), with two caveats: those tests ran before the test bush was fixed to keep animals out, and much of the value comes from the empty scene — those agents' no-animal injury effects are {{W1_NONE}} points, their chasing-rabbit injury effects {{W1_RAW}}. The groups trained with fast bush healing sit at or below zero: the smell study {{SMELL_MINUS}}, the thirst task {{THIRST_MINUS}}, the temperature scene set {{THERMAL_MINUS}}.

3. One body rule separates cleanly, but the test cannot be trusted there. In the body-rules worlds, the chasing rabbit's extra injury effect is {{HC_ON}} points when healing costs food and {{HC_OFF}} when it does not. Those same agents starve in the test: the scenes hold no food, and healing from injury 70 uses it up, so average survival falls to {{HC_SURV}} of 100 steps. Their values are drawn hollow and are not interpreted.

4. The two agent types overlap. Inside each group the modulated agents (triangles in Figures 2 and 3) and the ordinary agents (circles) spread over the same range; this page does not separate them further.

TWO KINDS

Put every run on one map. Across: animal dependence — how much more the unhurt agent sits in the bush when an animal is there than when the scene is empty. Up: state dependence — how much more it sits in the bush when it starts injured, with no animal at all.

  • The {{N_SLOW}} runs trained before fast bush healing lie on the zero line: injury does nothing to them (median {{NONE_SLOW}} points). How much an animal matters varies a lot from run to run. Among the runs trained with the ordinary learning method, a chasing rabbit adds a median {{ANIM_SLOW_MED}} points and up to {{ANIM_SLOW_MAX}} (the strongest July and August runs); the {{N_DREAMER}} Dreamer runs, a different learning method, barely react to any animal.
  • The {{N_FAST}} runs trained with it sit about {{NONE_NEW}} points up, and react to the chasing rabbit more evenly: a median {{ANIM_FAST_MED}} points, at most {{ANIM_FAST_MAX}}.
  • The wandering rabbit: small for every run, but larger before fast bush healing. For an unhurt agent, the older runs trained with the ordinary learning method sit in the bush a median {{N00_OLD}}% of the time with no animal, and a wandering rabbit adds a median {{WAND_OLD}} points: from almost nothing to a clear response. The recent runs already sit there {{N00_NEW}}% with no animal, and the wandering rabbit adds a median {{WAND_NEW}}. The Dreamer runs do not react at all. (An earlier version of this page pooled all rows into one median and said the rabbit was ignored “old and new alike”; that hid the difference.)
  • One older study shows injury working the other way. In the August rest-premium runs (injury heals faster the longer the agent rests), starting injured often lowers bush dwell when a predator or a wandering rabbit is near (Figure 4h): the injured agent stays out rather than heading for cover.
  • No run is in the top right: none both reacts strongly to animals and changes with its own injury. Combining the two is the open question this page points to.

FACTORS

Which factor makes the agent (A) hide more when injured with a wandering rabbit, and (B) treat the wandering rabbit like a threat?

Each factor is measured only between runs that differ in that factor and as little else as the existing runs allow: the same runs tested two ways, the same seed and agent in two smell worlds, two body-rule worlds that differ in one rule, and so on. A is bush dwell with a wandering rabbit at injury 70 minus at injury 0. B is bush dwell with a wandering rabbit minus with no animal, both at injury 70: how much more the injured agent hides because the rabbit is there.

  • A has one strong factor: fast healing on a bush. Going from curriculum wave 1 to the runs trained a day later raises A in every one of the six matched pairs (Figure F, top row). Nothing else moves A by more than a few points on average.
  • The same factor lowers B. Once the bush heals, the injured agent goes there whether or not the rabbit is present, so the rabbit itself adds less.
  • No factor in the existing runs raises B much. The largest average rises are small and mixed: a predator that can jump (level 04 instead of 03) and a training bush that keeps animals out. Removing the ambush predators lowers B.

So hiding more when injured and treating a harmless animal as a threat are moved by different things; getting both at once is not something any existing run shows.

03 How it is computed

Every saved checkpoint of a run was replayed 30 times in each scene, by the project's dwell-sweep driver. For one run, one scene and one starting injury this gives one bush-dwell number per checkpoint: the share of the test's steps the agent spent on the bush, averaged over the 30 episodes. Early checkpoints come from an agent that is still learning, so only the newest 20 checkpoints are summarised, as on the Basic Behaviour page. The injury effect is formed checkpoint by checkpoint first (injury 70 minus injury 0, same checkpoint), and the 20 differences are then averaged. The rabbit's extra effect subtracts the no-animal scene's injury effect, again checkpoint by checkpoint. Each interval is a 95% t-interval on those 20 values (the range that would contain the true average 95 times in 100 if the values were independent draws, using Student's t distribution because 20 values is a small sample), widened when neighbouring checkpoints are correlated: the effective number of values is 20 × (1 − r) / (1 + r), where r is the correlation between each checkpoint and the next. The interval therefore describes how much the value moves from checkpoint to checkpoint within one training run. It says nothing about seed-to-seed variation; only the smell study has more than one seed per setting. A value is drawn hollow when, in a scene it uses, the agent's average survival is under 95 of 100 steps: bush dwell is then a share of a shorter episode and is not interpreted. A run needs at least 20 tested checkpoints to be included.

05 Issues found while building this page

  1. The body-rules agents starve in the experiment tests. The scenes hold no food. In worlds where healing costs food, an agent starting at injury 70 uses its food to heal and dies around step 71–82. Their injury effects are drawn hollow. A fair test of those worlds needs a scene with food, or a start with more food.
  2. The July runs use an older scene set. Their tests ran before the bush was made to block animals, so a rabbit could follow the agent into it. They also wrote the bush-dwell measure under an older column name; it is the same quantity (share of the episode's steps on the bush cell) and was renamed when read.
  3. Curriculum wave 1 was tested before the test bush was fixed. Its tests ran on 22 September; on 23 September the test scenes were found to let animals walk into the bush, which the training worlds never allowed, and were fixed. Wave 1 was never re-tested, so it sits in its own scene set. The two "wave 2" test results are the same two runs as the fast-bush-healing level-04 rows, measured before the fix; their re-test after the fix is used instead, and the old one is in the dropped table.
  4. “Newest 20 checkpoints” is a short window for the long July runs. The July curriculum runs at levels 03 to 05 were trained to 80–100 million steps, so their newest 20 checkpoints cover only the last 2 million. Their response to the wandering rabbit fades late in training: for level 04 it is about 6 points over the newest 20 checkpoints but about 11 over the whole run. The full-training figures for these runs (results/eval/avoidance/metrics_history/) show the stronger response.
  5. Bush healing speed is read from each run's saved settings. Wave 1 states it (no bonus); the July runs predate the setting, so "same as elsewhere" is assumed for them, not read.
  6. The injury-grid scene set has no chasing rabbit. Its 30 rows appear only in the wandering-rabbit panels.
  7. One seed per setting, except the smell study. The intervals are within-run only, so a difference between two single-seed runs may be seed-to-seed variation.
  8. The temperature variants with no fire are the most negative. In those scenes the agent is cold and the warm cell, not the bush, may decide where it goes; this is a cue to check, not a finding.
