Question. Does an injured agent hide more when a harmless rabbit wanders nearby than when no animal is around, by more than it does when unhurt, while the rabbit alone barely moves an unhurt agent? That two-part pattern is what this project calls hypervigilance. This page reports the first test of agents trained in the lookalike-animals world, where the predator and the rabbit smell the same and the predator only reveals itself when it pounces, beside twelve earlier level-05 agents tested the same way.

Answer: no agent shows the pattern.

The rabbit alone already makes unhurt agents hide. At injury 0 the rabbit adds about 1–10 points of bush dwell (25 for one agent), with an interval above zero in 13 of the 16 agents (section 06 table). That fails the second half of the pattern, and in the lookalike world it is the expected result of an agent that cannot tell the rabbit from the predator by smell.

With bush healing on, as in training, injury does not change the rabbit's effect. In 13 of 16 agents the rabbit adds the same amount at injury 10 as when unhurt, within ±1 point; the other three differ by 2–3 points in both directions (Figures 1–3, section 05). A likely reason, not yet checked directly: healing on the bush is fast, so a test that starts injured barely stays injured.

With healing switched off in the test only, ordinary agents show a larger raw gap, but not the pattern. When the starting injury lasts the whole episode, the rabbit's extra hiding at injury 10 exceeds its unhurt value by +6.7 points on average over the 8 ordinary agents (steadily above zero in 5, +6.5 to +13.9) and by +1.3 over the 8 modulated agents (Figures 4–6). That gap disappears on the log-odds scale, which removes the effect of where an agent sits on its S-shaped hiding curve: there the rabbit's effect at injury 10 is no larger than at injury 0 in any of the 16 agents, and it is smaller in 6 (section 06 table). Modulated agents already hide about twice as much at injury 10 with no animal, so their smaller raw gap may only reflect sitting higher on that curve; a real difference between the two kinds of agent is not shown. At injury 70 nothing shows in any agent, because an injured agent without the rabbit already hides 80–90 % of the time.

How far this goes. Injury 10 was chosen after looking at the curves; the only contrast stated in advance was injury 70. At injury 20 two agents go the opposite way. Healing-off is a test-only change the agents never saw in training, and each group has two or three seeds. The raw-scale gap at mild injury is a lead only if a fresh test, fixed in advance and measured on the log-odds scale, finds it again.

Correction. A first draft of this summary read the raw gap as “yes for most ordinary agents”. The review before publication pointed to the rabbit-alone effect and the log-odds scale, and the log-odds table, computed for all 16 agents, reversed that reading.
