What Both Agents Compute

For a month the agent with a neuromodulator and the agent without one have come out almost identical in every world we built. Before designing a better modulator, this page asks what computation the two agents share, and only then why the modulator adds nothing to it. The first answers are in: at the layers the modulator acts on, the two agents are less alike than two ordinary agents are, while surviving equally well.

PURPOSEWhy this page exists

The long-term goal is to find a neuromodulator that actually helps an agent. Across a month of comparisons in many different worlds, the modulated and ordinary agents have come out almost the same every time. That consistency is too tight to be noise: it points to both architectures converging on the same way of processing information, shaped more by the structure of the world than by the network design. So it is not enough to treat the result as a failed hypothesis and try another modulator at random. A modulator can only help by adding something the network does not already compute, and right now we do not know what that shared computation is.

The two questions, in this order

First: what computation does every architecture converge on in these worlds?

Then: why does the modulator not help?

This page collects the evidence behind that reordering, the algorithmic explanations a review of the literature turned up, and the measurements made to answer both questions. The comparison ran on six freshly trained agents, three of each kind, trained on the same three random seeds. Every verdict word on the page comes from decision rules written down before any number existed, and two independent reviews checked what the page may claim from them.

Headline so far

To judge whether two agents are alike inside, we check how well one agent's layer activity predicts the other's on the same inputs, and compare that with the range spanned by pairs of ordinary agents trained from different seeds (the ordinary band). At the end of training the modulated agent's encoder output, actor hidden layer and critic hidden layer are less interchangeable with an ordinary agent's than two ordinary agents are with each other (all 6 pairs below the ordinary band, both directions on the encoder and actor), while both survive equally well. Modulated agents are also less interchangeable with each other than ordinary agents are. Whether the difference reaches the memory the modulator never touches, the test cannot say.

All three layers that differ are places where the modulator applies its gain and offset, so they receive its input by construction. The finding is that the agents are different at the layers the modulator acts on, not that the main network learned a different function. Survival is equal but measured at a ceiling: both agents reach the 500-step episode limit in about 94 % of episodes.

On Question 2, when the modulator starts to matter during training was measured in 16 worlds. Some of its measures make half of their change before the agent's survival levels off; a measure of the size of its weights makes half of its growth after, as the main network's own weights do. The pre-registered test therefore points both ways (section 04). Freezing its modulation after survival levels off costs about 31–33 survival steps in all 16 worlds (a descriptive number, not a registered test). Section 05 says what this changes for the search for a helpful modulator.

01A month of near-identical results

About 200 training runs compared the two agents. The ordinary agent is a recurrent network trained with PPO. The modulated agent is the same network plus a small side network, the neuromodulator, which at every step sends each unit in up to four places of the main network a gain (a multiplier) and an offset (an added amount). Performance is always measured in survival steps: how long the agent stays alive.

What the pattern says

If a real but modest difference were hidden by noise, the gaps between independently trained agents would scatter: sometimes clearly positive, sometimes clearly negative, about as far apart as two ordinary agents with different seeds. They do not scatter. They stay pinned near zero across worlds that differ a great deal, and in the 16-world factorial the gap has the same sign every time. That is the signature of something systematic making the two agents come out the same.

02Four candidate reasons the agents come out the same

A review of this project's runs, code and the FiLM and reinforcement-learning literature it holds produced four explanations. They are not exclusive. Each is a claim the planned measurements can test.

1. The two agents start from the same place

The main network's layers are built before the modulator (in src/models/recurrent_ppo_network.py, the encoder at line 400, the actor and critic at lines 424–429, the modulator at line 458), and the ordering is kept deliberately so the random-number stream is unchanged. With the same seed, the modulated agent's main network therefore starts with exactly the ordinary agent's weights and sees the same stream of environments. The modulator's gain starts near 1 (1 ± 0.3) and its offset near 0, so at the start it barely changes anything. Small, clipped PPO updates can then keep both runs in the same solution.

2. The worlds ask for threshold shifts, which an ordinary network already has

Internal states (hunger, injury, temperature) do act as task variables here: the reward is the change in homeostatic drive, so what counts as rewarding depends on the agent's needs, in the sense of Keramati and Gutkin{{CITE:keramati}}. But the kind of task family matters. An ordinary network receives the internal state as an input, and that adds a context-dependent amount to each unit:

← wider than the screen — scroll it sideways; the right-hand end is cut off

ordinary:   h = ReLU( W_x·x + W_c·c + b )
FiLM:       h = ReLU( γ(c)·(W_x·x + b) + β(c) )

With the gain γ fixed at 1 the two are the same: feeding the condition in as an input is FiLM without the gain{{CITE:perez}}. An added amount moves the point at which a unit switches on, which is a threshold shift (a hungrier agent approaches fainter food, an injured one hides sooner). The gain does something different: it changes how strongly a sense drives a unit, which is what is needed when a need must change which senses matter. The behaviour measured in ordinary agents so far looks mostly like threshold shifts. The ordinary agent's memory is also a GRU, whose gates already multiply its state by functions of its inputs, internal state included.

Three more properties make these worlds easy for an ordinary network. The needs rise and fall many times within every episode, so every training batch contains every context and there is no interference between them to relieve. The internal state is visible at every step, so there is no hidden context to infer. And the published cases where FiLM beats a plain network all involve a regime that changes which behaviour is correct, such as a task switch or a hidden context. The one large positive result here came from the May probe, whose predator alternated between dangerous and harmless phases.

3. Training gives the modulator no reason to carry context, and it may wake up late

One optimiser updates every parameter, modulator included, with the same learning rate (gradient clip 0.5, then Adam at 5×10−4), no weight decay and no objective of its own. The critic's error also trains the modulator. The main network is roughly 100 times larger and absorbs the body-related part of the learning signal first. Two measured facts fit this. Only about 15% of the modulator's gain variation follows the situation; the rest is a fixed per-unit setting the main network could have learned itself. And its share of the gradient grows over training, reaching 40–75% in some arms only by the end. A part that starts working only in the second half of training can shape the final solution but cannot shorten the early climb, where a faster-learning advantage would appear. This was a candidate when the page was planned; section 04 (B2) reports what the timing measurement found, and it does not settle the question either way.

4. The measurements could not see the kind of gain FiLM usually gives

Every comparison so far used final performance. In reinforcement learning, FiLM's reported advantage is mostly faster learning, and learning speed has not been measured here with seeds.

A puzzle any explanation has to fit

Freezing a trained modulated agent's modulation at its own average costs it 47 to 180 of about 200 survival steps (measured on modulators reading every sense, and not at every site). Yet the modulated agent trained end to end does no better than an ordinary one. The reading that fits both: training routes part of the same solution through the modulator, so freezing it removes a working part, not a benefit.

03Question 1: what do the two agents compute?

If the two agents converge on the same information processing, the evidence should show it at the level of their internal activity, not only their scores. The measures all run on identical inputs: one stored episode is fed to every agent, with its memory reset at the first step, so every activation is paired with the other agent's activation at the same moment. The data are the May replication: three ordinary and three modulated agents trained from scratch through five stages (different worlds), read at the end of training in the final world, where a predator is active. A reading taken at the end of the first stage was also made and is shown beside it as interim.

Answer so far

The two agents do not converge on interchangeable activity at the layers the modulator acts on: at the encoder, actor and critic they are further apart than two ordinary agents from different seeds, while surviving equally well (A1, A3). They hold the same information in broadly the same places, with hunger slightly less linearly readable from the modulated agent's encoder output and memory state (A2). Whether their memories differ, the registered test cannot say. Whether they move together from world to world (A4) is still being built.

A1

Layer similarity

Measured, reviewed

Centred kernel alignment{{CITE:kornblith}} asks whether two layers arrange the same set of inputs in the same way: whether inputs that look alike to one agent's layer also look alike to the other's. It compares layers of different networks without needing their units to line up.

Layers
Encoder output, memory state, memory output, actor and critic hidden layers; for the modulated agent both before and after the gain and offset are applied.
Caution
Linear CKA ignores rotations and uniform scaling but not per-unit rescaling, which is exactly what the modulator's large fixed gain does. It is paired with a rescaling-robust measure (SVCCA{{CITE:raghu}}, or how well one agent's layer linearly predicts the other's).
Reads as
High similarity beyond the seed yardstick (A3): the agents represent the world the same way. High only within the same seed: inherited from the shared start.
Registered statistic
Mutual linear predictivity: how much of one agent's layer activity a linear map from the other agent's layer recovers on held-out episodes, taking the weaker of the two directions. CKA is reported beside it and decides nothing.

Interim readings are provisional by rule; the end-of-training reading is the verdict. Encoder and critic also carry the rules' qualifier that modulated agents differ among themselves more than ordinary agents do; in words, the modulated–modulated pairs sit below the ordinary band on every layer.

  • The three layers that differ are all places where the modulator acts. Each receives the modulator's gain and offset by construction, so the result says the agents are different at the layers the modulator acts on. It does not say the main network learned a different function.
  • Critic: different; the modulated critic carries activity the ordinary critic cannot predict, and keeps more of its units active (58–66 vs 37–48 of 128).
  • Memory: on the memory layers the registered test cannot tell trained from untrained networks, so it gives no verdict; the secondary measure (CKA, reported, not verdict-bearing) puts modulated-vs-ordinary below the ordinary band on all 6 pairs there too.
  • Why the memory reading changed between stage 1 and the end. The memory layers read "different" at the end of stage 1 and "no verdict" at the end of training. That is a change in a gate, not in the agents: the rules refuse a verdict where untrained networks score as high as trained ones, and between the two points the ordinary band moved up until it touched the untrained band. The critic moved the other way, from "undetermined" to "different".
  • Other ways of scoring it (Figure A1b, descriptive): the direction a fixed per-unit gain cannot change, modulated predicting ordinary, is below every ordinary pair for all 6 pairs on the encoder, both memory layers and the actor; on the critic it overlaps.
A2

Decoding profiles

Measured, reviewed

A small linear readout is trained to recover a quantity from each layer. How well it succeeds, layer by layer, is that agent's decoding profile.

Quantities
Hunger (satiation), injury (injury_level), distance to the nearest predator, and value, measured as survival steps remaining in the episode.
Caution
Readouts are trained and tested on different episodes, never on different steps of the same episode, because neighbouring steps are nearly identical and a step-level split inflates every score.
Reads as
Matching profiles: the same information is present in the same places. A mismatch shows where the modulated agent holds information differently.

Result, end of training

  • Hunger (registered reading: differs): hunger is slightly less linearly readable from the modulated agent's encoder output and memory state (R² lower by ~0.02); both agents encode it at every layer, far above the clock baseline.
  • Injury (registered reading: undetermined): descriptively, the modulated agent's encoder output holds the body's injury level better (R² higher by about 0.22). That is one layer, and the rules do not count a one-layer difference.
  • Distance to the nearest predator (undetermined): a one-layer difference at the critic, not counted.
  • Survival steps remaining: blocked by gate G6. Only episodes that end in death have a known remaining lifetime, and too few of them remain for the minimum number of held-out episode groups the rules require.

04Question 2: why does the modulator not help?

These test the first and third candidate reasons directly. They come second because their results are easier to read once Question 1 has said what the two agents share. B2 has been measured and reviewed; B1 needs new training and a decision to launch it.

B1

Break the shared start

Needs a decision

Status: needs a decision. Either option means training new agents; nothing has been launched.

Two ways to stop the modulated agent from beginning as a copy of the ordinary one.

  • Train modulated agents whose main network starts from a different seed than their paired ordinary agent.
  • Or keep the same seed but give the modulator a stronger start: output heads initialised to zero so the gain is exactly 1 and the offset exactly 0, plus a learning rate of its own so its early updates are not swamped.
Reads as
If the gaps then scatter at the size of ordinary seed-to-seed differences, the shared start produced the identical results. If they stay pinned near zero, the equivalence is real.
B2

When the modulator starts to matter

Measured, reviewed

Four kinds of measure of when the modulator starts to matter during training, because each can mislead on its own. They were read on 16 worlds of the level-05 body-rules factorial (one seed each, 50 saved checkpoints) and, descriptively, on the three modulated May-replication seeds.

MeasureWhat it answers
Share of the gradient reaching the modulator, split by loss termHow much of the learning signal it receives
Size of its weight updates relative to its weights, against the main network'sHow fast it actually changes (Adam rescales gradients, so the share alone can mislead)
How much its output varies with the situation, at each checkpointWhen its output starts responding
Cost of freezing it, at a subset of checkpointsWhen it becomes something the network relies on
Summary
Wake-up lag: the wake point minus the survival plateau, in checkpoints. The wake point is the first checkpoint at which a measure has covered half of its change from the untrained network's value to its final value, in the direction it actually moves, and stays there; no point is named when that change is within the curve's own checkpoint-to-checkpoint noise. The plateau is the checkpoint at which survival has covered 90% of its rise. A lag within one checkpoint either way counts as coincident. Across worlds, a sign test reads each measure as "late", "early" or "undetermined". (An earlier draft used half of the final value; that version was withdrawn because several measures start above half their final value, so it would be met at the very first checkpoint.)
Refutation
As registered: a measure reading "early" refutes the explanation "the modulated agent does not learn faster because the modulator wakes up late"; a measure reading "late" supports it. "Undetermined" does neither. The rules do not combine measures.
Also
The success test for B1's stronger start: it should pull the wake-up earlier. If the lag shrinks but learning speed does not change, late wake-up was not the cause.

Result

  • Gain swing, late. The weights-only ceiling on how far the modulator could move each gain (the gain swing) grows throughout training, roughly linearly, so the point where it has made half of its rise comes after the survival plateau in all 16 worlds (the three May seeds agree). Five sites, one measure. The main network's own total weight norm, read with the same rule, is also "late" in all 16 worlds, so this reading reflects how weight norms grow in this trainer, not a late awakening of the modulator.
  • Contextual fraction, early. The share of the modulator's gain signal that varies with the situation (rho) is lower at the end of training than at the untrained network, at the encoder and recurrent sites in all 16 worlds; half of that fall is complete by the first saved checkpoints, well before survival levels off ("early" in 15–16 of 16). At the actor and critic sites no consistent timing is found.
  • Update size and gradient probes. The modulator's relative step per checkpoint interval is roughly constant (≈ 0.10) across training; where its small net change clears the noise guard, its half-point is early. The share of the entropy-term gradient reaching the modulator rises from near zero at the untrained network to 8–37 % by the first saved checkpoint (early, 12 of 12 defined). The policy- and value-term shares from the single-batch probe are too noisy for the timing rule (no reading), as is the logged total share.
  • Freeze cost, no timing reading. Freezing the gain or the offset at its time-averaged value gives no timing reading: the cost's checkpoint-to-checkpoint wobble (11–28 survival steps) is as large as its net change over training, so the rule names no wake point in 15 of 16 worlds. This says nothing about the size of the cost. Descriptively, after the plateau the frozen agent survives fewer steps in every world (mean −33 steps for the gain, −31 for the offset, against roughly 250 live); no test of that difference was registered.
  • The registered refutation, both ways. Here the modulator's realised output (rho at three sites), its entropy-gradient share and its update size read "early", which refutes the late-wake explanation for those measures; only the weights-only gain-swing ceiling reads "late", which supports it. The rules do not combine measures, so no single timing verdict exists.

05What this changes for the modulator question

The final goal is a modulator that helps. Three results bear on it directly; each is stated here only as far as the reviews allow.

  • The modulator changes what its own sites carry. At the encoder, actor and critic, where it applies its gain and offset, the modulated agent's activity is less interchangeable with an ordinary agent's than two ordinary agents' are with each other (A1, A3). Whether anything downstream of those sites, such as the memory state, differs is not established.
  • It is relied on. Freezing the modulation at its time-averaged value after survival has levelled off costs about 31–33 survival steps in 16 of 16 worlds, against roughly 250 live (descriptive, not a registered test).
  • "No benefit" is partly a measurement limit. In the May replication both agents reach the 500-step episode limit in about 94 % of episodes, so survival cannot show an advantage that lies above the cap.

Unregistered interpretation

Taken together, these fit a modulator that is not idle: it changes the activity at the layers it acts on and the trained agent depends on it, yet the agent's survival does not improve. Two readings remain open and no registered test separates them: the modulator may take over part of a solution the ordinary network also reaches, or it may add something the capped survival measure cannot see. The next measurements that would bear on this are A4 (whether the two agents move together across worlds), B1 (breaking the shared start), and a task with room above the step cap or with learning speed as the outcome.

06Where things stand

Established

  • No modulated-vs-ordinary survival difference beyond seed noise in about 200 runs.
  • The gaps are pinned near zero across very different worlds, not scattered.
  • At the end of training, the modulated agent is different at the layers the modulator acts on (encoder, actor, critic), with equal survival at the ceiling (A1, A3).
  • Both agents hold hunger, injury and predator distance at every layer; hunger is slightly less linearly readable from the modulated encoder output and memory state (A2).
  • The modulator is relied on inside a trained agent: freezing it costs survival in every world measured.
  • Both agents start from the same main-network weights under the same seed.
  • The modulator has no training signal or learning rate of its own.

Open

  • Whether the memory layers differ: the registered test gives no verdict there.
  • Whether the two agents' processing moves together across worlds (A4, being built).
  • Whether the shared start explains the identical results (B1, needs a decision).
  • Whether the modulator wakes up late: the registered measures point both ways (B2).
  • Whether the modulated agent learns faster than the ordinary one; learning speed has not been measured with seeds.

Discussed, not yet planned

  • Train a plain network to imitate a modulated agent's recorded behaviour: if the copy matches it, the modulator added nothing a plain network cannot represent.
  • Re-read existing runs for learning speed: area under the learning curve and time to a threshold.
  • Controls never run: the same freeze applied to an ordinary agent; how many units are permanently switched off; whether the modulator's varying gain follows the agent's internal state.
  • A world-conflict measure: how often identical external situations call for different actions depending on internal state.
  • Give the modulator its own training signal, such as predicting changes in the body.
  • A two-by-two design: internal-state signals hidden from the main network or visible to it, crossed with a stable or an alternating world.
