Injury, Behaviour and the Modulator

An exploration, not a verdict. Two agents trained side by side — an ordinary one and one with a neuromodulator — are asked one question in four different ways: how much does what they do depend on how injured they are, and where does the modulator strengthen or weaken that dependence?

01 What this page asks

The project's goal is an agent whose behaviour depends on the state of its own body. Injury is the state studied here: does an injured agent act differently from a healthy one — hide, rest, stay in cover until healed — and does it do so because of the injury it feels? A rabbit, a predator or an empty field are only different settings in which to look; the question is the dependence on injury itself, whatever the cue.

Two agents were trained in every world, from curriculum level 02 to level 06: an ordinary agent, and a neuromodulated one — a small side network reads every input the agent receives and rescales the signals at four places in its brain (the two sensory encoders, the memory and the decision heads). Levels 05 and 06 add body temperature and, at level 06, a sense of smell and sight that grows noisy as the agent is injured; they were built to make the body's state matter more.

An earlier version of this page surveyed many measures and found one lead worth following: in small controlled scenes, starting injured changed the modulated agent's hiding more than the ordinary agent's at levels 04 and 05, and less at level 06. This page follows that lead with four kinds of evidence, and keeps the earlier survey, unchanged, as an appendix.

How to read every figure

Blue is the ordinary agent, orange the neuromodulated one. A badge above each caption says what kind of evidence it is: a controlled test scene; an input manipulation (only the injury the agent feels was changed, the world was not); causal (the agent's starting injury was assigned at random); or observational (what goes together in the agent's own history). Spreads are how much ONE training run moves between its saved checkpoints — never how much two runs would differ, which nothing here can measure.

02 Four ways of looking

  1. Controlled scenes at ten starting injuries. A small fixed world — one bush, and either nothing, a predator or a wandering rabbit — started at injury 0, 10, … 90, with every saved checkpoint of each agent replayed there (sections 03–05).
  2. Step by step inside those scenes. How long injured hiding lasts, and how injured the agent still feels when it leaves cover (sections 06–07).
  3. Observation manipulation. The agent's only injury input — a single number we call its felt injury — is replaced before the network reads it, while the world keeps the true injury. An unhurt agent can be made to feel injured, and an injured one to feel nothing (sections 08–10, and section 13).
  4. The training world. A million recorded episodes per agent from the world it was trained in, read by a randomly assigned starting injury and by the injury felt at each decision (sections 11–13).
Felt injury
The number the agent receives about its injury, from 0 (none) to 1 (drawn on the injury scale, 0–100). It follows the true injury with a short delay: it starts at zero, builds over the first few steps of an injured start, and falls as the agent heals. The agent never sees its injury directly.
Healing
Fast while resting in the bush — about five points a step — so an agent that starts at 90 and rests in cover is close to healed within about twenty steps.
Memory
Both agents carry a recurrent memory from step to step; the modulator has a small memory of its own. A changed input also changes what is remembered, which is why section 09 exists.
Checkpoint spread
Training saved each agent 50 times. Values here are usually the mean over the newest 20, and the spread is how much they move between those 20.

04 Level by level, against a rule fixed in advance

Before looking at these data, the analysis plan fixed when this page may say that one agent's behaviour depends more on injury than the other's: the two agents' averages must differ by more than either agent's own checkpoint-to-checkpoint spread. Anything weaker is reported as a lean, with its count. Over the first 25 steps the rule holds in 12 of the 45 scene-version rows. At level 05 the neuromodulated agent's dependence is the larger in 11 of 12 rows and clears the rule in 4 — all in its favour; at level 02 it clears the rule in all 3 scenes; at level 03 the direction is mixed and one row clears the rule in the ordinary agent's favour; at level 04 the neuromodulated agent's is the larger in all 3 rows without clearing it; at level 06 it is the larger in only 11 of 24 rows and the 4 rows that clear the rule split two and two. The whole-episode measure the sweeps record gives the same picture at level 05 (larger in 12 of 12, 5 clear the rule, all in its favour), reverses it at level 02 (the ordinary agent's is the larger in 2 of 3 and clears the rule in both, because it keeps flickering in and out of cover all episode; Figure 4), finds no passing row at levels 03–04, and strengthens the level-06 reversal (the ordinary agent's is the larger in 19 of 24 rows, and 4 of the 5 passing rows favour it).

05 Over the course of training

The steadiness the earlier page first noticed is the clearest difference between the two agents on this page. At levels 04 and 05, once training is under way, the neuromodulated agent's injury dependence moves much less from checkpoint to checkpoint than the ordinary agent's. At level 04 the ordinary agent's whole-episode slope even drops below zero in about a third of its checkpoints — but over the first 25 steps, where the injury is felt, it is negative in only 5–10 % of the newest 20: the reversals come from hiding later in the episode that does not depend on injury (Figure 4).

Inside an episode

06 How long injured hiding lasts

Healing sets the time scale. Started at injury 90 in the empty scene, both agents at levels 03–05 go straight into cover, stay until the felt injury has fallen, and leave at step 22–24; by step 30 their felt injury is down to 1–11. Two exceptions carry the clues.

07 When the agent leaves cover

If staying in cover is set by injury, the chance of leaving should be low while the agent feels injured and high once it has healed. At levels 03–05 that is what both agents do.

Is it the feeling? Observation manipulation

08 Making an unhurt agent feel injured

Everything above changes the true injury, and with it everything that goes with being hurt. Here the agent starts unhurt and only the felt-injury number it receives is changed — held at 0, 0.1, … 0.9 from the first step — while the world, the true injury and every other input stay as they are. The tool that does this reproduces the scene replays exactly when nothing is changed: step for step, for every run in every scene version checked — 600 checkpoints, 30 episodes each. (One checkpoint first failed on a different make of processor: at one step the agent rated two moves exactly equal, and that processor broke the tie the other way; on the sweep's own kind of processor it matches.)

Because both agents carry a memory, the change is applied two ways. Sustained: the changed input flows into the memory, as a real feeling would. One step: at every step the agent acts from the memory it would have had without the change, and only that step's input is altered — the immediate effect of the feeling, with its effect through memory removed.

09 What the manipulation does to memory

A changed input also changes what the agent carries forward. This figure measures that, so the effects above can be read with it in view rather than hidden in them.

10 What the modulator does with the feeling

The modulator reads every input, so changing the felt injury changes what it reads, and some response is expected. The clue is where in the network it responds, and how that shifts from level to level.

The training world

11 A randomly assigned starting injury

From level 03 on, every training episode starts at a random injury, so a million recorded episodes per agent give a causal dose-response in the world the agent actually learned in.

12 The injury the agent feels

13 Replaying the agent's own history

The observation manipulation can also be applied to recorded training episodes. The agent does not act: it is fed exactly the inputs it received, once as recorded and once with only the felt injury changed, so the whole remembered history is identical and the difference in what it would choose is caused by the feeling. The sustained version is the natural one; the one-step version (a jump of 0.3 in a single step) never happens in life, because the felt injury changes smoothly. Fed unchanged, both agents reproduce every recorded decision (the neuromodulated one differs on 6–17 of 435,000–771,000 decisions per level, each a near-exact tie between two moves).

14 Leads and directions

  1. Both agents act on the feeling itself. An unhurt agent made to feel injured hides like an injured one (Figure 6), and erasing the feeling removes most of the pull to rest (Figure 11). Injury dependence is present without the modulator, so the modulator's contribution has to be read in its shape, not its presence.
  2. At levels 04 and 05, the neuromodulated agent's injury dependence is steadier in this run pair. Over the whole episode it moves a quarter to a half as much from checkpoint to checkpoint (Figure 3); over the first 25 steps about half as much at level 04 and in most level-05 scene versions, not all; at levels 02, 03 and 06 the two agents are not clearly different. If this repeats in a second seed, it is the modulator's most reliable effect so far — worth measuring directly as a training-stability outcome.
  3. The neuromodulated agent's response is consistent with the feeling being read against its memory. Given a felt injury its memory has not caught up with, it overreacts (Figure 11, within the natural range) or, at large values, stops hiding (Figure 6); the ordinary agent does neither. Its modulator's own memory is the part most moved by the feeling (Figure 7). Whether this is integration or fragility is untested — a modulator without memory, or one whose memory is reset, would separate them.
  4. Level 05 is the modulator's best level; level 06 its worst — in the controlled scenes. Only at level 05 does its stronger injury dependence clear the pre-stated rule in both windows (Figure 2). At level 06 it barely responds to the feeling in scenes without a fire (Figure 6), neither agent stays in cover to heal (Figure 4), and the modulator responds in the decision heads rather than the encoders (Figure 8). This happens with the injury-driven noise switched off too, so the noise is not the first suspect; and in level 06's own training world both agents still hide with felt injury (Figure 10). Whether the scenes — which sit outside level 06's training world — or the training world is the fairer read is open. Manipulating body temperature with the same tool is a direct next test.
  5. The training world hides most of this. In the million-episode recordings the two agents' injury curves nearly coincide (Figures 9–10); the one apparent causal difference, a steeper Rest curve for the neuromodulated agent at level 04, has a late-checkpoint band that overlaps the ordinary agent's. The scenes and the manipulation were needed to see the differences at all.
  6. What would turn leads into findings: two or three more seeds per agent at levels 04–06; the same analyses at a matched-capacity ordinary agent, to separate the modulator from the extra parameters it brings; and a manipulation that holds the modulator's output fixed while the feeling changes, to test whether its gain changes cause the behaviour.

15 What this page cannot say

The binding limit

One training run per agent per world. Every comparison here is one run against one run. Spreads are how much a single run moves between its checkpoints, not how far two runs of the same configuration would land apart. That is why the page gives leads, not effects.

  • Levels 05 and 06 are measured in scenes the agents never trained in. A survivable scene needs a body temperature the training world did not produce, so all four temperature versions are shown and read together.
  • Level 02's agents never started injured in training, so an injured start is unfamiliar to them, and its neuromodulated agent was still training (44 of 50 checkpoints): its scene values use the newest 20 it had, and it is missing from the training-world figures.
  • A felt injury held constant is not natural, and larger values move the memory steadily further from where it sits in unmanipulated episodes (Figure 7). The sustained replay manipulation in section 13 is the natural counterpart.
  • The no-animal scene has no randomness, so its 30 episodes per checkpoint are identical; its only variation is between checkpoints.
