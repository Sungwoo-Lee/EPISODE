Continual Worlds

A plan, now updated with its pilot results: keep the agent's body rules exactly the same while the outside world changes from stage to stage, and test whether the neuromodulated agent copes with those changes better than the ordinary agent.

01 What this experiment asks

The modulator is a small side network that rescales the agent's brain according to its body state — how hungry, hurt and cold it is. So far it has been compared with an ordinary agent one world at a time, and in that setting the two agents behave almost the same. The neuromodulation literature reports its main benefit elsewhere: in continual learning, where the task changes back and forth (A → B → A → B) and a modulated network forgets less and relearns faster. This plan tests that idea with a twist that comes from the user's own view of the problem: the body's rules never change, only the world does. Hunger, injury and temperature keep their own dynamics in every world, so the body state is a context that means the same thing everywhere — exactly the kind of signal a modulator could use to keep its skills when the world around it changes.

Status

Status (28 September, late evening): the pilots are read out; no main run has started. Short trial runs tested each new world with both agents. Two worlds pass — scarce food (Famine) and cold with a single fire (Winter) — and three were dropped after failing twice: crowded with hunters (Danger), foggy (Fog) and cold, hungry and hunted at once (Harsh). One sequence, Winter ↔ Famine, is ready to launch; two replacement sequences, built from worlds found by extra exploratory runs, are drafts that need the user's approval. Before anything launches, the switching trial still has one check to pass (the memory test, section 07) and the user has open decisions (section 09).

02 How we got here

This plan grew out of a long working conversation. The steps that led to it:

  1. New body rules, one world at a time. Four rules linking hunger, injury and temperature were built and switched on in all 16 combinations; each world trained an ordinary and a modulated agent. The rules changed behaviour a lot, but for both agents equally: no combination made the modulator's behaviour depend on body state more (Body Rules and the Modulator, a null result).
  2. What makes a world balanced. A planning simulation found that the agent's time is already split between hiding, eating and warming, that no food setting makes choices depend more on combinations of body states, and that deaths in the simulation are almost all from predators — while real agents also starve (Interactions Between Internal States).
  3. Hidden danger and exploration. Today's agents react to predators they can smell and ignore unsmellable ambushers — but knowing the ambusher count is worth nothing, while knowing the predator count is worth about 5 points of survival. A short smell range hides predators and makes finding food a search; on a 10 × 10 map with a 3-cell smell range the agent still thrived, while very sparse food on big maps left fresh agents unable to learn to eat.
  4. The idea. The user proposed moving to continual learning, where the neuromodulation literature reports its main effect, with one constraint that follows from the project's view of internal states: the body's rules stay fixed and only the world changes. The world concepts, the Nursery (the user asked for one slow predator there, so hiding can be learned), the larger maps with short smell range, the pre-trained warm-up and the A-B-A-B priority order were all settled in that conversation.

03 The body stays, the world changes

Every setting of the environment was sorted into two groups. The body group is fixed in all eight worlds and matches today's level 05 exactly; the world group is what the eight worlds vary. Two settings sit on the boundary — they belong to things in the world but act directly on the body — and are kept fixed so the body's arithmetic never changes: the energy in one bite of food, and how much faster injury heals in a bush.

06 The run plan

Training a new agent to competence takes about 10 million episodes, and until that point the ordinary and modulated agents behave almost the same. So the warm-up is pre-training: every sequence starts from the two agents already trained for 10 million episodes in Home (one ordinary, one modulated, from the 16-world study). Each continues with a single Forage stage — learning to search on a big map — and then alternates between two worlds, A → B → A → B, so each world is visited twice. Forage is trained once per agent, and every sequence branches from the same Forage checkpoint at 11 million episodes (one million episodes after the pre-trained counter), so all sequences start from identical weights.

How long each visit lasts was set from the pilots by a rule written down before they ran: 1.5 times the time the slower agent needed to level off in that world, at least 1 million and at most 3 million episodes. That gives Winter 3 million episodes per visit (the agents levelled off late there, so it hits the cap) and 1 million for Forage, Famine, Danger-A and Fog-B (all levelled off early, so they get the floor). The three sequences are now:

  • P3 Winter ↔ Famine — two needs competing for the agent's time; exactly as planned, and ready. 8 million episodes after the branch.
  • P1 replacement: Danger-A ↔ Famine — hide vs. search, with a Danger world whose hunters give up as quickly as Home's. Draft, pending the user's approval.
  • P2 replacement: Fog-B ↔ Danger-A — Fog without sensor noise against the same softer Danger. Draft, pending the user's approval.

The original P1 (Danger ↔ Famine) and P2 (Fog ↔ Danger) lost their worlds in the pilots and will not be run. The short switching trial (Pilot 2) did run them, one million episodes per stage, purely as a check that the machinery works; it is drawn at the top of Figure 4 because that is what ran.

07 What the pilots showed

Before the long runs, each new world got a trial: both pre-trained agents were moved from Home into that world alone and trained for up to 3 million episodes (Forage 4 million). A world counts as survivable if, over the last 200,000 episodes, each agent stays alive at least half as long as it did in Home — 124.9 steps for the ordinary agent, 126.7 for the modulated one — and eats at least one bite per episode.

  • Famine and Winter pass for both agents, so the sequence Winter ↔ Famine survives intact. Forage passes easily — both agents reach almost the 500-step episode limit — and carries the planned "too easy to be a distinct world" flag; that is expected for the safe first stage.
  • Danger, Fog and Harsh fail for both agents, twice. Each was softened once by the step named in advance (fewer hunters, half the sensor noise, more food) and failed again, so by the rule they are dropped. Deaths there came mostly from injury and starvation, in similar shares: the agents could neither avoid the danger nor find enough food.
  • Scouting runs — extra, not planned in advance — point at the cause. In Danger it is how persistent the hunters are, not how many: hunters that give up as quickly as Home's (Danger-A) bring the agents close to the line, while fewer but persistent hunters (Danger-B) or a longer smell range (Danger-C) do not. In Fog it is the sensor noise, not the short smell range: without noise (Fog-B) both agents pass; a longer smell range with half the noise (Fog-A) does not. Danger-A passes for the modulated agent only (131.0 steps); the ordinary agent falls just short (122.6). These are single runs, so they are options for the user, not results.
  • The Nursery warm-up works. Fresh agents trained from scratch in Nursery pass every skill check — surviving, eating, hiding when hurt, warming — and then reach Home's competence level within about a million Home episodes (those Home runs are still training).
  • The switching machinery works, with one check left. The short A-B-A-B trial passed 3 of its 4 checks: every world switch happened on time, no run crashed, and every stage saved its 10 checkpoints. The fourth — the memory test, which replays each stage's final agent in the other worlds without training — has not yet run on a finished trial, and must pass before the sequences start.
  • Winter: the modulated agent is weaker there. It settles at 160.8 steps against the ordinary agent's 198.0 and dies of cold or heat more often. It also peaked at 175.7 and then slid about 8.5 %, while the ordinary agent stayed near its best. This is one run of one pair of agents, so it is a finding to look at, not evidence about the modulator — but it matters, because P3 spends 6 of its 8 million episodes in Winter.

08 What will be measured

Survival — steps alive per episode — is the only performance measure; reward is never used. For every switch into a world, four things are compared between the modulated and the ordinary agent, all written down before any training:

  • Dip: how far survival falls in the first 20,000 episodes after the switch, relative to that world's level for the same agent (its last 200,000 episodes on the first visit).
  • Recovery: how much training it takes to climb back to 90 % of that level — counted in episodes and in environment steps, and only counted if both agree.
  • Return: on the second visit, how much better the agent does than on the first. Better on the return means something was kept, not relearned.
  • Forgetting: at the end of each stage the agent is tested, without training, in every other world of its sequence (2,000 episodes each), giving a "what does it still know" table.

A shared yardstick for dip and recovery (added 28 September, before any main run). Dip and recovery as defined above are measured against each agent's own settled level, and the Winter pilot showed why that alone is unfair. Right after the switch the two agents were almost level (129 and 138 steps), but the ordinary agent later settled much higher (198 against 161). Measured against its own higher target, the ordinary agent took 132,000 episodes to climb back to 90 % of it and the modulated agent 40,000 — a threefold "win" for the modulator that only reflects its lower target. Against one shared target, 90 % of the lower of the two settled levels (145 steps), the order reverses: 28,000 against 40,000 episodes. So dip and recovery are now read both ways, and a switch counts for the modulator only when both readings agree (for recovery, in episodes and in environment steps as well); a switch whose readings disagree is recorded as "not counted". Return and forgetting are already plain differences in survival steps and are unchanged.

The modulator counts as helping only if, in at least two of the three sequences, at least three of the four measures favour it and at least one clearly exceeds noise. With only one sequence ready today, whether that rule can be met depends on the user's decisions D4 and D5 below. A small earlier probe (May) found exactly this pattern — the modulated agent recovered faster on returns and forgot less — so this is a replication at a much larger scale. The body-balance numbers and how random the agent's choices are (a sign of how easily it can still learn) are logged at every stage.

09 Open decisions

Decided (user, 2026-09-28, "follow your recommendations"):

  • Body rules fixed in every stage; energy per bite and bush healing fixed too.
  • Warm-up = the existing pre-trained Home agents; Nursery with one slow, weak predator for fresh seeds.
  • Forage run once per agent and every sequence branched from it.
  • Fog uses heavier blur and sensor noise instead of a narrower view (a narrower view would change the agent's number of inputs, which the trainer refuses); Winter's fire slightly cooler so the first step onto it is survivable; Famine uses one or two food items that take 30 steps to reappear.
  • A known trainer bug with predator counts between stages is avoided by starting every sequence with Forage (no hunters); the cost is that per-predator distance curves are not logged.

Open — for the user (each with the design's recommendation):

  • D1 · How long is a visit? Per world, as written in advance (Winter 3 million, Famine 1 million: P3 runs 8 million episodes after the branch), or per pair (Famine visits also 3 million: 12 million, about 8–10 hours more per run). Recommended: per world.
  • D2 · Where do the sequences branch? At 11 million episodes, the rule's value, or at 14 million, where the Forage trial ends. Recommended: 11 million — the checkpoints exist, and it avoids 3 million extra episodes in a world flagged too easy.
  • D3 · Launch P3 now or wait? Launch it once the pre-launch checks pass, or wait for the P1 and P2 decisions. Recommended: now — P3 is exactly as planned and independent of the others.
  • D4 · What replaces P1? Danger-A ↔ Famine (the draft), Fog-B ↔ Famine, a new Danger variant one step easier than Danger-A with its own trial, or nothing. The user's call; Fog-B ↔ Famine is the only option where both worlds pass for both agents.
  • D5 · What replaces P2? Fog-B ↔ Danger-A (the draft), a new Danger variant with its own trial, or drop P2. The user's call. If Danger-A is used, the verdict is computed with and without every sequence that contains it, because only one agent passed there.
  • D6 · The Winter finding. Proceed, or look first — for example, read both agents' Winter episodes step by step. Recommended: proceed, since both pass; the modulated agent's peak-then-slide is an argument for looking first.
  • D7 · The later Harsh ↔ Forage sequence. Harsh was dropped, so it cannot run as designed: drop it or replace Harsh. Recommended: decide later.
  • D8 · Must the sequences wait for the Forage trials to finish? The written rule asks for the full Forage curve, but the Forage visit length cannot leave its 1-million floor and the 11-million checkpoints already exist; waiting costs about 1.5–2 hours. Waiving is defensible; the user's call, and a waiver would be recorded as a departure from the written rule.

Still true: every run of an agent starts from the same pre-trained weights, so the sequences are "one starting pair, several world pairs", not independent replicates. The fresh Nursery → Home agents are the start of the fix.

10 Corrections to the first version of this page

  • The main sequences' stages were drawn at a placeholder 2 million episodes. The pilots set the real lengths: 3 million per Winter visit, 1 million per visit to Forage, Famine, Danger-A and Fog-B.
  • The sequences were drawn branching after a 2-million-episode Forage stage; they branch at 11 million, one million episodes into Forage.
  • P1 (Danger ↔ Famine) and P2 (Fog ↔ Danger) were planned as the first two sequences. Danger and Fog were dropped, so both are replaced by drafts waiting for approval, and P3 is now the only ready sequence.
  • The page said Harsh was close to the edge and should be revisited before it entered a sequence. Harsh failed twice and was dropped.
  • Dip and recovery were defined only against each agent's own level; they are now also read against a shared level (section 08).
  • The pilots were described as three sets, ready to launch. They ran, and grew: six softened re-pilots and seven exploratory scout runs were added after three worlds failed.
