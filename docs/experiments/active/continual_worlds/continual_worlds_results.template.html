Continual Worlds: Results

Two studies asked whether an agent with a neuromodulator copes better than an ordinary agent when the world keeps changing. Neither gives a clear yes. This page shows what was measured, what it can and cannot say, and what to test next.

PURPOSE

The project compares two reinforcement-learning agents that are identical except for one part. The modulated agent has a small side network (the modulator) that reads the agent's body state — hunger, injury, temperature — and rescales the main network's layers. The ordinary agent has no modulator. Earlier comparisons, one world at a time, found the two agents behave almost the same. The literature on neuromodulation reports its main benefit somewhere else: when the task keeps changing, a modulated network is said to forget less and relearn faster. A small probe in May 2026 seemed to show exactly that.

So two studies were run. The main study kept the body's rules fixed and switched the world back and forth — A, B, A, B — in three pairs of worlds. The May replication re-ran the May probe's own schedule (a hunting predator, then a harmless world, then the hunter again, twice) with three independent seeds per agent. Performance is measured only in survival steps: how many steps the agent stays alive per episode, at most 500. Reward is never used.

The plan behind the main study, with its worlds and pilot runs, is on the planning page Continual Worlds. A parallel study of what the two agents compute internally, which reads the same May-replication runs, is What Both Agents Compute. The full written analyses are the design documents named in section 06.

01 Take-home, in plain words

The short answer

Neither study shows the modulated agent coping better with a changing world. The main study's rule, written before the data, does not decide either way. The May replication did not reproduce May's large advantage — but its world was too easy to show one. Two small differences are worth a proper test.

  • Main study: the pre-set rule is not decisive. Whether the modulator "wins" depends on one question the design answered both ways: does the foraging world, learned before the back-and-forth began, count when scoring what the agent forgot? Count it and the rule says "supported"; leave it out and the rule says "refuted" (section 02, table after Figure 4).
  • The modulated checkpoint starts ahead in new worlds. Before any training in an unseen world, it survives __N:r04_head_start.zs_min__ to __N:r04_head_start.zs_max__ steps longer (in the cold world Winter, __N:r04_head_start.zs_winter_mod__ against __N:r04_head_start.zs_winter_ord__). Most of what first looked like "coping better with the switch" is this head start; measured from its own starting level it does not improve faster (Figure 4).
  • It kept more of its foraging skill after the cold world. After 3 million episodes in Winter, the modulated checkpoint played in the foraging world is ahead by __N:r04_head_start.samp_forage_gap__ steps (95 % interval __N:r04_head_start.samp_forage_lo__ to __N:r04_head_start.samp_forage_hi__, re-checked with sampled actions). This is the main study's clearest difference — but it rests on one starting pair of agents that share their initial weights, it reverses once, and it concerns the world learned before the alternation (Figures 3–4).
  • May replication: not replicated, and limited by the step cap. On the returns of the hunter the modulated agent was ahead by __N:r06_mayrep_pairs.d3__ steps, inside the pre-set noise band of ±__N:r06_mayrep_pairs.d_band__; May saw __N:r06_mayrep_pairs.may_d3__ and __N:r06_mayrep_pairs.may_d5__. But __N:r05_mayrep_curves.cap_hunt_min__–__N:r05_mayrep_curves.cap_hunt_max__ % of hunting-world episodes already lasted the full 500 steps, leaving room for at most about __N:r05_mayrep_curves.room__ steps of advantage (Figures 5–6).
  • Two hints, not findings. Chosen after the data were seen, so they need a pre-registered test: in every hunting stage the modulated agent died in __N:r08_learning_speed.dr_min__–__N:r08_learning_speed.dr_max__ percentage points fewer episodes, in all three seed pairs; and it learned faster from scratch — though counted per environment step rather than per episode, that lead is about half as large (__N:r08_learning_speed.s10M_diff__ against __N:r08_learning_speed.a100k_diff__ steps; Figure 8).

The set-up in one paragraph

Both agents live on a grid, find food, avoid or hide from predators, and keep warm; the body's rules are the same in every world. An episode ends when the agent dies of injury, starvation or temperature, or after 500 steps. The agents learn with recurrent PPO, a standard reinforcement-learning method; training is counted in episodes and in environment steps (one action each). In the main study both agents were pre-trained for 10 million episodes, trained 1 million more in the foraging world, and then every sequence branched from that one pair of checkpoints. The May replication trained three fresh pairs from scratch.

02 Main study: three sequences of changing worlds

Each sequence visits two worlds twice, A → B → A → B, after the shared foraging stage. Every run is one ordinary and one modulated agent, all six branching from the same pair of checkpoints. Four things were scored, written down before training: how far survival drops right after a switch; how fast it recovers; whether the agent does better on its return to a world; and how much of a world it forgets while training in another.

Why look at the raw curves first. Summary scores hide shape. The curves show whether a switch caused a real drop, whether the two agents' curves differ anywhere, and whether a world's curve is steady enough for one number to describe it.

Why score every switch separately. The design's rule counts votes switch by switch, and a pooled number could hide a switch that goes the other way. Figure 2 puts every switch's reading on one scale, with the noise yardstick each reading had to clear and the vote it produced.

Why test frozen checkpoints. Training curves show only the world being trained on. To see what an agent still knows about the other worlds, each stage-end checkpoint was frozen and played in all seven worlds. This is the memory test the design called the forgetting matrix.

Why separate head start from adaptation. A smaller drop after a switch could mean the agent adapts faster, or simply that it arrived already playing the new world better. The branch-point row of the memory test answers the second question directly, before any training in the new world. A reviewer asked for exactly this check, and for the key cells to be re-run with sampled actions, since the memory test plays greedily.

How the verdict depends on the scoring choices

The pre-set rule asks whether, in at least two of three sequences, at least three of the four measures favour the modulated agent with at least one beyond noise; it also has a refutation clause. It never said how a measure's several per-switch votes combine into one sign, nor whether the foraging world counts in the forgetting vote — both were chosen after the data were seen. 03 May replication: three seeds, May's schedule

In May, one run per agent suggested the modulated agent came back far better when a hunter returned after a harmless phase. The replication trained three independent seeds per agent from scratch on May's five-stage schedule: hunting for 1.5 million episodes, then harmless, hunting, harmless and hunting again, 700,000 episodes each. Its verdict rule was fixed before training: the return advantage or the forgetting difference had to favour the modulated agent in at least two of three seed pairs and exceed twice its standard error.

Why look at the ceiling first. If most episodes already last the full 500 steps, a better agent has nowhere to show it. Figure 5 shows how close to the cap every stage ran.

Why show each seed pair. The rule is a three-seed rule; the pairs show whether a mean hides disagreement, and putting May's number on the same axis shows the scale of what was being tested.

Why a frozen-agent test. The training curve relearns so fast that it hides forgetting. Freezing each stage-end checkpoint and playing it in the hunting world shows what a harmless stage actually erased — and, unlike the training curve, it is not pinned at the cap.

Why look at learning speed. The parallel study "What Both Agents Compute" listed learning speed as never measured with seeds. Stage 1 is the one place in the replication where agents learn from scratch, far below the cap. A reviewer pointed out that counting training in episodes favours the agent that survives longer, since its episodes contain more steps and so more training; Figure 8 shows both counts.

04 Caveats that travel with every sentence above

  • One shared start. A same-seed ordinary and modulated agent begin from bitwise-identical main-network weights. For the main study's pre-trained pair this was checked directly (__N:r01_training_curves.shared_same__ of __N:r01_training_curves.shared_of__ weight arrays identical); for the May replication it was checked on the seed-42 pair, and for seeds 43 and 44 it follows from the same code but has not been checked. In the main study all six runs branch from one such pair, so every comparison is "this one start, perturbed by the modulator". There is no yardstick yet for how far two different starts drift apart in these worlds.
  • One seed in the main study. One run per agent per sequence; the three sequences share their starting agents, so they are not three independent votes.
  • Scouted worlds. Danger-A and Fog-B were found by exploratory runs after the planned hunter and fog worlds proved unsurvivable, and Danger-A passed its survival check for the modulated agent only.
  • The ceiling. The May replication's training curves are all near the 500-step cap; its survival readings could not have shown an effect of May's size. The foraging world, too, starts at the cap in the main study.
  • Most-likely vs sampled actions. The memory tests play the most likely action, training samples. Only the four key cells of Figure 4 were re-checked with sampled actions; the other cells are greedy only.
  • Choices made after seeing the data, each flagged where it appears: how per-switch votes combine and which worlds count in the forgetting vote (the table in section 02); the head-start and gain measures (Figure 4); the 80 % ceiling label (Figure 5); the three-seed interval and May-sized line (Figure 7); the death-rate and learning-speed hints (Figure 8).

What changed during the analysis

  • The main study was first written up as "fragile support" for the modulator. An independent review showed the pre-set rule also reaches its refutation clause, depending on the forgetting entry set; the label is now "not decided".
  • The first write-up credited the modulated agent with "coping better with the first switch". Most of that is the zero-shot head start of Figure 4; the claim now reads "its checkpoint generalises better to unseen worlds, for this one pair".
  • The read-out script scored recovery ties as exact equality of episode counts; the rule said "the same logged row". The script was fixed, and Figure 2's votes are checked against both sources.
  • The May replication's learning-speed hint was first reported on the episode axis only; on the environment-step axis it is about half as large.

05 What to test next

  1. A world with room above the cap. Re-run the double return where the hunting stage leaves the ordinary agent well below 500 steps — for example with bushes the predator can enter, slower healing in cover, or a pouncing predator — screening candidate worlds with the ordinary agent first.
  2. Learning speed as a pre-registered outcome, on the environment-step axis. Register the early-window mean and the steps needed to reach a threshold, with a noise rule fixed in advance and several seeds.
  3. Pairs that do not share a start. Compare ordinary agents from different seeds with each other (ordinary seed 42 against seeds 43 and 44) to measure how far two starts drift with no modulator at all, and pair each modulated agent with an ordinary agent from a different seed.
  4. Zero-shot generalisation as its own question. Play several seeds' pre-trained checkpoints, both agents, in unseen worlds with sampled and most-likely actions. Only if the head start is stable across seeds is a design about adaptation speed worth running.
  5. Settle the scoring choices before any fresh runs: how votes combine, which worlds count in forgetting, most-likely or sampled test play, and the ceiling-free outcomes above.
