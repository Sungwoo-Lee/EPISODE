Level 04, Measured Again

On 22 September we reported that the ordinary agent sits in a bush out of habit while the neuromodulated agent hides only when threatened. Measured properly, both agents answer a threat the same way — and the difference that remains belongs to the test scene, not the agent.

01 What this page is about

Level 04 of the training curriculum is a small grid world with food, bushes to hide in, and predators that stalk the agent and pounce from two or three squares away. Two agents were trained there: an ordinary one, and one carrying a neuromodulator — a small extra network that reads the agent's internal state and rescales the signals flowing through the rest of its brain. The question was whether the two hide differently.

The first answer, published on 22 September, was striking: the ordinary agent sat on the bush 86% of the time in a scene with nothing in it, as if parked, while the neuromodulated agent went from 6% with nothing around to 58% with a predator present — hiding as a response. That answer rested on two measurement defects, both now fixed:

  1. It read every number off the last saved checkpoint. Training saves the agent 50 times, and late in training its behaviour still swings widely from one save to the next — far more than the error in any single reading. Every number here is the average of the last 20.
  2. Its test bush let predators walk in. In training the bush blocks animals; in the test scenes it did not, so a hidden agent could be bitten. The test scenes now match training.

What the corrected measurement shows, in three sentences. Both agents hide more as the threat grows, and the extra hiding a predator causes is the same for both — about 41 points. The ordinary agent hides more overall in the small test scenes, but in its own training world the two agents hide the same amount at every injury level. And the second version of the world roughly triples calm hiding for both agents alike.

04 The extra hiding belongs to the test scene

The ordinary agent's higher floor in Figure 2 invites the old reading back in a milder form: a more bush-bound agent. The agents' own training world says otherwise. There, replaying a million episodes each, the two agents spend the same share of time on a bush at every level of injury, threat or no threat.

The clearest way to see the disagreement is at one and the same checkpoint — the last one, the only one the training-world replays exist for. In the empty test scene the ordinary agent hides 86% of the time and the neuromodulated agent 6%. In the training world, with no predator nearby, they hide 52% and 51%. Same two agents, same saved brains, and an 80-point gap in one world is no gap in the other. The difference is something the test scene elicits, not a standing trait.

07 What we would still not claim

The binding limit

One training seed per agent. Every interval on this page describes how much one run moves between its own checkpoints, not how much two runs of the same agent would differ. Even the one clear difference — the ordinary agent's idle hiding in the empty scene — could belong to this particular run rather than to agents without a neuromodulator.

  • The training-world replays exist for the final checkpoint only, the very reading this page distrusts. The argument in section 04 uses them at a matched checkpoint, where the comparison is fair, but their values at other checkpoints are unknown.
  • The two worlds differ in three ways at once, so the tripling of calm hiding cannot be attributed to faster healing in cover alone.
  • The explanation for the scene effect is a hypothesis. That an agent with nothing to do falls back on its most practised behaviour fits the data but has not been tested.
