Making rest in the open not worth taking

Which healing settings leave an injured agent unable to recover anywhere except inside a bush — and why the two settings that ship today do the opposite.

PURPOSE

In this simulation an animal walks a grid, eats, gets hurt, and heals only by choosing the Rest action. Injury is measured on a scale from 0 to {{VAL:max_injury}}; one point on that scale is an injury point, and at {{VAL:max_injury}} the animal dies. Some grid squares hold a bush, which hides the animal from a hunting predator and which predators cannot walk into. A setting called body.recovery_in_bush_multiplier can make resting inside a bush heal faster than resting on open ground. It ships at {{VAL:shipped_mult}}, meaning “no difference at all”.

This page asks a tuning question: what values of the healing settings would make resting out in the open not worth doing at all, so that an injured animal has to travel to cover to get better? That is a design goal, not a fact about the world — the point of making cover the only place to heal is to see whether an animal that is hurt starts using cover, which is a behaviour this project wants to be able to study.

The answer. Set the open-ground healing rate to recovery_base_rate: {{VAL:rec_base}}, switch the compounding off with recovery_accel_rate: {{VAL:rec_accel}}, and set recovery_in_bush_multiplier: {{VAL:rec_mult}}. Under those values, undoing a bad {{VAL:wound}}-point wound takes {{VAL:rec_cover_steps}} rest steps on a bush and {{VAL:rec_open_steps_wound}} out in the open. And an animal can afford only about {{VAL:budget}} consecutive rest steps before it starves — resting earns no food while hunger keeps draining. So out in the open, recovery is not slow. It is out of reach.

Both of the settings that ship today close that same wound in under twenty rest steps out in the open — {{VAL:shipped_steps_wound}} for the default, {{VAL:a01_steps_wound}} for a01 — comfortably inside what the animal can pay for. Under either of them a bush is worth nothing to an injured animal, which is the problem this page exists to fix.

01 What a reader needs before the figures

Everything below refers to one simulation. Two short lists, because none of it is guessable from the figures: first the ordinary words this page leans on, then the settings it tunes.

Words this page keeps using

injury point
One percent of the way from unhurt to dead. The scale runs 0 = unhurt to {{VAL:max_injury}} = dead, and it is a single number the simulation carries for the body — not tissue damage or blood loss. Only two things move it. It goes up when a predator lands a hit, by an amount drawn between 15 and 120 points, so one hit is anywhere from a seventh of the way to dead to instantly fatal. It goes down only when the animal rests. And it does only two things in return: at {{VAL:max_injury}} the animal dies, and it is the number the animal's nociception sense reports. It does not slow the animal down or change what it can see.
cover, and the open
Cover means a square with a bush on it. The open means every other square. Whether healing should differ between the two is the entire question on this page.
rest budget
How many steps in a row an animal can afford to spend resting. A resting animal eats nothing while its hunger keeps draining, so this is set by food, not by injury: about {{VAL:budget}} steps from a typical start, {{VAL:budget_fixed}} from a full stomach. It is an upper bound, and a fatal one — an animal that really spends all of it resting starves at the end.
heal away
To reduce injury by resting. “Heals away 10 points” means the injury number goes down by 10.
streak
A run of rest steps with nothing in between. Any step on which the animal does something other than Rest sets the count back to zero, which matters because one of the settings heals faster the longer the streak gets.
the formula (a “closed form”)
A single expression that gives the total healing after any number of rest steps directly, instead of adding the steps up one at a time. Five of the six figures are drawn from it; Figure 6 exists to check it against the simulation itself.
compounding
Healing that speeds up the longer the animal rests without interruption, rather than staying at a flat rate. One of the two settings that ship today compounds; the other does not.
a01
A nickname for one particular set of values from an earlier round of training runs, which raised the healing rate and switched compounding off. This page keeps it on the charts as a comparison, because it is the only setting already in the repository that does not compound.

The settings this page tunes, and the machinery around them

step
One environment timestep. An episode runs at most {{VAL:max_steps}} steps. In every figure the t = 0 row is the initial state, not a step.
injury_level
The body variable this study is about, on a 0–{{VAL:max_injury}} scale. Reaching {{VAL:max_injury}} ends the episode.
Rest
One of the agent's actions. It is the only way injury ever goes down. A resting agent does not move and does not eat.
nutrition
The hunger variable, also 0–{{VAL:max_nutrition}}. It falls by metabolic_cost = {{VAL:metabolic_cost}} every single step; reaching 0 ends the episode by starvation.
bush
An obstacle carrying hides_agent: true. It conceals the agent from a hunting predator, and since 2026-09-14 also blocks animals from entering the cell. It is the “cover” this page keeps referring to.
recovery_base_rate
Injury points healed on the first rest step of an uninterrupted run of them. The prose below calls it the base rate.
recovery_accel_rate
How much more each consecutive rest step heals than the one before it, as a fraction. At 0 the healing rate is flat; at {{VAL:shipped_accel}} each step heals 1.5× the last.
recovery_in_bush_multiplier
What the healing is multiplied by on a step the agent rests while standing on a bush — the multiplier below. Must be greater than 0. At exactly 1.0 the whole mechanism is not even compiled into the simulation.
nociception
The signal the agent receives about its injury. This page does not use it — the study is about the body variable, not about what the agent can feel.

02 How long does recovery take?

Why this question first

Recovery has one plain property that owes nothing to food, to thresholds, or to what the animal is rewarded for: given the healing settings, how many rest steps does undoing a wound actually take? That is a fact about the recovery parameters and nothing else, and it is the only thing this section asks. Everything the page goes on to argue is built on top of it, so it is worth having on its own before anything is layered over it.

A worked case first. Take an animal that is badly hurt — injury {{VAL:ex_injury}} of {{VAL:max_injury}} — and ask only how long it would have to sit still to undo that, under each of the settings this page compares.

That gap is the whole design, and it is stated in nothing but steps — no threshold, no food, no reward. 03 Can the animal afford that?

Why this analysis

Figure 1 says how long recovery takes. It says nothing about whether the animal has that long, and the answer is that it very often does not. Resting earns no food while hunger keeps draining at a fixed rate every step, so there is a hard ceiling on how many steps in a row an animal can spend resting before it starves: {{VAL:budget}} steps from the typical half-full start, {{VAL:budget_fixed}} from a full stomach. That ceiling is the rest budget, and it is set by food alone — it is the same number whether the animal is barely scratched or nearly dead.

So hunger decides how long, and the recovery settings decide how much. They meet in exactly one place, which is the comparison this figure draws. Take the worked animal again: under the recommendation it needs {{VAL:ex_rec_full_steps}} rest steps to heal in the open, and it can afford {{VAL:ex_afford}}. Needing {{VAL:ex_rec_vs_budget}} times the food you have is not slow recovery. It is no recovery.

Why this page stops at hunger

Hunger is the only competing demand measured in the same currency — steps — that this page has numbers for. Body temperature is a second one, and it is being tuned in a separate piece of work; when that lands it adds another line to this figure and changes nothing in Figure 1, because Figure 1 is a fact about recovery alone. That is the reason for building the page in this order rather than defining everything at once.

04 The same comparison, stated formally

Sections 2 and 3 answered the question in steps, for one wound at a time. To check it over every setting rather than the four on those charts, the comparison has to be written as arithmetic. This section does that, and introduces the two pieces of shorthand the rest of the page uses.

The arithmetic, as the simulation actually computes it

Five lines, transcribed from {{VAL:source_file}} lines {{VAL:source_lines}}. Everything on this page is derived from them in code; no cumulative total anywhere is typed by hand.

← the code is wider than the box — scroll it sideways

# streak counts CONSECUTIVE rest steps; any non-rest step resets it to 0
streak(t)       = rested ? streak(t-1) + 1 : 0
recovery_amount = recovery_base_rate * (1 + recovery_accel_rate) ** (max(streak,1) - 1)
# the in-bush multiplier multiplies the AMOUNT, so it inherits the two conditions below
if recovery_in_bush_multiplier != 1.0 and in_bush:  recovery_amount *= multiplier
can_recover     = rested AND applied_inc <= 0
new_injury      = clip(injury + applied_inc - recovery_amount * can_recover, 0, max_injury)

Because the streak is 1 on the first rest step and the exponent is streak - 1, the heal on the n-th consecutive rest step is base × (1 + accel)^(n-1), and the total over n steps is a geometric sum:

cumulative(n) = base × n
cumulative(n) = base × ((1 + accel)n − 1) / accel

The first line is the case with compounding switched off, the second the case with it on; in cover, both are multiplied by the in-bush multiplier. Figures 1 and 2 are this same expression solved the other way round, for n — which is why they can be drawn in steps.

The two requirements, and their short names

The page needs to refer to two things constantly, so they get names. Condition A is the one Figures 1 and 2 have been testing: the open must not heal. Condition B is its necessary partner: cover must heal — a world in which nobody can recover anywhere is not the goal. An animal resting on a bush should get a bad {{VAL:wound}}-point wound closed inside roughly {{VAL:cover_steps}} rest steps. Pushing healing down until the open is useless also pushes it down inside the bush, so the two pull against each other, and the tuning problem is the gap between them.

Written out at recovery_accel_rate = 0, both become one line each. Condition B is base × multiplier ≥ wound / cover steps, which is {{VAL:cover_rate_min}} injury points per rest step. Condition A needs one more number before it can be written down, and that number is the only genuinely arbitrary thing on this page.

The one chosen number, and what it is for

“The open must not heal” is not yet testable, because the open always heals a little. A line has to be drawn, and this page draws it at θ = {{VAL:theta}} injury points: resting in the open for the whole rest budget must not undo more than that. Written out, condition A is base ≤ θ / budget — {{VAL:ratio}} injury points per rest step at the primary setting.

θ is a quantity of healing, not an injury level, and it exists only so the later figures have a boundary to draw. In Figures 1 and 2 no such number was needed: comparing steps against steps settles the question on its own. Note too that θ and the budget only ever appear as the ratio between them, so halving θ and doubling the budget are the same move — which is why §10 needs one table rather than a grid.

05 Where the open is already unrecoverable

Why this analysis

Figures 1 and 2 answered condition A for four settings. This figure answers it for every setting in the plane that any shipped configuration occupies. Doing it over a whole plane forces a change of view: a step count needs a wound size to be a step count, and a plane of settings does not have one. So from here on the page uses the formal version from §4 — how much injury the open can clear inside the rest budget, against the θ line — which is the same comparison with the wound divided out of it. An informative answer tells us whether the workable region is a large comfortable area or a sliver — and therefore whether the recommendation is robust or delicately balanced.

06 The answer figure: where both conditions hold

Why this analysis

This is the figure the study exists to produce. Condition A caps the base rate; condition B puts a floor under the base rate times the multiplier. Plotted against each other, the settings that satisfy both form a region, and the tuning question becomes “which point in that region?” rather than “does one exist?”. An informative answer would show a region with room in it — a single feasible point would mean the design was balanced on a knife edge and would break the first time somebody edited a threshold.

07 Does any compounding at all survive?

Why this analysis

Figure 4 fixes recovery_accel_rate at zero, and a fixed parameter is an assumption until something tests it. The worry is specific: compounding is exactly the mechanism that lets a tiny base rate clear a large wound if the agent simply keeps resting, so a small positive acceleration might quietly undo condition A. An informative answer is a number — the largest acceleration each base rate can tolerate — not a yes or a no.

08 The check that the algebra is the simulation

Why this analysis

Figures 1 to 5 are all the same closed form, transcribed by hand from the simulation's source. If that transcription is wrong, five figures are confidently wrong together and nothing else on this page would show it. So this figure drives the real environment with a scripted always-Rest policy, once with the agent standing on a bush and once on bare ground, and plots what the simulation actually did against what the formula predicted. Agreement validates the algebra. Disagreement would mean the algebra is wrong, and the honest response would be to say so rather than adjust the plot.

Validation result

Agreement. Over {{VAL:f06_n}} compared values the largest disagreement between the simulation and the closed form is {{VAL:f06_worst}} injury points, which is single-precision rounding accumulated over {{VAL:f06_steps}} steps — the smallest representable gap near injury {{VAL:f06_start_injury}} is {{VAL:f06_eps}}. After {{VAL:f06_steps}} rest steps the open-ground agent's injury measured {{VAL:f06_open_final}} against a predicted {{VAL:f06_open_pred}}; the bush agent reached zero injury on step {{VAL:f06_bush_zero}} and stayed there.

09 The recommendation, and the rule that produced it

The region of Figure 4 where both conditions hold has room in it, so a point inside it has to be chosen rather than found. The rule is stated here because a point picked by eye is not reproducible.

CLAUSE 1
Switch the compounding off
Figure 5 is the evidence. At any base rate large enough to be useful in cover, a positive recovery_accel_rate pushes the open past θ inside the budget.
CLAUSE 2
Anchor the healing rate inside cover to an experiment that already ran
The a01 round of runs was calibrated so that a {{VAL:wound}}-point wound closes in about {{VAL:a01_steps_wound}} rest steps, at a flat {{VAL:a01_base}} points per step. Making the in-cover rate equal to that keeps the injured window comparable with those runs — base × multiplier = {{VAL:rec_cover_rate}}.
CLAUSE 3
Both conditions must hold at both budgets, strictly
Not only the {{VAL:budget}}-step median budget but the {{VAL:budget_fixed}}-step budget of a world that starts at full nutrition — and with room to spare rather than exactly on the line, so the recommendation does not sit on its own edge.
CLAUSE 4
Take the weakest intervention that survives
Among the {{VAL:rec_candidates}} round-number pairs that pass, take the largest base rate, which is the smallest multiplier. A smaller multiplier is a smaller change to the world.

An independent check the design did not ask for

Everything above is about what is possible, not about what an agent would choose. The agent's reward is the reduction in homeostatic drive, the distance of its body state from its setpoints — and resting costs a unit of nutrition, which pushes that distance back up. Taking a typical mid-episode state, injury {{VAL:rw_injury}} and nutrition {{VAL:rw_nutrition}}, one rest step under the recommended setting is worth {{VAL:rw_open}} in the open and +{{VAL:rw_cover}} on a bush. The open-ground rest step is not merely futile, it is punished: the hunger it costs outweighs the injury it clears. That is a separate argument from the definition and it lands in the same place, which is the most reassuring thing on this page.

11 Where the analysis design needed correcting

The study was specified before it was run. Six things in that specification turned out to be wrong, incomplete, or true only under a condition that was not stated. They are recorded here rather than quietly fixed, so a reader who remembers the original brief can see what moved.

1. The {{VAL:budget}}-step budget is not the budget of the shipped default world
The brief derived {{VAL:budget}} steps as the median of a start nutrition drawn uniformly from 0 to {{VAL:max_nutrition}}. That draw is real, but only in worlds that switch randomised starting nutrition on. The shipped base configuration does not: it starts every episode at full nutrition, giving a {{VAL:budget_fixed}}-step budget, which is twice as strict. Every figure and table on this page now carries both.
2. The step-by-step rule quoted in the brief dropped a term
The brief wrote the injury update as clip(injury - recovery, 0, max_injury). The simulation writes clip(injury + applied_inc - recovery, 0, max_injury), where applied_inc is this step's slice of any damage still being spread over {{VAL:smoothing}} steps. (That line is the step-by-step rule the simulation applies once per step, and the single formula this page uses is derived from it.) It makes no difference to the undamaged case this study is about, but it hides a real asymmetry: the rest streak keeps climbing during those {{VAL:smoothing}} steps while healing is blocked, so a compounding setting comes out of a hit faster than the clean curve suggests. The conclusion about acceleration is therefore conservative, and the direction of the error is worth knowing.
3. “A resting agent eats nothing” is true only while eating is an action
It holds on every maintained world, because eating is its own action there and resting is a different one. But the simulation also supports worlds where eating happens automatically on contact with food, and in those the flag is a function of the agent's position alone, not of what it chose to do. An agent resting on a food cell in such a world eats every step, and its rest budget is no longer bounded by nutrition at all. The definition should name that assumption.
4. The budget is a fatal bound, which makes the test conservative rather than merely approximate
An agent that really spends nutrition / metabolic_cost steps resting starves on the last one. So the definition grants the open strictly more rest than any surviving agent could take, which means it overstates what the open can heal. A setting that fails condition A under this definition fails it with room to spare. That is a strength of the definition and it was not stated.
5. The definition is about possibility, not about choice
Nothing in it asks whether an agent would take the rest steps it is granted. Under the homeostatic reward the recommended setting makes an open-ground rest step actively negative at a typical mid-episode state (§9), which is a stronger result than the definition claims — and a different one. The two are kept apart on this page rather than merged.
6. Figure 4 cannot show both shipped settings
The brief asked for the two shipped settings to be drawn throughout. Figure 4 is a plane at recovery_accel_rate = 0, and only one of the two shipped settings lives there. Plotting the other one on it would have placed a point at coordinates the setting does not have. It appears in Figures 1, 2, 3 and 5, and its absence from Figure 4 is stated in that figure's caption.

12 What this page does not show

Limitations, stated rather than implied

No agent was trained. This is a tuning study of the environment's arithmetic. Whether an injured agent actually learns to walk to a bush under these settings is the experiment this page exists to make worth running, and it has not been run.

The validation is narrow. Figure 6 is two runs of a single setting, under one random seed. It confirms the shape of the step-by-step rule and the fact that cover heals faster; a defect that only appears at some other recovery_accel_rate would not be caught.

Adopting the recommendation switches on a piece of the simulation that is currently switched off. At exactly 1.0 the in-bush multiplier is not compiled into the simulation at all, which is what makes today's runs bit-identical to runs that predate the setting. A multiplier of {{VAL:rec_mult}} switches that branch on. That is intended, but it means any world adopting these values is a new experiment rather than a continuation of an old one.

The wound and the cover-step count are borrowed. {{VAL:wound}} points in {{VAL:cover_steps}} rest steps comes from the calibration of an earlier sweep, not from a measurement of what wounds agents actually carry. A measured distribution of injury at the moment agents choose to rest would be a better anchor, and does not exist yet.
